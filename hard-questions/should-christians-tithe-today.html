50 Hard Questions · Question 49 of 50

Should Christians Tithe Today?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Israel’s tithes — several of them, about 23 percent a year — were a requirement of the Mosaic Law. The New Testament calls the church to voluntary, proportional, generous, cheerful giving.

Key verses2 Corinthians 9:7 · 1 Corinthians 16:2 · 2 Corinthians 8:2–3

Tithing — giving a tenth of one’s income — was a specific requirement under the Mosaic Law given to national Israel, tied to the support of the Levitical priesthood and the broader civic and religious structure of the Old Covenant (Leviticus 27:30; Numbers 18:21, 24; Deuteronomy 14:22–29). Because Free Grace and dispensational theology holds that believers today live under the New Covenant rather than under the Mosaic Law’s specific civil and ceremonial requirements (Romans 6:14; Galatians 3:24–25), the question of whether the tithe itself carries forward as a binding requirement deserves careful, honest examination rather than simply assuming Old Testament practice transfers automatically.

It helps to see what the Law actually required, because it was more than a single tenth. Israel gave a tithe to the Levites, who in turn gave a tenth of what they received to the priests (Numbers 18:21–28); a second, festival tithe was brought to the place the LORD chose and eaten there in a feast before Him, shared with the Levite (Deuteronomy 14:22–27); and every third year a tithe was stored in each town for the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow (Deuteronomy 14:28–29; 26:12). Taken together, these averaged roughly 23 percent a year. The temple tax was separate: a half-shekel “ransom” paid at the census as “atonement money” for the service of the tabernacle (Exodus 30:11–16), later collected yearly for the temple (Matthew 17:24–27). All of this was Israel’s requirement under the Mosaic Law; none of it is commanded to the church in the New Testament.

Notably, the New Testament never repeats the tithe as a command for the church. The three Gospel references to tithing (Matthew 23:23; Luke 11:42; 18:12) all concern the Pharisees, including Jesus’ critique of them for tithing meticulously while neglecting “the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faith” (Matthew 23:23) — addressed to Jews still living under the Mosaic Law prior to the cross, not instituting a new requirement for the church. Paul, writing extensively to Gentile and Jewish believers alike about financial giving, never once mentions the tithe or the specific ten-percent figure, which would be a remarkable omission if the New Testament intended tithing to continue as a binding requirement for the church.

What the New Testament teaches instead is a pattern of giving characterized by different principles than a fixed percentage: giving that is voluntary and cheerful rather than compelled — “not grudgingly or of necessity; for God loves a cheerful giver” (2 Corinthians 9:7); giving that is proportional to one’s actual means and blessing rather than a flat rate applied identically regardless of circumstance — “let each one of you lay something aside, storing up as he may prosper” (1 Corinthians 16:2); and giving that is sacrificial and generous, exemplified by the Macedonian churches who gave “beyond their ability” out of “deep poverty” and “great trial of affliction” (2 Corinthians 8:2–3).

This does not mean the tithe’s historic ten-percent figure is irrelevant or that believers should give less thoughtfully than it implies — many believers find it a helpful, concrete starting benchmark for cheerful, proportional giving, and Abraham’s own voluntary tithe to Melchizedek (Genesis 14:20), given centuries before the Mosaic Law existed, shows that grateful, generous giving in response to God’s blessing has always been a fitting response for God’s people, Old Covenant or New. It means the New Testament grounds the practice of giving in gratitude, love, and grace-motivated generosity rather than in a legally binding, fixed percentage carried over automatically from the Mosaic system.

Consistent with the Free Grace framework throughout this paper, a believer’s giving — like every other area of discipleship — is never a condition of salvation or a measure by which a believer’s standing before God is assessed; it is one among many expressions of grateful discipleship, evaluated for reward at the Judgment Seat of Christ (2 Corinthians 5:10), offered from a heart already secure in Christ rather than as a payment toward or maintenance of that security.
