Why Does the Old Testament Seem So Violent?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

The conquest was a bounded, specific judgment on long-entrenched evil, delayed for centuries and open to mercy, as Rahab shows. At the cross God bore judgment Himself.

Key versesGenesis 15:16 · Deuteronomy 9:5 · Joshua 6:25

Passages describing divinely commanded warfare, such as the conquest narratives in Joshua, are among the most difficult in Scripture for modern readers, and a thoughtful answer requires taking the difficulty seriously rather than dismissing it, while also placing these passages in their full biblical and historical context rather than reading them in isolation.

First, these commands were bounded, specific, and tied to a particular moment in redemptive history — the judgment of specific Canaanite nations at a specific point in time — rather than a general biblical mandate for violence that continues into the present or applies to any other people group. God’s own explanation to Abraham centuries earlier reveals that this judgment was long-delayed precisely because it was not yet deserved: “the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete” (Genesis 15:16) — God waited roughly four hundred years, giving the Canaanite nations centuries of opportunity, before executing judgment on practices Scripture elsewhere describes in graphic detail, including child sacrifice to false gods (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 12:31). Moses told Israel plainly that the land was not given for their righteousness, “but because of the wickedness of these nations that the LORD your God drives them out from before you” (Deuteronomy 9:5; cf. 9:4).

Second, these accounts are consistently framed as divine judgment on severe moral corruption, not as ethnic conquest for its own sake or for Israel’s material benefit alone — the same standard applied to Israel itself whenever it fell into the identical practices, resulting in Israel’s own eventual judgment and exile by other nations (2 Kings 17:7–18). Scripture does not present God as playing favorites in applying this standard; the moral standard used to judge Canaan is the same standard later used to judge Israel and, ultimately, every nation (Amos 1–2, a series of judgment oracles against surrounding nations and Israel alike). And judgment was never closed to mercy: Rahab of Jericho, a Canaanite who believed the God of Israel (Joshua 2:9–11), was spared with her household and lived among Israel (Joshua 6:25).

Third, and most importantly for understanding Scripture’s own unfolding storyline, the Old Testament’s portrait of God’s justice and holiness is not left in isolation — it is completed and reinterpreted through the person of Christ, who bears the full weight of divine judgment against sin Himself rather than requiring it be executed against sinners directly (Isaiah 53:5–6; Romans 3:25–26). The same God who commanded judgment on entrenched, generations-long evil in the Old Testament is the God who, in the New Testament, absorbs that judgment personally at the cross rather than requiring humanity to bear it — a trajectory of increasing, not decreasing, divine mercy across the whole of Scripture’s storyline, not a contradiction between an Old Testament God of wrath and a New Testament God of love.

None of this eliminates the genuine difficulty modern readers feel confronting these texts, and honest engagement does not require pretending the difficulty away. It does show that these passages describe bounded, specific, morally accountable acts of judgment within a particular historical moment, framed by a God whose patience consistently exceeds His judgment (2 Peter 3:9), and whose ultimate response to human evil, both Testaments together reveal, was to bear its penalty Himself at the cross rather than to inflict it indiscriminately.
