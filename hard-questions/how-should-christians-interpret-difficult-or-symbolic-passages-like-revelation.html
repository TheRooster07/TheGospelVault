How Should Christians Interpret Difficult or Symbolic Passages Like Revelation?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Revelation uses symbols that point to real, literal events, often explained in the text itself. Read that way, it describes a future Tribulation, Christ’s return, and His kingdom.

Key versesRevelation 1:3 · Revelation 1:20 · Revelation 20:4

The book of Revelation, along with other prophetic and apocalyptic material such as Daniel and portions of Ezekiel and Zechariah, uses a distinct literary genre — apocalyptic literature — that employs vivid symbolism to convey real, literal future events, and much confusion arises from either over-literalizing every image or dismissing the entire book as purely symbolic with no literal referent at all. The dispensational and Free Grace hermeneutical tradition represented throughout this paper follows a consistent principle: interpret Scripture literally wherever the text itself signals a straightforward historical or predictive claim, while recognizing that apocalyptic literature regularly uses symbols to represent real, specific, literal realities rather than functioning as pure allegory detached from any actual future fulfillment.

Revelation itself provides the interpretive key for this approach within its own text: John sees “seven golden lampstands” and is then told directly, “the seven lampstands which you saw are the seven churches” (Revelation 1:12, 20) — a symbol standing for a specific, identifiable, literal referent, not an image meant to remain permanently mysterious or purely spiritual. This pattern repeats throughout the book: symbols are regularly explained, either within the text itself or by cross-reference to the Old Testament imagery Revelation consistently draws upon, allowing careful interpreters to move from the symbol to its intended literal referent rather than either stopping at the symbol or inventing an unanchored meaning for it.

This approach also takes seriously the historical occasion of the book: Revelation was written to seven specific, real churches in first-century Asia Minor (Revelation 1:4, 11), facing real persecution and real doctrinal challenges, and its message carried genuine pastoral relevance to those original readers even as it also anticipates future events extending to the very end of history. A sound interpretation holds both the original historical audience and the future predictive dimension together rather than collapsing the book into only one or the other.

The dispensational, premillennial reading followed throughout this paper’s treatment of last things holds that Revelation’s central chapters (roughly 6–19) describe a literal, future seven-year Tribulation period, followed by Christ’s literal, visible return and a literal thousand-year earthly reign (see Q43, “What Is the Millennium, and Why Does It Matter?”), rather than reading these chapters as already fulfilled in the first century or as purely symbolic descriptions of the ongoing church age. This reading is grounded in the consistent, literal fulfillment of previous biblical prophecies — no Old Testament prophecy about Christ’s first coming was fulfilled merely symbolically or spiritually; each was fulfilled in specific, literal, historical detail (see Q6, “How Do We Know the Bible Is Trustworthy?”) — which grounds confidence that prophecies about His second coming will likewise be fulfilled literally rather than only symbolically.

Practically, this means approaching Revelation neither with the confident, detailed prediction charts that have repeatedly embarrassed the church when specific guesses proved wrong, nor with the opposite temptation to treat the entire book as a closed, irrelevant mystery. Revelation’s own repeated purpose statement is worship and encouragement, not primarily prediction for its own sake: “Blessed is he who reads and those who hear the words of this prophecy, and keep those things which are written in it; for the time is near” (Revelation 1:3) — a book meant to move its readers toward faithful endurance and worship of the Lamb who is worthy (Revelation 5:12), whatever remains uncertain about its more difficult details.
