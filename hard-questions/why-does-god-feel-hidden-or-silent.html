Why Does God Feel Hidden or Silent?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Felt silence is not God’s absence. He has spoken fully in His Son and His Word, believers walk by faith rather than sight in this age, and face-to-face fellowship is coming.

Key versesHebrews 1:1–2 · 2 Corinthians 5:7 · 1 Corinthians 13:12

The experience is common even among mature believers: prayers that seem to go nowhere, a sense that God has withdrawn. It helps to say plainly that this feeling does not mean God has actually left, and Scripture never treats felt silence as evidence against His presence.

God has spoken decisively and finally, and that revelation has not been withdrawn: “God ... has in these last days spoken to us by His Son” (Hebrews 1:1–2). The normal means by which He communicates in this age is His completed written Word, not fresh subjective impressions. When His presence feels distant, the Bible remains exactly as available and exactly as full of Him as it was the day it felt close — the silence, where it exists, is experiential, not actual. The book of Esther never mentions God’s name even once, and yet the plot turns on a chain of “coincidences” so precise that the reader is left in no doubt God was governing events the characters could not see Him governing.1 His absence of visible activity is not the absence of actual activity.

This present age is, by design, a period in which believers relate to God by faith rather than by sight (2 Corinthians 5:7; John 20:29). The felt, permanent presence promised for the future — “the tabernacle of God is with men” (Revelation 21:3) — is not yet the ordinary experience of this age; its absence now is a feature of where we stand in God’s unfolding plan, not a defect in His faithfulness. The Psalms give believers permission to say exactly what silence feels like without treating the saying of it as unfaithful: “My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?” (Psalm 22:1). The psalmists cry out precisely because they still believe God is there to hear — true silence would produce no prayer at all.

There is also a purifying purpose to felt absence. “Count it all joy when you fall into various trials, knowing that the testing of your faith produces patience” (James 1:2–3). Seasons without vivid felt nearness strip away a faith that had quietly come to rest on feelings rather than on the object of faith Himself. Dallas Willard observed that the spiritual disciplines exist to train the whole person, including the emotions, to remain oriented toward God across the full range of felt experience, not only its peaks.2 And because eternal life rests on Christ’s finished work and God’s unbreakable promise, not on felt closeness (John 10:28–29), a believer in a dry season is not thereby in danger of losing anything essential.

The hiddenness of this age is explicitly temporary: “Now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face” (1 Corinthians 13:12). Until an unmediated, face-to-face fellowship arrives, His Word remains as near as it ever was, His promises as certain as they ever were — and the silence, however real it feels, is never the whole story.
