50 Hard Questions · Question 47 of 50

What Are Spiritual Gifts, and Do They Still Operate Today?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Every believer has at least one spiritual gift to serve the church in love. Christians differ on whether the sign-gifts continue, but all agree every gift is to be used faithfully.

Key verses1 Corinthians 12:4–7 · 1 Peter 4:10 · 1 Corinthians 13:1–3

Spiritual gifts are Spirit-given abilities distributed to every believer at salvation for the purpose of building up the church, not natural talents earned or a status symbol reserved for especially mature believers. “There are diversities of gifts, but the same Spirit ... But the manifestation of the Spirit is given to each one for the profit of all” (1 Corinthians 12:4, 7). Paul’s central image for how these gifts function is the human body: many distinct parts, each with a different function, all necessary, none able to claim self-sufficiency or superiority over the others (1 Corinthians 12:14–21).

The New Testament provides several overlapping lists of specific gifts rather than one single, exhaustive catalog: Romans 12:6–8 names prophecy, service, teaching, exhortation, giving, leadership, and mercy; 1 Corinthians 12:8–10 names wisdom, knowledge, faith, healing, miracles, prophecy, discerning of spirits, tongues, and interpretation of tongues; Ephesians 4:11 names apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, and teachers. The variety and non-identical overlap among these lists suggests Scripture is illustrating the range and diversity of gifts God distributes rather than providing a closed, complete inventory.

Whether the more visibly miraculous gifts — particularly tongues, the interpretation of tongues, and the gift of healing exercised at will — continue in the same form as the apostolic era is a debated question among Bible-believing Christians, addressed in more general terms in Q25, “Does God Still Perform Miracles Today?”. Continuationist believers hold that all the gifts listed in the New Testament remain fully operative without change; cessationist believers hold that the specific sign-gifts tied to authenticating new revelation served their purpose once the New Testament canon was completed, while gifts oriented toward ordinary edification — teaching, service, mercy, giving, leadership — clearly continue without dispute on either side.

Whatever position a believer holds on that specific debate, Scripture’s practical instruction about gifts applies without controversy across the whole spectrum of views: every believer, without exception, has received at least one gift and bears responsibility to identify and use it rather than neglect it (1 Peter 4:10, “as each one has received a gift, minister it to one another”), gifts are to be exercised “in love” rather than for personal status or recognition (1 Corinthians 13:1–3, where the absence of love renders even the most spectacular gift “nothing”), and no single gift, however impressive its outward display, is more spiritually significant than another in God’s own assessment of the body’s health (1 Corinthians 12:22–25).

For the individual believer, the practical takeaway is straightforward regardless of which side of the continuationist debate one holds: discovering, developing, and faithfully using whatever gift God has given — whether a quiet gift of mercy or a more visible gift of teaching — is a normal, expected part of the Christian life, evaluated for faithfulness at the Judgment Seat of Christ (see Q38, “What Is the Judgment Seat of Christ?”), not an optional pursuit reserved for especially gifted or spiritually elite believers.
