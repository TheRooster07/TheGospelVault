No Throne Beyond His Reach

“It is He who changes the times and the epochs; He removes kings and establishes kings.”
— Daniel 2:21 (NASB95)

Daniel was a young exile in the most powerful city on earth. Babylon’s king had just ordered every wise man in the kingdom put to death, Daniel included. That night God showed Daniel the king’s dream, and before Daniel said a word to the king, he worshiped: “He removes kings and establishes kings.”

The dream laid out the future of the world’s great empires, one after another. Daniel 8 would later name two of them outright, Media-Persia and Greece. Prophets foretold the fall of proud cities like Nineveh and Tyre, and Jesus wept over Jerusalem and said its temple would be torn down stone from stone. Each word came true.

The headlines can make it seem as if presidents, markets and armies run the world. Daniel knew better. The God who raised and removed empires still holds every throne. You can face an uncertain world with a quiet heart, because history is in His hands, and so are you.

Reflect

  1. What news or world event has unsettled you lately?
  2. How does Daniel’s response to a death sentence (worship first) challenge the way you respond to pressure?
  3. What would change in your week if you truly believed no ruler is beyond God’s reach?

Kingdoms rise and fall; the God who names them stands forever.

I. The God Who Rules the Rise and Fall of Kings

A. A Prayer Under a Death Sentence

Daniel 2 opens with a king who cannot sleep. Nebuchadnezzar, ruler of Babylon, had a dream that troubled him, and he demanded that his wise men tell him both the dream and its meaning (Daniel 2:1–2). When they protested that no one on earth could do such a thing, he ordered them all executed. Daniel and his three friends were among those marked for death.

Daniel asked for time, gathered his friends to pray, and “the mystery was revealed to Daniel in a night vision” (Daniel 2:19). Before he went to the king, before he secured his own safety, he worshiped. Our key verses are that prayer. They are not a theory of history written in a quiet study. They are the praise of a young man who had just seen God answer, while the sword was still hanging over him.

B. “He Changes the Times and the Epochs”

Daniel praises God for three things. First, wisdom and power belong to Him (2:20). Second, He governs history: “It is He who changes the times and the epochs; He removes kings and establishes kings” (2:21). The words for “times” and “epochs” (Aramaic iddan and zeman) cover both the long ages of history and the appointed moments within it. The verbs are participles: God is continually changing, removing and setting up. He is not a distant founder who wound up the world and walked away. He is actively ruling it.

That claim is bold anywhere, but it was especially bold in Babylon. Nebuchadnezzar believed he had built his kingdom by his own power (Daniel 4:30). Daniel tells him, in effect, that his throne was given and could be taken. The king would learn that lesson the hard way in chapter 4, and Belshazzar, a later king, would learn it on the night Babylon fell (Daniel 5:30).

Nebuchadnezzar’s own testimony in Daniel 4 is one of the most remarkable confessions in Scripture. After God humbled him for his pride, the most powerful man on earth wrote: “His dominion is an everlasting dominion, and His kingdom endures from generation to generation … He does according to His will in the host of heaven and among the inhabitants of earth; and no one can ward off His hand” (Daniel 4:34–35). The king learned what Daniel had prayed in chapter 2. Thrones are on loan.

34 “But at the end of that period, I, Nebuchadnezzar, raised my eyes toward heaven and my reason returned to me, and I blessed the Most High and praised and honored Him who lives forever;

For His dominion is an everlasting dominion,

And His kingdom endures from generation to generation.

35 “All the inhabitants of the earth are accounted as nothing,

But He does according to His will in the host of heaven

And among the inhabitants of earth;

And no one can ward off His hand

Or say to Him, ‘What have You done?’

— Daniel 4:34–35 (NASB95)

C. “He Reveals the Profound and Hidden Things”

Third, God reveals what no one else can know: “It is He who reveals the profound and hidden things; He knows what is in the darkness, and the light dwells with Him” (2:22). This is the same point Isaiah made with the idols. Babylon’s experts could not tell the king his dream, let alone the future. Daniel told the king plainly that “there is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries” (Daniel 2:28). Daniel claimed no credit. The God who rules history is the God who can tell it ahead of time.

II. Empires Foretold: Daniel 2 and 8

A. The Great Statue

The king had seen a huge statue with a head of gold, chest and arms of silver, belly and thighs of bronze, legs of iron, and feet of iron mixed with clay. A stone “cut out without hands” struck its feet, the whole statue was crushed to chaff, and the stone became a mountain that filled the earth (Daniel 2:31–35).

Daniel interpreted the first part directly: “You are the head of gold” (2:38). Babylon was the first kingdom. After it would arise “another kingdom inferior to you, then another third kingdom of bronze, which will rule over all the earth. Then there will be a fourth kingdom as strong as iron” (2:39–40). Daniel does not name the second, third and fourth kingdoms in chapter 2, but the rest of the book identifies them. In chapter 5 Babylon falls to “the Medes and Persians” (5:28). In chapter 8, as we will see, the angel names Media-Persia and Greece. Conservative interpreters, including dispensational scholars such as John Walvoord, identify the four as Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome.1

Notice two patterns in the statue. The metals decline in value from top to bottom: gold, silver, bronze, iron, then iron mixed with clay. Yet they increase in hardness and strength until the last, which is strong but unstable. Interpreters have long seen in this a picture of world power: each empire less splendid than the one before, but more crushing, until the final form is a brittle mixture that cannot hold together (Daniel 2:41–43). Human empires do not progress toward paradise. They move toward judgment, and the stone ends them.

39 After you there will arise another kingdom inferior to you, then another third kingdom of bronze, which will rule over all the earth.

40 Then there will be a fourth kingdom as strong as iron; inasmuch as iron crushes and shatters all things, so, like iron that breaks in pieces, it will crush and break all these in pieces.

— Daniel 2:39–40 (NASB95)

History followed that sequence exactly. Babylon fell to Cyrus and the Medes and Persians in 539 BC. Persia ruled for two centuries until Alexander the Great swept it away in the 330s BC. Alexander’s Greek empire was in turn swallowed piece by piece by Rome, the iron kingdom that crushed everything before it. Four empires, in order, from a dream given to a Babylonian king before the second of them had arisen.

B. Daniel 8: The Ram, the Goat, and the Broken Horn

Daniel 8 is even more specific, because the angel Gabriel names the kingdoms. Daniel saw a ram with two horns, one higher than the other, overrun by a goat from the west with one large horn between its eyes. The large horn was broken at the height of its strength, and four horns grew up in its place (Daniel 8:3–8). Then Gabriel explained:

20 The ram which you saw with the two horns represents the kings of Media and Persia.

21 The shaggy goat represents the kingdom of Greece, and the large horn that is between his eyes is the first king.

22 The broken horn and the four horns that arose in its place represent four kingdoms which will arise from his nation, although not with his power.

— Daniel 8:20–22 (NASB95)

Media and Persia (the ram with two horns, Persia the stronger) were conquered by Greece (the goat). The “first king” of Greece, the large horn, is Alexander the Great, who conquered the Persian Empire with astonishing speed. He died in 323 BC at about thirty-two, at the height of his power. His empire did not pass to a son. After years of struggle it settled into four kingdoms under his generals, “although not with his power.” Daniel saw it in the third year of Belshazzar (8:1), more than two hundred years before Alexander was born.2

Even the speed of Alexander’s conquest is in the vision. The goat came “from the west over the surface of the whole earth without touching the ground” (Daniel 8:5). Alexander crossed into Asia in 334 BC and within about a decade had conquered the Persian Empire from Asia Minor to the edge of India. Few conquests in history moved so fast. A picture of a goat whose feet do not touch the ground is a fitting image for it.

Because the details are so precise, critical scholars date Daniel to the 160s BC and treat these chapters as history written to look like prophecy. We will look at that objection in Study 4. For now notice what even a late date cannot explain: the fourth, iron kingdom of chapter 2 is Rome, which had not yet conquered the east in the 160s BC, and Daniel 9 places the Messiah’s death before the city and sanctuary are destroyed (9:26), a destruction that came in AD 70. John Lennox’s Against the Flow is a helpful, readable treatment of these questions.3

C. The Kingdom That Will Never Be Destroyed

The dream does not end with Rome. The stone cut without hands strikes the statue on its feet and fills the earth. Daniel explains: “In the days of those kings the God of heaven will set up a kingdom which will never be destroyed … it will crush and put an end to all these kingdoms, but it will itself endure forever” (Daniel 2:44). Then he adds a sentence worth underlining: “the great God has made known to the king what will take place in the future; so the dream is true and its interpretation is trustworthy” (2:45).

Dispensational interpreters understand the stone as Christ at His second coming, when He will establish His kingdom on earth, crushing the final form of Gentile world power rather than slowly transforming it.4 The point for this study is the logic of Daniel 2:45. The fulfilled parts of the dream (Babylon, Persia, Greece, Rome) are the guarantee of the part still to come. The same God who got the first four kingdoms right will bring in the fifth.

III. Cities Under Judgment

The prophets also spoke about individual cities. These prophecies have an advantage for us: we can look at the places today. Bloom points out that one of the strengths of fulfilled prophecy as evidence is that some fulfillments can still be investigated: is the present state of a city consistent with what was said about it thousands of years ago?5

A. Nineveh

Nineveh was the capital of Assyria, the empire that destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BC. In Nahum’s day it seemed invincible. Nahum announced its fall: “All who see you will shrink from you and say, ‘Nineveh is devastated! Who will grieve for her?’” (Nahum 3:7). In 612 BC a coalition of Babylonians and Medes captured and destroyed it. The city was so thoroughly ruined that for centuries its site lay as mounds of earth, until archaeologists uncovered its palaces in the nineteenth century.

Nineveh’s story also shows God’s patience. More than a century earlier, Jonah had preached there, and when the city repented, “God relented concerning the calamity which He had declared He would bring upon them” (Jonah 3:10). God’s warnings are not cold forecasts. They carry an invitation. When Nineveh later returned to its cruelty, Nahum announced a judgment that would not be turned back: “Your wound is incurable” (Nahum 3:19). The same God who had shown mercy kept His word in judgment.

B. Babylon

Isaiah and Jeremiah foretold Babylon’s ruin in strong terms: it would be “as when God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah” and “never be inhabited” (Isaiah 13:19–20); it would “become a heap of ruins … without inhabitants” (Jeremiah 51:37). We should be careful and honest here. Babylon fell to Cyrus in 539 BC without a long siege, and the city was not destroyed that day. It declined slowly over the following centuries until it was abandoned. Its ruins remain a desolate site in Iraq.

Isaiah 13 also speaks of “the day of the Lord” (13:6, 9), language the prophets use for God’s final judgment. For that reason many dispensational interpreters see a further fulfillment still ahead, connected with the Babylon of Revelation 17–18.6 Either way, the ancient city Isaiah called “the beauty of kingdoms” is a ruin, as the prophets said.

C. Tyre

The most detailed city prophecy is Ezekiel 26, spoken against Tyre, the wealthy Phoenician sea power that rejoiced at Jerusalem’s fall. It is also the prophecy skeptics challenge most often, so it deserves care.

The reason for the judgment matters. When Babylon broke through Jerusalem’s walls, Tyre gloated: “Aha, the gateway of the peoples is broken; it has opened to me. I shall be filled, now that she is laid waste” (Ezekiel 26:2). Tyre saw Jerusalem’s ruin as a business opportunity. God’s answer was that Tyre itself would become a ruin. Pride and cruelty toward God’s people do not go unnoticed by the God who rules the nations.

3 therefore thus says the Lord God, ‘Behold, I am against you, O Tyre, and I will bring up many nations against you, as the sea brings up its waves.

4 They will destroy the walls of Tyre and break down her towers; and I will scrape her debris from her and make her a bare rock.

5 She will be a place for the spreading of nets in the midst of the sea, for I have spoken,’ declares the Lord God, ‘and she will become spoil for the nations.

— Ezekiel 26:3–5 (NASB95)

Ezekiel says “many nations” will come against Tyre “as the sea brings up its waves” (26:3), that is, in successive waves, not all at once. The first wave is Nebuchadnezzar (26:7–11), and the pronoun is “he”: he will slay the people “on the mainland” and besiege the city. Then the pronoun changes to “they” (26:12): they will plunder it, break down its walls, and “throw your stones and your timbers and your debris into the water.” The city will become “a bare rock … a place for the spreading of nets” (26:5, 14).

History unfolded in waves. Nebuchadnezzar besieged Tyre for about thirteen years and destroyed the mainland city, but the island fortress offshore held out. Ezekiel himself records that Nebuchadnezzar got “no wages from Tyre” for all his labor (Ezekiel 29:18). Notice that the prophet did not hide this. Then, in 332 BC, Alexander the Great came. To reach the island he scraped the ruins of the old mainland city into the sea to build a causeway, and took the island by storm. Stones, timbers and debris were thrown into the water, just as Ezekiel had said “they” would do. McDowell and Geisler both treat Tyre at length; we will return to the hardest questions (including “you will be built no more”) in Study 4.7

IV. Jerusalem and the Temple

A. Jesus Weeps and Warns

The most important city prophecy in the New Testament came from Jesus Himself. As His disciples admired the temple’s massive stones, He said, “Do you not see all these things? Truly I say to you, not one stone here will be left upon another, which will not be torn down” (Matthew 24:2). As He approached Jerusalem a few days earlier, He wept over it:

43 For the days will come upon you when your enemies will throw up a barricade against you, and surround you and hem you in on every side,

44 and they will level you to the ground and your children within you, and they will not leave in you one stone upon another, because you did not recognize the time of your visitation.”

— Luke 19:43–44 (NASB95)

Jesus gave specific details: a barricade (charax, a palisade or siege wall), the city surrounded, its people killed, not one stone left on another. He also gave the reason: “because you did not recognize the time of your visitation” (19:44). The nation’s leaders had rejected their Messiah.

B. Fulfilled in AD 70

20 “But when you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, then recognize that her desolation is near.

21 Then those who are in Judea must flee to the mountains, and those who are in the midst of the city must leave, and those who are in the country must not enter the city;

22 because these are days of vengeance, so that all things which are written will be fulfilled.

23 Woe to those who are pregnant and to those who are nursing babies in those days; for there will be great distress upon the land and wrath to this people;

24 and they will fall by the edge of the sword, and will be led captive into all the nations; and Jerusalem will be trampled under foot by the Gentiles until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled.

— Luke 21:20–24 (NASB95)

Roughly forty years later, in AD 70, the Roman general Titus surrounded Jerusalem. The Jewish historian Josephus, an eyewitness, describes the siege walls the Romans built, the famine inside the city, and the burning of the temple.8 The temple was destroyed, and Jerusalem was left in ruins. The early church historian Eusebius records that the believers in Jerusalem, warned beforehand, left the city and escaped to Pella across the Jordan, just as Jesus had told them to flee (Luke 21:21).9

Jesus also said, “Jerusalem will be trampled under foot by the Gentiles until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled” (Luke 21:24). That phrase looks beyond AD 70 to a long period of Gentile domination and to an end point God has set. It points ahead to Study 3, where we will look at the scattering and regathering of Israel.10

V. Why This Matters

A. History Is Not Out of Control

When we read the news, the world can look like a storm of powers and accidents. Daniel saw it differently. Empires rise and fall on God’s schedule. Proud cities fall when He says. Even the destruction of the temple, a disaster for the Jewish people, happened exactly as Jesus had warned, with His followers spared because they believed His word. Believers do not have to be naive about evil, but they do not have to be afraid of it either.

Think of the believers in Jerusalem in the late 60s AD. Armies were gathering, the city was in turmoil, and many voices said God would surely defend His temple. The believers remembered what Jesus had said: when you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, flee. They did, and they lived. Trusting His word was not blind optimism. It was the most realistic thing they could do. The same is true for us. God’s word does not promise that we will avoid every hardship, but it is the one sure guide through history, because it comes from the One who writes it.

B. The Fulfilled Past Guarantees the Promised Future

Daniel’s own logic applies to us: because the dream’s fulfilled parts are true, “the dream is true and its interpretation is trustworthy” (Daniel 2:45). Four empires came and went as foretold. The cities fell. The temple fell. That record gives us every reason to trust what God has said about the future, including His kingdom, and including His promise to us.

The kingdom that will never be destroyed is Christ’s. Everyone who believes in Him has eternal life now (John 5:24) and will share in that kingdom when He comes. Babylon, Persia, Greece and Rome are gone. His word, and His promise, remain.

Lord, thank You.

What was Daniel’s situation when he prayed Daniel 2:20–23?Under a death sentence with all the wise men of Babylon; God had just revealed the king’s dream to him.
What four kingdoms does the statue represent?Babylon (named in 2:38), then Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome — the conservative reading, confirmed by Daniel 5:28 and 8:20–21.
What two kingdoms does Gabriel name in Daniel 8:20–21?Media and Persia (the ram) and Greece (the goat); the large horn is the first king, Alexander.

Part 2 · Discussion Questions

1. Daniel worshiped before he went to the king (Daniel 2:19–23). What does that teach us about responding to pressure?

Show Leader Note

Daniel’s confidence rested on who God is, not on the outcome being secured yet. Praise came first because God’s rule over kings was settled whether or not the king spared him.

2. Why is it significant that Daniel 8 names Persia and Greece rather than leaving the kingdoms unnamed?

Show Leader Note

Names make the prophecy checkable. A specific prediction can be shown false; Daniel’s were shown true, centuries before the events.

3. How should we handle the Babylon and Tyre prophecies honestly with a skeptic?

Show Leader Note

Admit what happened (Babylon declined slowly; Nebuchadnezzar did not take the island), note the prophecies’ own wording (“many nations … as waves”), and show how the fulfillment unfolded. Ezekiel 29:18 shows the prophet himself did not hide the facts.

4. What do Jesus’ predictions about AD 70 tell us about Him?

Show Leader Note

He spoke as a true prophet, with specific details that came true within a generation — and His followers were spared because they believed His warning.

5. How does the fulfilled history in Daniel strengthen your trust in God’s promises about the future and about eternal life?

Show Leader Note

Daniel 2:45 reasons from fulfilled to unfulfilled: the dream is true. The same God who ruled the empires has promised a kingdom that will never be destroyed and eternal life to all who believe (John 5:24).

⑤ Application

Living and sharing what we have studied

Application 1 — Worship Before the Answer Arrives

Daniel 2:19–23

From ③ I.A–C.

Daniel praised God while the death sentence still stood. Faith does not wait for circumstances to improve before it trusts God’s rule. It begins with who He is.

Reflection: What pressure are you facing where praise would be an act of faith?

Practical Step: Pray Daniel 2:20–22 aloud over one specific worry each day this week.

Application 2 — Read the News Through Daniel 2:21

Daniel 2:21

From ③ I.B and V.A.

No election, war or market sits outside God’s hand. That does not make evil less real, but it makes fear less necessary. He removes kings and establishes kings.

Reflection: Which headline has shaped your mood more than God’s word this week?

Practical Step: Before reading or watching the news, read Daniel 2:20–22 first.

Application 3 — Tell the Story of Tyre Honestly

Ezekiel 26:3–14; 29:18

From ③ III.C; cf. McDowell; Geisler.

Tyre is one of the clearest and most challenged prophecies. Learn the outline — waves of nations, Nebuchadnezzar on the mainland, Alexander’s causeway — and tell it accurately, including what Ezekiel himself admitted.

Reflection: Are you tempted to overstate a good story to win an argument?

Practical Step: Explain the Tyre prophecy to someone in your group in three minutes, with the verses.

Application 4 — Trust the King Whose Kingdom Stands

Daniel 2:44–45; John 5:24

From ③ II.C and V.B; Walvoord; Pentecost.

Every empire in Nebuchadnezzar’s dream is gone. Christ’s kingdom will never be destroyed, and everyone who believes in Him has eternal life now. The fulfilled past is God’s guarantee of the promised future.

Reflection: Are you building your hopes on kingdoms that will pass, or on the One that will stand?

Practical Step: Memorize Daniel 2:44 and share it with someone anxious about the future.

End of Study · Daniel 2:20–22
