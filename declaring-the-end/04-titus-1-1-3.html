② Devotional

Drawn from the commentary · for personal reading

The Promise Older Than Time

“In the hope of eternal life, which God, who cannot lie, promised long ages ago.”
— Titus 1:2 (NASB95)

Over these studies we have watched God keep His word: kings named before they were born, empires that rose and fell in order, cities brought down, a scattered nation preserved and gathered, and a Messiah whose portrait was drawn centuries in advance. Skeptics raise honest questions, and we can answer them honestly. God’s record holds.

Paul sums it up in a single phrase: God “cannot lie.” Every fulfilled prophecy is a witness to that. And Paul attaches that truth to the most important promise of all — eternal life, promised “long ages ago,” before the world began.

That is where all the evidence is meant to lead. Not to a debate won, but to a promise trusted. John wrote so that those who believe in the name of the Son of God “may know” that they have eternal life. The God who has never failed to keep His word has promised it. You can rest there.

Reflect

  1. Which fulfilled prophecy from this series has strengthened your confidence most?
  2. What question about the Bible do you still find hard, and how might you look into it honestly?
  3. Do you know that you have eternal life, on the basis of God’s promise alone?

The God who cannot lie has promised. That settles it.

③ Commentary

Teacher preparation resource · Full text organized under outline headings

PropositionFulfilled prophecy can be examined honestly, and the common objections to it can be answered without overclaiming. Reformed and dispensational believers differ on how evidence functions and how some prophecies are fulfilled, but Scripture itself points to fulfilled prophecy as evidence. The God whose word has proven true has promised eternal life to everyone who believes in His Son — and because He cannot lie, that promise can be known and trusted.

I. Weighing the Evidence Fairly

A. Ready, With Gentleness and Reverence

15 but sanctify Christ as Lord in your hearts, always being ready to make a defense to everyone who asks you to give an account for the hope that is in you, yet with gentleness and reverence;

— 1 Peter 3:15 (NASB95)

Peter tells believers to be ready to make “a defense” (apologia, the word from which we get “apologetics”) to anyone who asks about the hope within us. He also tells us how: “with gentleness and reverence.” A defense of the faith is not a performance or a fight. It is an honest answer, given respectfully, to a real question. That means we must handle the evidence honestly, including the hard parts.

B. Examining the Scriptures Daily

Luke commends the Jews of Berea because they “received the word with great eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so” (Acts 17:11). The verb (anakrinō) means to examine closely, the way a judge examines a case. The Bereans did not accept Paul’s message without checking it, and they did not reject it without checking it either. That is the posture this study asks of believers and seekers alike. The evidence from prophecy is strong enough to be examined, and the objections are serious enough to deserve real answers.

C. A Fair Test Cuts Both Ways

A fair test requires that we not assume the answer in advance. A believer should not claim more than the evidence shows. But a skeptic should not rule out prophecy before looking at it. Many objections to biblical prophecy begin with the assumption that predictive prophecy is impossible, and then conclude that every accurate prophecy must have been written after the event. That is not a conclusion from the evidence. It is a starting point that decides the case before the evidence is heard.1

Believers have their own temptation. Because we love the Bible, we can be quick to repeat any argument that seems to help it, without checking whether it is true. That does not honor God. The God “who cannot lie” (Titus 1:2) is not served by exaggeration. The best defense of His word is an accurate one. In the sections that follow we take the four most common objections in turn, and then look at how believers in the Reformed tradition see the whole question differently.

II. Objection One: “It Was Written After the Fact”

A. Daniel

The book of Daniel describes the empires of Persia and Greece, and the career of Alexander and his successors, so precisely that many critical scholars date it to about 165 BC, during the persecution under the Seleucid king Antiochus IV. On that view, chapters 8 and 11 are history dressed up as prophecy.

This view goes back to the third-century philosopher Porphyry, a critic of Christianity, who argued that Daniel was written in the time of Antiochus because its prophecies about that period were so accurate. Notice again the reasoning. The prophecies fit the events too well to be prophecy, so they must be history. If God truly knows the future, that reasoning collapses. Accuracy is exactly what we would expect from a true prophet.

There are serious answers to this. First, multiple copies of Daniel were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, and the book was already treated as Scripture by the community that preserved them. That is hard to explain if the book had only just been written. Second, Jesus Himself called Daniel a prophet: “the abomination of desolation which was spoken of through Daniel the prophet” (Matthew 24:15). Third, and most important, even a date of 165 BC does not remove the prophecy. The fourth, iron kingdom of Daniel 2 and 7 fits Rome, which had not yet conquered the east. And Daniel 9:26 says the Messiah would be cut off before the city and the sanctuary were destroyed, which happened in AD 70, more than two centuries after the latest date critics give the book. John Lennox develops these points carefully in Against the Flow, and Geisler surveys them in his encyclopedia.2

B. Isaiah and Cyrus

In Study 1 we met the claim that Isaiah 40–66 was written after Cyrus appeared. The New Testament credits both halves of Isaiah to the one prophet: John quotes Isaiah 53:1 and Isaiah 6:10 together and says, “These things Isaiah said because he saw His glory” (John 12:38–41). The complete Isaiah scroll from Qumran presents the book as a single work. The first-century Jewish historian Josephus even records a tradition that Cyrus read Isaiah’s prophecy about himself and was moved to fulfill it.3 Whatever we make of that tradition, it shows that Jews of Josephus’s day understood the prophecy to come before Cyrus, not after.

C. Jesus and AD 70

Some argue that Jesus’ detailed predictions of Jerusalem’s fall (Luke 19:41–44; 21:20–24) must have been written after AD 70. But there are good reasons to date the Gospels earlier. The book of Acts ends with Paul alive, under house arrest in Rome, preaching “unhindered” (Acts 28:30–31), around AD 62. It does not mention Paul’s death, Peter’s death, the Neronian persecution, or the fall of Jerusalem. Luke wrote his Gospel before Acts (Acts 1:1). Former cold-case detective J. Warner Wallace has made this argument accessible, and Lee Strobel’s interviews in The Case for Christ cover the dating of the Gospels at a popular level.4

D. Prophecies Fulfilled Long After Any Date

Finally, some fulfillments came so long after even the latest proposed dates that the objection cannot touch them. No one dates Moses, Hosea, Isaiah or Ezekiel after AD 70, yet their words about Israel’s worldwide dispersion, preservation and regathering (Study 3) were fulfilled over the following two thousand years. An after-the-fact writer cannot describe events that had not yet happened when the last scroll was copied.

III. Objection Two: “The Prophecies Are Vague”

Some prophecies, like some poetry, can be read more than one way. But many biblical prophecies are not vague at all. They name people (Josiah, Cyrus), nations (Media, Persia, Greece), places (Bethlehem, Bethel, Tyre), sequences (four kingdoms in order), and timing (before the sanctuary is destroyed). They describe outcomes that could easily have failed. That is very different from the ancient oracles whose answers could fit any result, like the famous answer to King Croesus mentioned in Study 1. A vague prophecy cannot be shown false. The prophets made predictions that could have been.

IV. Objection Three: “They Were Self-Fulfilled”

Could people have read the prophecies and simply acted them out? Sometimes people did know the prophecies. But many fulfillments were in the hands of people who did not know or care about Israel’s Scriptures. Alexander did not build his causeway at Tyre to fulfill Ezekiel; he built it to take the island. Titus did not destroy the temple to fulfill Jesus’ words; Josephus even reports that Titus wanted to spare it. Persia and Greece did not rise and fall in order to make Daniel right.

The messianic prophecies make the point even more sharply. No one can arrange the town of his own birth, the timing of his death relative to a Roman siege forty years later, or the actions of the soldiers who crucify him. Jesus was born in Bethlehem because a Roman emperor ordered a census (Luke 2:1–7). The soldiers gambled for His clothing for their own reasons (John 19:23–24). Self-fulfillment does not explain fulfillments carried out by enemies and strangers.

V. Objection Four: “Some Prophecies Failed”

A. Tyre Revisited

Ezekiel said of Tyre, “You will be built no more” (Ezekiel 26:14). Critics point out that there is a town called Tyre (Sur) in Lebanon today. They also note that Nebuchadnezzar did not take the island city, and that Ezekiel himself later said Nebuchadnezzar received no wages from Tyre (Ezekiel 29:18). Bloom admits that it is “no simple matter to refute the charge that Ezekiel’s prophecy failed,” until the whole history is considered.5

When the whole history is considered, the picture changes. Ezekiel said “many nations” would come “as the sea brings up its waves” (26:3), and the pronouns move from Nebuchadnezzar (“he,” 26:7–11) to the nations (“they,” 26:12). Alexander completed what Nebuchadnezzar began, throwing the old city’s stones and timber into the sea. The Tyre of Ezekiel’s day, the proud, independent Phoenician sea power, was never restored to what it had been. Much of the ancient site is ruins, and fishermen there have spread their nets. A later town grew up nearby, as towns often do, but the city God judged did not rise again. And Ezekiel’s candor in 29:18 is itself evidence of honesty. A prophet inventing fulfilled predictions would not record that one had cost Nebuchadnezzar his wages. Geisler and the McDowells both work through the details.6

B. Conditional Warnings and Future Fulfillment

Two other principles help. First, some prophecies of judgment carry an unstated condition. God explains this through Jeremiah: “At one moment I might speak concerning a nation … to uproot, to pull down, or to destroy it; if that nation against which I have spoken turns from its evil, I will relent” (Jeremiah 18:7–8). Nineveh in Jonah’s day is the clearest example (Jonah 3:10). That is not failure. It is mercy, and the prophecy said so from the start.

Second, “not yet fulfilled” is not the same as “failed.” Many prophecies, such as the stone that fills the earth (Daniel 2:44–45), the final gathering of Israel in faith (Zechariah 12:10), and the reign of Messiah on David’s throne, still await fulfillment. A dispensational reading expects those promises to be fulfilled as literally as the past ones were.

VI. A Caution for Believers: Do Not Overclaim

Some popular presentations assign enormous odds against one man fulfilling many prophecies. The best known come from Peter Stoner’s Science Speaks, which the early editions of Evidence That Demands a Verdict made widely known.7 Those numbers can be impressive, but they often backfire with educated listeners, because the starting estimates are debatable. The cumulative portrait is persuasive on its own terms. We do not need to inflate it.

Honesty also means saying when a text works by pattern rather than direct prediction. Matthew applies Hosea 11:1, “Out of Egypt I called My Son,” to Jesus (Matthew 2:15), although Hosea speaks of Israel’s exodus. Matthew is drawing a God-designed pattern: Jesus relives Israel’s story and succeeds where Israel failed. And it means acknowledging real textual questions. At Psalm 22:16, the NASB reads “They pierced my hands and my feet,” following the ancient Greek translation and a Dead Sea manuscript, while the standard Hebrew text reads differently. The point of Psalm 22 does not rest on one word. Truthfulness about small points earns a hearing on the large one.8

VII. A Reformed Counterpoint

A. On Method: How Evidence Functions

Many Reformed theologians, especially in the presuppositional tradition associated with Cornelius Van Til of Westminster Theological Seminary, hold that evidence cannot serve as a neutral foundation for faith. In their view Scripture is self-attesting, and a person comes to accept it as God’s word through the inner testimony of the Holy Spirit rather than through arguments from evidence. Herman Bavinck and Michael Horton, from somewhat different angles, stress Scripture’s self-authentication and the Spirit’s witness.9

There is real truth here. The Holy Spirit persuades, and no argument can do His work. But Scripture itself presents fulfilled prophecy as evidence, on purpose: “so that you may know” (Isaiah 45:3); “so that when it does occur, you may believe” (John 13:19); Paul “reasoned with them from the Scriptures, explaining and giving evidence” (Acts 17:2–3). The Spirit uses evidence. This study holds that fulfilled prophecy is genuine evidence God Himself has given.

In practice, believers in both traditions often meet in the same place. A presuppositional apologist will still point a skeptic to Isaiah 53, and an evidential apologist will still pray for the Spirit to open a skeptic’s heart. The difference is one of emphasis and order. This study begins where Scripture begins in Isaiah 41: God invites the nations to test Him, and He passes the test.

B. On Meaning: How Prophecy Is Fulfilled

Covenant theology also tends to read prophecies such as Daniel’s seventieth week and the Olivet Discourse as fulfilled in the first century, especially in AD 70, and to see many promises to Israel as fulfilled spiritually in the church. Dispensational interpreters answer that the past prophecies we have studied were fulfilled literally: real kings, real empires, real cities, a real scattering and a real regathering. That pattern gives good reason to expect the future prophecies to be fulfilled literally too.10 For the full picture of that future, see the Vault 5 study “God’s Prophetical Program for the Ages.”

VIII. God, Who Cannot Lie, Promised

A. A Promise Made Long Ages Ago

1 Paul, a bond-servant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ, for the faith of those chosen of God and the knowledge of the truth which is according to godliness,

2 in the hope of eternal life, which God, who cannot lie, promised long ages ago,

3 but at the proper time manifested, even His word, in the proclamation with which I was entrusted according to the commandment of God our Savior,

— Titus 1:1–3 (NASB95)

Paul opens his letter to Titus by anchoring everything in one fact: God “cannot lie” (apseudēs, literally “without falsehood”). Moses said the same: “God is not a man, that He should lie … Has He said, and will He not do it? Or has He spoken, and will He not make it good?” (Numbers 23:19). Hebrews adds that it is “impossible for God to lie” (Hebrews 6:18). Every prophecy we have studied is a witness to that truth.

And notice what Paul attaches to it: “the hope of eternal life, which God … promised long ages ago.” The phrase means before the ages of time began. Eternal life is not an afterthought. It is the oldest promise of all, and it rests on the character of a God who cannot lie.11

B. The Testimony of God Is Greater

9 If we receive the testimony of men, the testimony of God is greater; for the testimony of God is this, that He has testified concerning His Son.

10 The one who believes in the Son of God has the testimony in himself; the one who does not believe God has made Him a liar, because he has not believed in the testimony that God has given concerning His Son.

11 And the testimony is this, that God has given us eternal life, and this life is in His Son.

12 He who has the Son has the life; he who does not have the Son of God does not have the life.

13 These things I have written to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, so that you may know that you have eternal life.

— 1 John 5:9–13 (NASB95)

John makes the same point with the language of testimony. We accept human testimony every day. “The testimony of God is greater” (5:9). And God’s testimony is this: “that God has given us eternal life, and this life is in His Son” (5:11). To refuse that testimony is to make God a liar (5:10). To believe it is to have the life He promises.

Then John states his purpose: “These things I have written to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, so that you may know that you have eternal life” (5:13). Assurance is not presumption. It is taking God at His word. Jesus said, “he who hears My word, and believes Him who sent Me, has eternal life, and does not come into judgment, but has passed out of death into life” (John 5:24). The verbs are present (“has”) and perfect (“has passed”): a settled possession from the moment of faith, not contingent on what follows.12

C. Where the Evidence Leads

This series has walked through God’s record: Josiah and Cyrus, the empires of Daniel, Nineveh, Babylon and Tyre, Jerusalem and the temple, Israel scattered, preserved and gathered, and a Messiah whose portrait was drawn ahead of time. The evidence is not meant to end in an argument. It is meant to bring us to a promise. The God who has never failed to keep His word has promised eternal life to everyone who believes in His Son. Because He cannot lie, you can know you have it.

Lord, thank You.

How does Ezekiel 26:3 help with the Tyre prophecy?“Many nations … as the sea brings up its waves” — fulfillment came in waves, Nebuchadnezzar first, Alexander later.
What principle does Jeremiah 18:7–8 teach about prophecies of judgment?Some carry an implied condition; if a nation repents, God may relent (as with Nineveh in Jonah 3:10).
What does Titus 1:2 say about God and eternal life?God, who cannot lie, promised eternal life long ages ago.

Part 2 · Discussion Questions

1. Why is it important to say “we don’t know” or “this is a pattern, not a prediction” when that is true?

Show Leader Note

Truthfulness about small points earns a hearing on the large one (1 Peter 3:15, “gentleness and reverence”). Overclaiming damages credibility and misrepresents Scripture.

2. How would you respond to someone who says, “Prophecies are just written after the events”?

Show Leader Note

Ask what evidence supports the late date, then point to fulfillments no late date can explain: Daniel 9:26 before AD 70, Israel’s dispersion and regathering, and the early dating of the Gospels.

3. What is the strength of the Reformed emphasis on the Spirit’s witness, and why does this study still treat prophecy as evidence?

Show Leader Note

The Spirit alone persuades the heart. But Scripture itself presents fulfilled prophecy as evidence (Isaiah 45:3; John 13:19; Acts 17:2–3), and the Spirit uses it.

4. Why does a literal fulfillment of past prophecy point toward a literal fulfillment of future prophecy?

Show Leader Note

The pattern God has shown — real kings, empires, cities, and a real scattering and regathering — gives reason to expect His promises about Israel and Messiah’s kingdom to be fulfilled the same way.

5. How does 1 John 5:13 change the way you think about assurance of salvation?

Show Leader Note

Assurance comes from believing God’s testimony about His Son. Because God cannot lie, the one who believes may know — not merely hope — that he has eternal life (John 5:24).

⑤ Application

Living and sharing what we have studied

Application 1 — Be a Berean

Acts 17:11

From ③ I.B–C.

The Bereans neither swallowed nor dismissed what they heard; they checked it against Scripture. Bring the same honest curiosity to the hard questions, and invite skeptical friends to do the same.

Reflection: Is there a question about the Bible you have avoided because you feared the answer?

Practical Step: Choose one objection from this study and read one resource on it this month (Lennox, Geisler, or the McDowells).

Application 2 — Answer With Gentleness and Honesty

1 Peter 3:15

From ③ VI; cf. NET Bible note on Psalm 22:16.

Give real reasons, and admit what is hard. Say when a text is a pattern rather than a prediction, and skip inflated statistics. Gentleness and reverence make the strong evidence easier to hear.

Reflection: Do you tend to argue to win, or to help?

Practical Step: Practice explaining one hard text (Tyre, Daniel’s date, or Psalm 22:16) to a believing friend in a calm, honest way.

Application 3 — Trust the God Whose Record Is Perfect

Numbers 23:19; Titus 1:2

From ③ VIII.A; series review.

Every fulfilled prophecy in this series testifies that God does what He says. Let His record shape how you face the unknowns of your own life.

Reflection: Which fulfilled prophecy from this series will you remember when you are tempted to doubt?

Practical Step: Write a one-paragraph summary of this series in your own words and share it with someone.

Application 4 — Know That You Have Eternal Life

1 John 5:13; John 5:24

From ③ VIII.B–C; Wilkin; Bing (GES); cf. Hodges, Absolutely Free!

All the evidence leads to one promise: whoever believes in Jesus has eternal life, a present possession that God, who cannot lie, will not take back. Assurance is simply taking Him at His word.

Reflection: Can you say, on the basis of God’s promise alone, that you have eternal life?

Practical Step: Memorize 1 John 5:13 and use it to explain assurance to someone this week.
