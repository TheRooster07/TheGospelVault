What Is the Difference Between the Church and Israel in God’s Plan?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Israel and the Church are distinct peoples in God’s one plan. God’s unconditional promises to Israel stand and will be fulfilled; the Church is a new body of Jew and Gentile in Christ.

Key versesRomans 11:1–2 · Romans 11:26–29 · Ephesians 3:5–6

The relationship between Israel and the church is one of the most significant hermeneutical questions in all of theology, and it is the central point at which the dispensational tradition represented throughout this paper differs most substantially from Reformed covenant theology — a foundational disagreement this article addresses candidly rather than minimizing.

The dispensational position holds that Israel and the church are two distinct peoples with distinct, unmerged callings and promises, both within God’s single overarching plan. Israel remains the specific, national recipient of the unconditional covenant promises made to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob — land, physical descendants, and worldwide blessing (Genesis 12:1–3; 15:18–21) — promises Paul insists have not been revoked or transferred elsewhere: “I say then, has God cast away His people? Certainly not! ... God has not cast away His people whom He foreknew” (Romans 11:1–2). Paul explicitly anticipates a future, national turning of Israel to faith in her own Messiah: “all Israel will be saved ... For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:26, 29).1

The church, by contrast, is described by Paul as a new entity — not a continuation, replacement, or expansion of Israel under a new name, but “one new man” formed specifically from previously separate Jewish and Gentile believers united in Christ (Ephesians 2:14–15), a “mystery” not disclosed in the Old Testament but revealed for the first time in the New: “which in other ages was not made known to the sons of men, as it now has been revealed by His Holy Spirit to His holy apostles and prophets: that the Gentiles should be fellow heirs, of the same body” (Ephesians 3:5–6). If the church were simply Israel under a different name, this could not accurately be called a previously undisclosed mystery.

This distinction is not a matter of ranking one group as more important or more loved by God than the other — Paul is emphatic that Gentile believers in the church have no basis for arrogance toward unbelieving, ethnic Israel, whom he describes as only temporarily and partially “hardened” (Romans 11:17–25) — but a matter of recognizing that God’s plan operates through two distinct programs that run alongside each other rather than merging into one, with the specific land-and-throne promises made to Israel, (see Q43, “What Is the Millennium, and Why Does It Matter?”), awaiting a future, literal fulfillment for the nation of Israel rather than being reassigned in their entirety to the church.

This is precisely the point on which Reformed covenant theology holds a substantially different reading, understanding the church as the continuation and expansion of true, spiritual Israel, with Old Testament promises to Israel fulfilled spiritually and universally in the church rather than awaiting a separate, future, national fulfillment.2 This paper’s Free Grace and dispensational framework holds the distinct-peoples reading throughout; readers should recognize this as a long-standing, respectful disagreement among Bible-believing Christians, not a matter this article claims to resolve, but one central enough to the dispensational hermeneutic running through this entire paper that it deserves to be named plainly.
