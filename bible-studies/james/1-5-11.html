Wisdom from Above, Wealth from Below

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

Source: The Gospel Vault · Developed from teaching outlines by Dr. C. Norman Sellers, ThD, used by permission

① The Biblical Text

James 1:5–11 · New American Standard Bible (NASB95)

5 But if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all generously and without reproach, and it will be given to him.

6 But he must ask in faith without any doubting, for the one who doubts is like the surf of the sea, driven and tossed by the wind.

7 For that man ought not to expect that he will receive anything from the Lord,

10 and the rich man is to glory in his humiliation, because like flowering grass he will pass away.

② Devotional

“If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all generously and without reproach, and it will be given to him.” — James 1:5

Trials have a way of showing us what we lack. Often the first thing we discover is that we do not know what to do. James’s answer is simple: ask. Before you call a friend or search for advice, go to the Father. He gives to everyone who asks, He gives generously, and He never scolds you for asking again.

But James adds a caution. Ask in faith, not with a divided heart. The person who prays while privately keeping another plan in reserve is like a wave pushed back and forth by the wind. God gives with a whole heart; He invites us to ask with a whole heart too.

Then James lifts our eyes. The poor believer can boast in the riches he has in Christ, and the rich believer can boast that his wealth cannot hold him, because it will fade like a summer flower. Wisdom sees life from eternity’s side.

Reflection Questions

  1. Where in my present situation do I most need God’s wisdom, and have I actually asked Him for it?
  2. Is there a decision in which I am praying one way but keeping another plan in reserve?
  3. What would change if I measured my circumstances, rich or poor, by what lasts forever?

Father, You give generously and without reproach. Today I ask You for wisdom.

Introduction: From Lacking Nothing to Lacking Wisdom

In the last study, James told us that trials, met with endurance, make us “perfect and complete, lacking in nothing” (1:4). Then, in the very next sentence, he uses the same word again: “But if any of you lacks wisdom…” The connection is deliberate. The goal is to lack nothing. But in the middle of a trial we quickly discover what we do lack, and the first thing is usually wisdom. We do not know what to do, what to say, or how to think about what is happening to us.

So far James has given us three keys for meeting trials: a settled attitude (“consider it all joy”), a surrendered will (“let endurance have its perfect result”), and an informed mind (“knowing”). Now he adds two more: believing prayer (1:5–8) and an eternal perspective (1:9–11). The first is faith at work; the second is hope at work. Both are wisdom.

Some have called James the Proverbs of the New Testament, and this passage shows why. It sounds like Solomon: ask God for wisdom, trust Him with an undivided heart, and remember that riches are as brief as a flower in the field.

I. The Invitation to Pray for Wisdom (1:5)

A. The Need: “If Any of You Lacks”

Prayer takes many forms in Scripture. There is praise, worshiping God for who He is. There is confession, dealing honestly with our sin. There is intercession, praying for others. And there is petition, asking God for what we ourselves need. James 1:5 is petition. It is the prayer of a believer who has come to the end of his own understanding.

When we lack wisdom, we have three places to turn. We can depend on ourselves, which in a trial usually means depending on our feelings. We can depend on the counsel of others, which is often good; Scripture says, “in abundance of counselors there is victory” (Proverbs 11:14). Or we can go to God. James does not forbid good counsel, but he puts God first. Go to the Father before you go to anyone else. A simple way to remember it: take it to the throne before you take it to the phone.

B. The Command: Keep On Asking

“Let him ask” is not a suggestion. It is a third-person imperative, a command. And it is in the present tense, which in a command often carries the sense of continuing: keep on asking, ask again and again. That tells us several things:

  • The trial may last a while. We may need fresh wisdom each day.
  • We may need to keep praying until the wisdom comes. Jesus taught the same persistence: “Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you” (Matthew 7:7).
  • God may not answer immediately. His delays are not denials. He has heard, He knows the trial, and He is working through it for our good.

C. The Request: Wisdom

What exactly are we asking for? Biblical wisdom is not the same as information or intelligence. It is the God-given skill to see life as God sees it and to live it accordingly. In a trial, it is the ability to understand what God is doing and to respond in a way that honors Him.

James will later contrast two kinds of wisdom. There is a false wisdom from below:

“This wisdom is not that which comes down from above, but is earthly, natural, demonic.”

James 3:15 (NASB95)

And there is the true wisdom from above:

“But the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits, unwavering, without hypocrisy.”

James 3:17 (NASB95)

That is the wisdom God offers. It does not make us clever; it makes us pure, peaceable, gentle and merciful.

The great Old Testament example is Solomon. When God offered him anything he wanted, he asked for wisdom:

“So give Your servant an understanding heart to judge Your people to discern between good and evil. For who is able to judge this great people of Yours?’”

1 Kings 3:9 (NASB95)

God was pleased and answered generously:

“behold, I have done according to your words. Behold, I have given you a wise and discerning heart, so that there has been no one like you before you, nor shall one like you arise after you.”

1 Kings 3:12 (NASB95)

Solomon’s story also carries a warning. The wisest man who ever lived did not always live wisely. In his old age, “his heart was not wholly devoted to the Lord his God” (1 Kings 11:4). Wisdom received must also be wisdom lived. James will say the same: “Who among you is wise and understanding? Let him show by his good behavior his deeds in the gentleness of wisdom” (3:13).

The final and perfect expression of God’s wisdom is the Lord Jesus Himself. In Christ “are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (Colossians 2:3). Paul says Christ “became to us wisdom from God, and righteousness and sanctification, and redemption” (1 Corinthians 1:30). Every believer, then, already has access to the source of all wisdom. When we ask God for wisdom, we are asking Him to make real in our situation what we already possess in His Son.

D. The Giver: God’s Generous Grace

James does not only tell us to ask; he tells us what kind of God we are asking. Three things are said about His giving:

  • It is universal. He gives “to all.” No believer is too small, too new or too troubled to ask.
  • It is generous. The word is haplōs. It means simply, single-heartedly, without mixed motives, and so, generously. God does not give with one hand while holding back with the other. Notice the contrast this sets up with the “double-minded” man in verse 8. God gives with an undivided heart; He asks us to receive with one.
  • It is without reproach. God does not scold us for asking. He does not say, “You again? Didn’t I already tell you?” or remind us of how foolish we were last time. Here is a picture of how all gifts should be given: in a way that leaves the receiver grateful, not ashamed.

Jesus made the same point about His Father:

“If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give what is good to those who ask Him!”

Matthew 7:11 (NASB95)

E. The Promise: “It Will Be Given”

James ends verse 5 with a flat promise: “and it will be given to him.” Not “it might be,” or “it will be if you deserve it,” but “it will be.” God keeps His word. A believer who asks his Father for wisdom will not be turned away. The wisdom may not come in the form or on the schedule we expect, but it will come.

“This is the confidence which we have before Him, that, if we ask anything according to His will, He hears us.”

1 John 5:14 (NASB95)

II. The Caution Against Divided Prayer (1:6–8)

A. The Caution Stated: Ask in Faith (1:6a)

“But he must ask in faith without any doubting.” Faith here is confidence that God will do what He has promised, in this case, give wisdom. The word for “doubting” is diakrinomai. It describes a person divided against himself, arguing with himself, going back and forth. It is the opposite of Abraham, who “did not waver in unbelief but grew strong in faith” (Romans 4:20).

Why do believers doubt in prayer? Often for one of three reasons: we do not know God’s Word, so we do not know what He has promised; we do not believe God’s Word, so His promises do not settle us; or we do not do God’s Word, so a guilty conscience makes us unsure He will hear. In each case our eyes have moved from the Lord to the wind and waves, as Peter’s did:

“But seeing the wind, he became frightened, and beginning to sink, he cried out, ‘Lord, save me!’”

Matthew 14:30 (NASB95)

“Immediately Jesus stretched out His hand and took hold of him, and said to him, ‘You of little faith, why did you doubt?’”

Matthew 14:31 (NASB95)

It is important to read this caution in its context. James is not talking about how a person receives eternal life. He is writing to believers about receiving answers to prayer, here, the wisdom they need in trials. A believer’s eternal life is not in question when he doubts. What is in question is whether he will receive the help he is asking for.

B. The Caution Pictured: The Surf of the Sea (1:6b)

“For the one who doubts is like the surf of the sea, driven and tossed by the wind.” James was writing to people who knew the sea. The klydōn is the rough water, the surf, heaving one way and then the other. It has no direction of its own; it goes wherever the wind pushes it.

That is a picture of the doubting believer. One day he trusts God; the next he is sure God has forgotten him. In the morning he asks for wisdom; by afternoon he has decided to handle it his own way. Paul uses a similar picture for immature believers, who are “tossed here and there by waves and carried about by every wind of doctrine” (Ephesians 4:14).

C. The Caution Sharpened: The Double-Minded Man (1:7–8)

“For that man ought not to expect that he will receive anything from the Lord, being a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways.” Notice how far this reaches. The doubter will not receive anything, not only wisdom. Divided prayer is unanswered prayer.

The word “double-minded” is dipsychos, literally “two-souled.” This word does not appear in Greek literature before James; he may have coined it. It describes a person with two minds, two loyalties, two sets of plans. He prays to God, but keeps a plan B in his back pocket in case God does not come through. James will use the word once more, calling worldly believers to return to God: “purify your hearts, you double-minded” (4:8).

The result is that he is “unstable in all his ways.” The word akatastatos means unsettled, restless. James uses the same word family for the tongue, “a restless evil” (3:8), and for the “disorder” that worldly wisdom produces (3:16). Double-mindedness never stays in one compartment. A divided heart in prayer soon becomes a divided life.

The remedy is not to try harder to feel certain. It is to fix our eyes on the God who gives “to all generously,” with a single heart, and to ask Him with the same single-heartedness.

III. The Eternal Perspective (1:9–11)

A. God’s Left-Handed Threads

Most bolts and pipe fittings have right-handed threads; you turn them to the right to tighten. But some fittings, such as those on certain gas lines, have left-handed threads, and they turn the opposite way from what you expect. The kingdom of God often works like that. Jesus taught a set of “left-handed threads”:

  • If you want the seat of honor, take the lowest seat. “Go and recline at the last place, so that when the one who has invited you comes, he may say to you, ‘Friend, move up higher’” (Luke 14:10).
  • If you want to be first, be a servant. “Whoever wishes to be first among you shall be your slave; just as the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve” (Matthew 20:27–28). Jesus washed His disciples’ feet, knowing exactly where He had come from and where He was going (John 13:3–5).
  • The way up is down. “Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted” (Luke 14:11).

The supreme example is Christ Himself:

“Being found in appearance as a man, He humbled Himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross.”

Philippians 2:8 (NASB95)

“For this reason also, God highly exalted Him, and bestowed on Him the name which is above every name,”

Philippians 2:9 (NASB95)

James now applies this left-handed principle to rich and poor. The poor are told to boast in their exaltation, and the rich in their humiliation. It sounds backward until we see it from eternity’s side.

B. The Poor Brother: Boast in Your High Position (1:9)

“But the brother of humble circumstances is to glory in his high position.” The word tapeinos means low, poor, of humble station. Many of James’s readers were exactly that. Persecution had cost them homes and livelihoods; famine struck Judea in those years (Acts 11:28–29); and the rich were oppressing them (2:6; 5:4).

So what does a poor believer have to boast about? Is it wrong to boast? Not if the boast is true, and not if the boast is in God. Jeremiah put it this way:

“but let him who boasts boast of this, that he understands and knows Me, that I am the Lord who exercises lovingkindness, justice and righteousness on earth; for I delight in these things,’ declares the Lord.”

Jeremiah 9:24 (NASB95)

The poor brother’s “high position” is his standing in Christ. Materially he may have little. Spiritually he has everything:

“Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ,”

Ephesians 1:3 (NASB95)

“and in Him you have been made complete, and He is the head over all rule and authority;”

Colossians 2:10 (NASB95)

He is “complete” in Christ, lacking nothing he needs, and if he did, there is more available, for he can ask (1:5). James will say it again: “did not God choose the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom which He promised to those who love Him?” (2:5). That is how a poor man boasts: not in himself, but in what God has made him in Christ.

C. The Rich Brother: Boast in Your Humiliation (1:10a)

“And the rich man is to glory in his humiliation.” The command is the same, “glory,” but the content is reversed. The rich believer must not boast in his wealth, but in his tapeinōsis, his lowliness.

Who is this rich man? Some hold that he is an unbeliever, since James later pronounces woe on the oppressive rich (5:1–6). But the grammar most naturally carries “brother” over from verse 9 to verse 10: the lowly brother and the rich brother. And a command to “glory” in something only makes sense for someone who has reason to glory in the Lord. This study takes him to be a wealthy believer.

How can a rich man boast in his humiliation? In one of two ways. He may give much of his wealth away, as Zaccheus did:

“Zaccheus stopped and said to the Lord, ‘Behold, Lord, half of my possessions I will give to the poor, and if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I will give back four times as much.’”

Luke 19:8 (NASB95)

Or, whether he keeps it or not, he must come to see how little his wealth is really worth. Two truths bring him there:

  • Everything he has belongs to the Lord. “What do you have that you did not receive?” (1 Corinthians 4:7). That is not easy for a rich man to accept. He worked for it; he may think he deserves it. But all of it is on loan from God.
  • Everything he has is temporary. His wealth will not last, and neither will he. That is the point James makes next.

When the rich believer accepts these truths, he stands exactly where the poor believer stands: humbled, dependent on God, and trusting in what is eternal. That is his boast. Paul gives the same counsel to wealthy believers:

“Instruct those who are rich in this present world not to be conceited or to fix their hope on the uncertainty of riches, but on God, who richly supplies us with all things to enjoy.”

1 Timothy 6:17 (NASB95)

“Instruct them to do good, to be rich in good works, to be generous and ready to share,”

1 Timothy 6:18 (NASB95)

“storing up for themselves the treasure of a good foundation for the future, so that they may take hold of that which is life indeed.”

1 Timothy 6:19 (NASB95)

It is harder for the rich than for the poor. The poor man has nothing to boast in except eternal things. The rich man has to choose them over what he can see and hold. James will have much more to say to the rich before he is finished (2:1–7; 4:13–5:6).

D. The Fading Flower (1:10b–11)

James explains why the rich must hold their wealth loosely: “because like flowering grass he will pass away.” He is echoing Isaiah:

“The grass withers, the flower fades, When the breath of the Lord blows upon it; Surely the people are grass.”

Isaiah 40:7 (NASB95)

“The grass withers, the flower fades, But the word of our God stands forever.”

Isaiah 40:8 (NASB95)

Then he paints the scene: “For the sun rises with a scorching wind and withers the grass; and its flower falls off and the beauty of its appearance is destroyed.” Anyone who has lived in the Middle East knows that picture. In spring, the hills are covered with wildflowers. Then the hot east wind, the kausōn, blows in off the desert, and within a day or two the flowers are gone.

“So too the rich man in the midst of his pursuits will fade away.” The word for “pursuits” (poreia) can mean journeys, the business trips of a merchant. James will return to that kind of man in 4:13: “Today or tomorrow we will go to such and such a city … and make a profit.” The rich man fades away right in the middle of his plans. Jesus told of such a man, whose barns were full and whose plans were large:

“But God said to him, ‘You fool! This very night your soul is required of you; and now who will own what you have prepared?’”

Luke 12:20 (NASB95)

“So is the man who stores up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God.’”

Luke 12:21 (NASB95)

Wealth itself is not the problem. The problem is trusting in it, as if what fades could hold us up.

IV. Wisdom, Wealth, and the Long View

A. Why These Verses Belong Together

At first, verses 5–8 (prayer for wisdom) and verses 9–11 (rich and poor) can look like separate subjects. But they belong together. Both are about how to think in a trial. Poverty is a trial: it brings anxiety, pressure and humiliation. Wealth is a trial too, in a different way: it tempts us to self-reliance and makes it easy to forget God. And the wisdom God gives in answer to prayer is, above all, the ability to see these circumstances as He sees them.

Wisdom says to the poor believer: “You are not as poor as you look. In Christ you are rich.” Wisdom says to the rich believer: “You are not as secure as you look. Your wealth is a flower in the wind.” Both are brought to the same place: humble dependence on the God who gives generously.

B. What Lasts

Paul states the principle:

“while we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen; for the things which are seen are temporal, but the things which are not seen are eternal.”

2 Corinthians 4:18 (NASB95)

This eternal perspective is not escapism. It is what makes faithful living possible now. Jesus told us to store up “treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust destroys” (Matthew 6:20). Believers who invest in what lasts, in God’s Word, in people, in faithful service, will find that investment waiting for them at the Judgment Seat of Christ. Eternal life is already theirs as a free gift; the reward for faithful stewardship is something they can still lay up (cf. Zane C. Hodges, The Epistle of James: Proven Character Through Testing [Irving, TX: Grace Evangelical Society, 1994]).

And it brings contentment in the meantime. Paul had known both plenty and poverty, and he wrote:

“I know how to get along with humble means, and I also know how to live in prosperity; in any and every circumstance I have learned the secret of being filled and going hungry, both of having abundance and suffering need.”

Philippians 4:12 (NASB95)

“I can do all things through Him who strengthens me.”

Philippians 4:13 (NASB95)

Conclusion: Ask, Trust, Look Up

James has added two more tools for meeting trials well:

  • Ask. When a trial shows you what you lack, go first to God and keep asking. He gives to all, generously and without reproach (1:5).
  • Trust. Ask with an undivided heart. Do not pray with a plan B in your back pocket. The double-minded man is tossed like the surf (1:6–8).
  • Look up. Measure your circumstances by eternity. The poor believer is rich in Christ; the rich believer is as passing as a flower. Both boast in the Lord (1:9–11).

Faith asks; hope looks ahead. And in the next study James will show where both lead: to the crown of life, and to the good God who gives every perfect gift.

Lord, thank You.

In what does the poor brother boast?His high position in Christ: every spiritual blessing (Ephesians 1:3).

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. Why do you think James puts God first, before human counsel, when we lack wisdom?

Show Leader Answer

Only God sees the whole picture and gives wisdom that is pure and peaceable (3:17). Human counsel is valuable (Proverbs 11:14), but it should follow prayer, not replace it.

2. What do the words “generously and without reproach” tell us about the character of God? How should that affect the way we pray?

Show Leader Answer

He gives single-heartedly and never scolds us for asking. We can come to Him often and honestly, without fear of rejection.

3. What does it look like in practice to pray with a “plan B” in reserve? Why does James call this double-minded?

Show Leader Answer

Asking God for direction while already intending to do what we prefer, or praying while trusting something else. It is a divided loyalty, two souls in one person, and it makes us unstable.

4. How can both a poor believer and a rich believer “boast” in the Lord according to 1:9–10?

Show Leader Answer

The poor boasts in his exalted position in Christ; the rich boasts in being humbled, recognizing that wealth is God’s and passing. Both end up trusting what is eternal.

5. How does the picture of the fading flower (1:10–11; Isaiah 40:7–8) help us in a trial involving money?

Show Leader Answer

It shows that wealth and earthly security pass quickly, while God’s Word lasts forever. That frees the poor from despair and the rich from false confidence, and points both to treasure in heaven.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Go to the Throne First

James 1:5; Matthew 7:7

Source: Commentary ③ — I.A–B

When we do not know what to do, our first move reveals where our trust really lies. James invites us to go to God first and to keep asking.

Reflection: When I face a hard decision, who or what do I usually turn to first?

Practical Step: Name one decision you are facing and pray specifically for wisdom about it every day this week before you discuss it with anyone else.

Retire Plan B

James 1:6–8; 4:8

Source: Commentary ③ — II.C

Double-minded prayer asks God for direction while keeping our own plan in reserve. Single-minded prayer is willing to follow what He shows.

Reflection: Is there an area where I am asking God for guidance but have already decided what I will do?

Practical Step: Write down the plan you are holding in reserve, and tell God honestly that you are willing to lay it down.

Boast in the Right Thing

James 1:9–10; Ephesians 1:3

Source: Commentary ③ — III.B–C

Whether we have little or much, our boast is the same: what God has given us in Christ. The poor are richer than they look; the rich are more fragile than they look.

Reflection: Do I tend to measure myself, or others, by money and possessions?

Practical Step: List five spiritual blessings you have in Christ (Ephesians 1) and thank God for them by name.

Invest in What Lasts

James 1:11; 1 Timothy 6:17–19; Matthew 6:20

Source: Commentary ③ — IV.B; cf. Hodges, The Epistle of James

Wealth fades like a flower, but faithful stewardship will be rewarded at the Judgment Seat of Christ. Believers can lay up treasure in heaven now.

Reflection: What am I investing in that will still matter in a hundred years?

Practical Step: Choose one specific, generous act this week with your time or money that will serve someone else in Christ’s name.
