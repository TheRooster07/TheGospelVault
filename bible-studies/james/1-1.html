② Devotional

“James, a bond-servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ.” — James 1:1

James could have opened with his family name. He grew up in the same house as Jesus, ate at the same table, and walked the same streets of Nazareth. Yet when he writes, the only title he claims is “bond-servant.”

That word means more when we remember where James started. “Not even His brothers were believing in Him” (John 7:5). Then the risen Lord appeared to James (1 Corinthians 15:7), and the doubter became a servant. Grace did not hand James a platform. It gave him a Master.

The same grace meets us. Nothing we bring, whether family, record, or reputation, earns eternal life; it is God’s free gift to everyone who believes in His Son. But once we have received it, we are invited into the highest calling there is: to belong to Him and to serve Him.

Reflection Questions

  1. What title or credential am I most tempted to lean on in front of others?
  2. Where was I, spiritually, before the Lord met me, and how does remembering that shape the way I see others who do not yet believe?
  3. What would it look like this week to live as “a bond-servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ” at home or at work?

Whatever I was before, by His grace I now belong to Him, and I gladly serve.

Introduction: Why Study James?

Few books of the Bible have been loved and argued over as much as the Epistle of James. It is short: five chapters and 108 verses. It is packed with commands, dozens of them, on everything from the way we listen to the way we spend money. Its pictures stick in the mind: the wave tossed by the wind, the man who forgets his face in a mirror, the usher who seats the rich man up front, the bit in the horse’s mouth, the spark that sets a forest on fire, the morning mist that is gone by noon.

Some readers have been troubled by it. Others have used it to trouble believers, quoting “faith without works is dead” to suggest that no one can be sure of eternal life. We will take that passage up carefully in Studies 09 and 10. For now it is enough to say that James, read in its own setting and on its own terms, is not a threat to the believer’s assurance. It is a pastor’s letter to people who already belong to Christ, written to help them live well when life is hard.

This first study looks at one verse. But that verse introduces a remarkable man, a scattered church, and a letter with a clear plan. If we understand who James was, to whom he wrote, and why, the rest of the book opens up.

I. The Man Who Wrote the Letter

The letter opens with a single name and no further explanation: “James.” A writer who needs no introduction is a writer everyone already knows. That simple fact helps us find him among the several men in the New Testament who carry the name.

A. Four Men Named James

The New Testament names four men called James:

  • James the son of Zebedee, brother of John and one of the Twelve (Matthew 4:21).
  • James the son of Alphaeus, also one of the Twelve (Matthew 10:3).
  • James the father of Judas (not Iscariot), known only by name (Luke 6:16).
  • James the brother of the Lord, the one Paul calls “James, the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19).

The son of Zebedee was put to death early, in the persecution under Herod Agrippa:

“And he had James the brother of John put to death with a sword.”

Acts 12:2 (NASB95)

The son of Alphaeus and the father of Judas are too little known to sign a letter with a bare first name. That leaves the Lord’s brother, the leader of the Jerusalem church, as the natural author. Some modern scholars suggest that an unknown writer borrowed James’s name to gain a hearing, but the first readers knew James personally, and a borrowed name would hardly have gone unnoticed. A forger would also likely have made more of the family tie. The real James did the opposite: he never mentions it.

B. Why the Lord’s Brother Fits

Several lines point the same way:

  • He writes with settled authority. James gives commands freely and never defends his right to do so.
  • His greeting matches Acts 15. The letter James helped send after the Jerusalem council opens with the same single word, “greetings” (Acts 15:23).
  • He is at home among Jewish believers. He calls their meeting an “assembly” (2:2), using the Greek word for synagogue.
  • He echoes his Brother’s teaching. James 5:12, “your yes is to be yes, and your no, no,” closely follows the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:37). We will see more of these echoes in section V.
  • His Greek is good. The letter is written in clear, polished Greek. That surprises some readers, but Galilee was a crossroads, and a capable man who led a church for decades and dealt with visitors from across the Roman world could well have written this way.

C. What His Name Tells Us

“James” is the English form of the Greek Iakōbos, which is simply the Hebrew name Jacob. Our English Bibles obscure the connection, but every first reader would have heard it. A man named Jacob writes “to the twelve tribes.” The original Jacob was the father of the twelve tribes of Israel, and his family, too, knew hardship, scattering, and God’s faithful care. Scripture does not draw out the parallel, so we should not press it. But it is a fitting picture: a servant named Jacob writing to Jacob’s scattered children, now gathered in Messiah.

D. What Tradition Adds

Later church writers add color to the picture, though we should hold it loosely. Tradition says that James was known as “James the Just” for his upright life, that he lived with Nazirite-like self-denial, and that he prayed so often his knees grew hard like a camel’s. These reports come from Hegesippus, a second-century writer preserved by Eusebius (cf. Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 2.23). There is no real evidence beyond that report, so we treat it as tradition, not as Scripture.

The Jewish historian Josephus is a different kind of witness. He records that in the interval after the Roman governor Festus died, before his successor arrived, the high priest Ananus had “the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James” brought before the Sanhedrin and stoned (cf. Josephus, Antiquities 20.9.1). Most date his death to about AD 62. Hegesippus adds that he was thrown from the temple and then stoned and beaten while he prayed for his attackers; again, that detail is tradition.

II. From Skeptic to Servant

The most remarkable thing about James is not who his brother was, but how long it took him to believe in his Brother.

A. Raised Alongside Jesus

James grew up in the home in Nazareth. When Jesus preached there, His neighbors listed the family by name:

“Is not this the carpenter’s son? Is not His mother called Mary, and His brothers, James and Joseph and Simon and Judas?”

Matthew 13:55 (NASB95)

Think of what James saw. He shared a home with the only sinless person who ever lived. He never once saw Jesus lose His temper in the wrong way, lie, sulk, or take the bigger portion. He knew Him from childhood, through the carpenter’s shop, and into the first years of His public ministry.

B. Unconvinced for Years

Yet familiarity did not produce faith. At one point His family concluded that Jesus had gone too far:

“When His own people heard of this, they went out to take custody of Him; for they were saying, ‘He has lost His senses.’”

Mark 3:21 (NASB95)

John states it plainly:

“For not even His brothers were believing in Him.”

John 7:5 (NASB95)

There is something deeply human here. It is often hardest to see God at work in the people closest to us. And here is encouragement for every believer who prays for an unbelieving family member. James watched Jesus for thirty years and still did not believe. The Lord was not finished with him.

C. Met by the Risen Lord

Paul’s list of resurrection appearances includes a private meeting:

“then He appeared to James, then to all the apostles;”

1 Corinthians 15:7 (NASB95)

Scripture tells us nothing about what was said. But we know the result. By the time the believers gathered to pray before Pentecost, the brothers were there:

“These all with one mind were continually devoting themselves to prayer, along with the women, and Mary the mother of Jesus, and with His brothers.”

Acts 1:14 (NASB95)

James believed in his Brother as the Christ, the Son of God, and in that moment received eternal life, exactly as John says every believer does (John 20:31). Years later Paul mentions that “the brothers of the Lord” traveled in ministry with their wives (1 Corinthians 9:5). The family that once came to take custody of Jesus became part of the work of spreading His name.

D. A Pillar in Jerusalem

Within a few years James was the recognized leader of the Jerusalem church. When Peter escaped prison he said, “Report these things to James and the brethren” (Acts 12:17). Paul counted James among the “pillars”:

“and recognizing the grace that had been given to me, James and Cephas and John, who were reputed to be pillars, gave to me and Barnabas the right hand of fellowship, so that we might go to the Gentiles and they to the circumcised.”

Galatians 2:9 (NASB95)

At the Jerusalem council it was James who summed up the matter and gave the judgment that Gentile believers should not be burdened (Acts 15:13, 19). Years later, when Paul returned to Jerusalem, he went to see “James, and all the elders” (Acts 21:18), and James asked Paul to join some men under a vow and pay their expenses (Acts 21:23–24). Men “from James” came to Antioch as well (Galatians 2:12), though Scripture does not say James himself sent them to teach law-keeping, and the council letter later disowned those who went out without instruction (Acts 15:24). The skeptic had become a shepherd.

E. Why His Conversion Matters

James’s story is not only personal; it is evidence. What turns a skeptical brother into a man who calls his own Brother “Lord” and later dies at the hands of His opponents? Brothers are not easily impressed. James had every reason to resist, and for years he did. The best explanation is the one Scripture gives: the risen Jesus appeared to him. His changed life is one more witness that the resurrection really happened.

It also shows us something about grace. James did not earn his place in the church by growing up in the right home. He came in exactly the way everyone comes in, by believing in Jesus. His leadership in Jerusalem came afterward, as the fruit of a life given over to the Lord who had saved him.

III. Did Mary Have Other Children?

Because James is called “the Lord’s brother,” the question naturally comes up. Two answers have been given.

A. The Case Some Make Against It

Some hold that Mary remained a virgin all her life. On that view the “brothers” were cousins, or sons of Joseph by an earlier marriage. Two arguments are offered: Scripture never directly says Mary had other children, and from the cross Jesus entrusted His mother to John rather than to a brother (John 19:26–27).

B. The Plain Reading

The more natural reading is that James was a younger half-brother of Jesus, a son of Joseph and Mary. Matthew says Joseph “kept her a virgin until she gave birth to a Son” (Matthew 1:25), which implies a normal marriage afterward. The neighbors in Nazareth name the brothers alongside the mother and add, “Are not His sisters here with us?” (Mark 6:3). Paul speaks of “the brothers of the Lord” as a known group (1 Corinthians 9:5). As for the cross, the brothers did not yet believe (John 7:5), so it made sense for Jesus to place Mary in the care of a disciple who did. None of this touches the virgin birth itself, which Scripture plainly teaches.

IV. When and to Whom

A. The Date

Some hold that James is the earliest book of the New Testament, written in the 40s or even the late 30s. They point out that the letter says nothing about the Gentile mission or the debate settled at the Jerusalem council, and that its setting feels very early. This is a reasonable opinion, but there is no real evidence that fixes the date. What we can say is that the letter was written before James’s death, about AD 62.

B. The First Readers

James writes “to the twelve tribes who are dispersed abroad.” Israel still thought of itself as twelve tribes in the first century; Paul could speak to King Agrippa of “our twelve tribes” (Acts 26:7). These readers were Jewish believers living outside the land. Acts tells how it happened:

“Saul was in hearty agreement with putting him to death. And on that day a great persecution began against the church in Jerusalem, and they were all scattered throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria, except the apostles.”

Acts 8:1 (NASB95)

“So then those who were scattered because of the persecution that occurred in connection with Stephen made their way to Phoenicia and Cyprus and Antioch, speaking the word to no one except to Jews alone.”

Acts 11:19 (NASB95)

Some hold that, when James wrote, the church was still entirely Jewish and that the letter reached Gentile churches only later. That is possible, but there is no real evidence for the timing.

Those readers faced real pressure: trials and temptations (chapter 1), favoritism toward the rich (2:1–7), quarrels among themselves (4:1), oppression by wealthy landowners (5:4–6), and the need for patient endurance (5:7–8). James writes to help them meet trials with faith and endurance and to restore peace in their fellowship (3:17–18).

C. Not a Reply to Paul

Whatever the exact date, James is not arguing with Paul. Paul answers the question of how a sinner receives eternal life: “to the one who does not work, but believes in Him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is credited as righteousness” (Romans 4:5). James answers a different question, asked of “my brethren,” people who already believe: what use is faith that is not put to work (2:14)? Paul speaks of the gift; James speaks of a useful, fruitful Christian life. When the two met in Jerusalem, they gave each other “the right hand of fellowship” (Galatians 2:9). They were on the same side.

V. The Kind of Book James Is

A. Practical, Not Less Doctrinal

James is often called a practical book, and it is. Martin Luther, in his 1522 preface, called it “an epistle of straw” compared with the letters he valued most, chiefly because he found little of Christ’s saving work in it. Some still dislike it for the same reason. But practical is not the opposite of doctrinal. James assumes the gospel; he writes to people who have already been “brought forth by the word of truth” (1:18). What he gives them is doctrine put to work: the character of God (1:5, 13, 17), the power of the Word (1:18, 21), the royal law of love (2:8), the coming of the Lord (5:7–8), and the reality of judgment and reward for believers (1:12; 2:12–13; 5:9).

B. Echoes of the Sermon on the Mount

No New Testament letter sounds more like the teaching of Jesus. James never quotes Him by name, but again and again he echoes the Sermon on the Mount. It is as if the brother who once rejected the message now cannot stop repeating it.

James also sounds like the James of Acts. His short speech and the letter he helped send after the Jerusalem council share several features with this epistle:

D. The Proverbs of the New Testament

James has often been called the Proverbs of the New Testament. Like Proverbs, it moves quickly from subject to subject, speaks in short, memorable sayings, and cares about wisdom for daily life. Wisdom is a theme of its own: James tells believers to ask God for it (1:5) and contrasts the wisdom “from above” with the wisdom that is “earthly, natural, demonic” (3:15–17). Like Solomon, James is concerned less with what we know than with how we live what we know.

VI. A Greeting Worth Slowing Down For (1:1)

A. The Writer: “James, a Bond-servant”

The word is doulos, a slave who belongs wholly to his master. Paul uses it of himself (Romans 1:1), and so does Jude, who calls himself “a bond-servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James” (Jude 1). The two brothers of the Lord both chose the same humble title. They learned it from Him, for Christ Himself “emptied Himself, taking the form of a bond-servant” (Philippians 2:7).

James could have written, “James, the brother of the Lord,” or “James, leader of the church in Jerusalem.” Either would have been true and would have carried weight. He chose neither. The only credential he claims is that he belongs to Someone else.

B. The Master: “Of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ”

James places God and the Lord Jesus Christ side by side as the One he serves. This is the man who once thought his Brother had lost His senses. Now he gives Him the full title: Lord, Jesus, Christ. Lord is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for God’s own name. Christ is the Messiah, Israel’s promised King. No one who grew up with Jesus would say that lightly.

C. The Readers: “The Twelve Tribes Who Are Dispersed Abroad”

The word translated “dispersed abroad” is diaspora, a scattering, as seed is scattered. It was the regular word for Jews living outside the land of Israel. These believers had lost much: homes, livelihoods, the comfort of Jerusalem. But seed is scattered in order to grow, and wherever these believers went, they carried the word (Acts 8:4; 11:19).

James clearly writes to believers. He calls them “brethren” fifteen times. As Bob Wilkin observes about the end of the letter, “Since James always uses the term brethren to refer to believers, the wanderer is a fellow Christian” (Robert N. Wilkin, “‘Soul Salvation,’ Part 2: Saving the Soul of a Fellow Christian [James 5:19–20],” Grace in Focus, January 1, 1992, https://faithalone.org/grace-in-focus-articles/soul-salvation-part-2-saving-the-soul-of-a-fellow-christian). The same holds from the first chapter to the last.

D. The Greeting: “Rejoice”

The greeting is a single Greek word, chairein, literally “rejoice” or “be glad.” It was the standard opening of a letter, and the same word opens the Jerusalem council letter:

“and they sent this letter by them, ‘The apostles and the brethren who are elders, to the brethren in Antioch and Syria and Cilicia who are from the Gentiles, greetings.”

Acts 15:23 (NASB95)

Paul would later turn the common greeting into “grace” (charis), a word from the same family. James keeps the older form, but he lets it do double duty. In the very next sentence he writes, “Consider it all joy” (1:2), using a related word, chara. He greets his scattered, hard-pressed readers with “rejoice,” and then shows them how joy is possible even in trials.

VII. Reading James Through the Lens of Grace

A. Written to the Saved

Everything in James is addressed to people who already have eternal life. James says so himself: God “brought us forth by the word of truth” (1:18). Eternal life is a free gift, received the moment a person believes in Jesus Christ for it, and it can never be lost. James does not question that. He asks a different question: now that you have life, what kind of life will you live, and what will it be worth when the Lord returns?

Read that way, James becomes a book of encouragement and direction, not a source of doubt. Its warnings are real, but they are warnings to God’s children about their walk, their fellowship, and their reward, not tests of whether they really belong to Him (cf. Zane C. Hodges, The Epistle of James: Proven Character Through Testing [Irving, TX: Grace Evangelical Society, 1994]; Robert N. Wilkin, ed., The Grace New Testament Commentary [Denton, TX: Grace Evangelical Society, 2010]).

B. Four Keys for the Rest of the Letter

Four keys will help us all the way through the series:

  • “Save your souls” means “save your lives.” The word psychē often means a person’s life. When James says the implanted Word “is able to save your souls” (1:21), he means it can rescue a believer’s life from sin’s destructive effects (Study 05).
  • “Dead faith” is useless faith, not false faith. James asks, “What use is it?” (2:14, 16). Faith that is not put to work does not help the hungry brother, and it does not help the believer either (Studies 09–10).
  • Justified “by works” is justification before men. God declared Abraham righteous by faith (Genesis 15:6). Years later his obedience showed that faith before others and earned him the title “friend of God” (2:21–23; Study 10).
  • Believers face judgment for reward, not for eternal destiny. James speaks of the crown of life (1:12), of being judged by the law of liberty (2:12), of a stricter judgment for teachers (3:1), and of the Judge at the door (5:9). These point to the Judgment Seat of Christ, where believers’ lives are evaluated for reward (2 Corinthians 5:10).

VIII. The Shape of the Letter

James is sometimes said to have no plan, but one key verse unlocks the structure:

“This you know, my beloved brethren. But everyone must be quick to hear, slow to speak and slow to anger;”

James 1:19 (NASB95)

The prologue (1:2–18) shows how to meet trials. The key verse (1:19–20) gives three marks of a believer who meets them well. The body of the letter then takes up those three marks one at a time, and the epilogue calls for endurance to the end.

Keep this map in hand. Each study in this series will show where its passage fits. And keep the greeting in mind, too. The whole letter comes from a servant who learned, by grace, to call his Brother “Lord,” and it is written to help us live as His servants in a hard world.

Lord, thank You.

1. Why do you think James calls himself a “bond-servant” rather than “the Lord’s brother”?

Show Leader Answer

Family connection gave him no standing before God; belonging to Christ did. He also follows the pattern of Christ, who took the form of a bond-servant (Philippians 2:7). Humility, not pedigree, is his credential.

2. James lived with Jesus for years and still did not believe. What does that teach us about how people come to faith?

Show Leader Answer

Nearness to the truth is not the same as believing it. Faith comes when a person believes God’s testimony about His Son. For James that came when he met the risen Lord. It encourages us to keep praying for family members who do not yet believe.

3. How does knowing the readers were scattered, pressured believers shape the way we read the rest of the letter?

Show Leader Answer

The letter is pastoral counsel to people who already have eternal life, about how to live through hardship. Its warnings concern their walk and reward, not whether they are saved.

4. Paul says we are justified by faith apart from works; James asks what use faith is without works. Why are they not in conflict?

Show Leader Answer

They answer different questions. Paul: how a sinner receives eternal life (Romans 4:5). James: how a believer’s faith becomes useful in daily life (James 2:14). We will develop this in Studies 09–10.

5. James greets his readers with “rejoice” and then says “consider it all joy” in trials. Is that realistic?

Show Leader Answer

Yes, because the joy rests on what God is doing through the trial, not on how the trial feels. Study 02 opens this up from 1:2–4.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Lead with Belonging, Not Pedigree

James 1:1; Philippians 2:7

Source: Commentary ③ — V.A

James had the most impressive family connection imaginable and set it aside. Our identity rests on belonging to Christ, not on the church we grew up in, the family we came from, or the record we have built.

Reflection: Where do I lean on my background to feel spiritually secure or respected?

Practical Step: This week, when you introduce yourself in a Christian setting, let what you say point to Christ rather than to your credentials.

Keep Praying for the Doubter at Home

John 7:5; 1 Corinthians 15:7

Source: Commentary ③ — II.B–C

James did not believe for years, and then the Lord met him. The person closest to Jesus’ life was not the quickest to believe. No one is beyond the reach of the risen Lord.

Reflection: Who in my family has watched my faith but not yet believed?

Practical Step: Write down one name and pray for that person by name every day this week.

Read James as a Letter to Believers

James 1:1; 2:14

Source: Commentary ③ — IV.B–C; cf. Hodges, The Epistle of James

James writes to “brethren,” people who already have eternal life as a free gift. His exhortations call us to a useful, rewarding life, not to wonder whether we are truly saved. Reading him this way turns the letter from a source of doubt into a source of direction.

Reflection: Have I read James before in a way that made me doubt my salvation?

Practical Step: Before Study 02, read James 1 straight through and underline each place James speaks to his readers as “brethren.”

Greet Hardship with Joy

James 1:1–2

Source: Commentary ③ — V.C

James opens with “rejoice” to people who had lost homes and security. Joy for the believer is not a denial of hard things; it rests on God’s purpose in them.

Reflection: What current pressure am I meeting with complaint rather than trust?

Practical Step: Name one trial you are facing, and thank God for one thing He may be doing through it.
