What Is the Gospel, Exactly?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Christ died for our sins, was buried, and rose again. Saving faith is believing Jesus’ promise of life — whoever believes in Him has eternal life — with His death, burial, and resurrection the ultimate picture of sacrifice for all mankind.

Key verses1 Corinthians 15:3–4 · John 3:16 · John 6:47

The word “gospel” simply means good news, and Paul gives its content in the most compact form found anywhere in Scripture: “Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, and ... He was buried, and ... He rose again the third day according to the Scriptures” (1 Corinthians 15:3–4). This is the historical core the entire New Testament assumes and builds on — a real death, for a real reason (sin), and a real, bodily resurrection, both anchored in Old Testament prophecy rather than invented after the fact.

It is worth being precise about what response the gospel calls for, since confusion here creates enormous unnecessary anxiety. The gospel is good news to be believed, not a program to be completed. “Whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life” (John 3:16). “To as many as received Him, to them He gave the right to become children of God, to those who believe in His name” (John 1:12). The verbs are believe and receive — not achieve, complete, or maintain. Zane Hodges spent his ministry insisting that adding any further condition — turning from sin, committing one’s life, persevering to the end — to the single condition of faith is to add to the gospel something the New Testament itself never adds.1

It also matters what, specifically, is being believed. Saving faith is not vague religious sincerity; it is believing Jesus’ promise of life — that whoever believes in Him has, right now, permanent eternal life (John 5:24; 6:47; 11:25–26) — with His death, burial, and resurrection standing as the ultimate picture of sacrifice for all mankind (1 Corinthians 15:3–4; 1 John 2:2). The Samaritan woman, the dying thief, and the Ethiopian eunuch were not theologically sophisticated, yet each responded rightly to what they had been shown about Christ and each was saved instantly, without probation or delay (John 4:10–14, 39–42; Luke 23:42–43; Acts 8:35–38).

The gospel is also unmistakably a gift rather than a wage: “the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 6:23). A gift, by definition, is received rather than earned, which is why Paul can say salvation is “not of works, lest anyone should boast” (Ephesians 2:9) in the very next breath after calling it “the gift of God” (2:8). Good works follow as the fruit of this gift in a believer’s ongoing life of discipleship, but they are never the means by which the gift itself is obtained.

Stripped to its essentials, the gospel is this: God became a man, lived a sinless life, died a substitutionary death for sin, rose bodily from the grave, and now offers everlasting life, free of charge, to anyone who simply believes in Him for it. Nothing more is required to receive it, and nothing less actually delivers it.
