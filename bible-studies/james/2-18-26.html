The Objector, the Patriarch, and the Harlot

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

Source: The Gospel Vault · Developed from teaching outlines by Dr. C. Norman Sellers, ThD, used by permission

① The Biblical Text

James 2:18–26 · New American Standard Bible (NASB95)

② Devotional

“And Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness,” and he was called the friend of God. — James 2:23

Abraham believed God under a sky full of stars, and in that moment God declared him righteous. Nothing Abraham did later added to that. But many years later, on Mount Moriah, Abraham’s faith went to work. He trusted God enough to offer the son of promise, and his faith came to full maturity. God called him something more than His child: He called him His friend.

Rahab’s story is the same in miniature. She believed what she had heard about Israel’s God, and when the spies came, she put her faith to work, at great risk. Her life and her family were spared.

Every believer is a child of God by faith alone. But faith that goes to work grows strong, is seen by others, and enjoys a friendship with God that idle faith never knows.

Reflection Questions

  1. When has God asked me to trust Him with something precious?
  2. What would it look like for my faith to be “perfected” by obedience this week?
  3. Do I want to be not only God’s child, but His friend?

Lord, I believe You. Now let my faith work, and let me walk with You as Your friend.

Introduction: The Thesis Under Fire

In the last study James stated his thesis: “faith, if it has no works, is dead” (2:17). Faith by itself cannot rescue a needy brother, and it cannot rescue a believer’s life from the destructive consequences of sin. James asked two questions, “What use is it?” and “Can that faith save him?” and the answer to both was: none, and no.

Now James anticipates an objection. Someone will argue that faith and works have nothing to do with each other. Adding works to faith makes no difference. James answers that objector, first briefly (2:20) and then with two examples from Scripture: Abraham, the father of the Jewish nation, and Rahab, a Gentile prostitute (2:21–25). He closes with a final picture: a body without the spirit (2:26).

The objection is not only an ancient one. A modern version says, “All you need is faith. How you live doesn’t matter.” Some even teach that faith alone guarantees health and prosperity, whatever one’s lifestyle. James would answer them the same way.

I. The Objection Raised (2:18–19)

“But someone may well say, ‘You have faith and I have works; show me your faith without the works, and I will show you my faith by my works.’”

James 2:18 (NASB95)

“You believe that God is one. You do well; the demons also believe, and shudder.”

James 2:19 (NASB95)

A. A Teaching Device

“But someone may well say.” James uses a common teaching method of his day: raise an objection, then answer it. It was used by Greek philosophers and Jewish teachers alike, and Paul uses it often:

“You will say to me then, ‘Why does He still find fault? For who resists His will?’”

Romans 9:19 (NASB95)

“But someone will say, ‘How are the dead raised? And with what kind of body do they come?’”

1 Corinthians 15:35 (NASB95)

Notice that in both cases Paul’s answer begins by showing how foolish the objection is: “who are you, O man?”; “You fool!” This is sometimes called reductio ad absurdum, reducing an argument to absurdity. James will do the same: “you foolish fellow” (2:20).

Good teachers anticipate questions. By raising the objection himself, James shows that he has thought the matter through, keeps his readers from stopping in mid-argument with a “Yes, but…,” and prepares them to answer the same objection when they hear it.

B. Where the Objection Begins and Ends

One of the challenges of this passage is knowing where the objector’s words stop and James’s begin. The original Greek manuscripts had no quotation marks; punctuation was added much later. Translations differ. Some end the quotation after “You have faith and I have works.” The NASB includes all of verse 18.

The view taken here, following Zane Hodges, is that the objection runs through verses 18 and 19, and James’s reply begins at verse 20 with “But are you willing to recognize, you foolish fellow…” Verses 18–19 do not answer the objection; they make it. And verse 20 clearly turns on the objector (cf. Zane C. Hodges, The Epistle of James: Proven Character Through Testing [Irving, TX: Grace Evangelical Society, 1994]).

C. The Objector’s Claim (2:18)

The objector’s argument is full of irony. “For the sake of argument,” he says, “let’s say you have faith and I have works. Now, you show me your faith without the works, and I will show you my faith by my works.” His point is that neither can be done:

  • You cannot start with faith and show it by works, because works do not prove what faith is inside.
  • I cannot start with works and show you my faith, because faith is invisible.

So, he concludes, there is no real connection between faith and works. They are completely separate things.

D. The Objector’s Proof: The Demons (2:19)

He then offers what he thinks is proof. “You believe that God is one.” This was the central creed of Israel, the Shema, recited daily: “Hear, O Israel! The Lord is our God, the Lord is one!” (Deuteronomy 6:4). “You do well.” Men believe that truth, and it leads them to do good. “The demons also believe, and shudder.” Demons believe exactly the same truth, but it leads them only to tremble in fear, not to do good. The word phrissō, “shudder,” appears only here in the New Testament; it describes bristling, the hair standing on end with terror.

The objector’s conclusion: the same belief produces completely different conduct in different beings. Faith and works have no necessary connection. You cannot read one from the other.

E. What the Demons Do Not Prove

This verse is often used to argue that demons have “faith” but not “saving faith,” so a person who believes without works is no better off than a demon, and is not truly saved. But that reading has serious problems:

  • There is no salvation of any kind offered to demons. The comparison does not fit the question of eternal life at all.
  • What the demons believe is not the gospel. Believing that God is one does not give anyone eternal life. Many people believe that, Jews and Muslims among them. Eternal life comes through believing in Jesus Christ for it (John 3:16; 6:47).
  • The emphasis is on knowledge. Demons simply know the facts. There are no atheist demons. They knew who Jesus was: “I know who You are, the Holy One of God!” (Mark 1:24). They knew Paul too (Acts 19:15). But knowing facts about God is not the same as trusting Christ for eternal life.
  • James is not distinguishing two kinds of faith here. These are the words of the objector, not James. And the subject of the passage is the usefulness of faith, not eternal salvation.

In short, James 2:19 does not teach that genuine faith will always produce visible works. It is the objector’s argument that faith and works are unrelated, and James is about to refute it.

II. James’s First Reply (2:20)

“But are you willing to recognize, you foolish fellow, that faith without works is useless?”

James 2:20 (NASB95)

“You foolish fellow.” The word kenos means empty, empty-headed. James calls the objector what Paul called his: a fool. And he questions whether the man even wants to learn: “Are you willing to recognize…?” Do you really want to know, or will you stay empty-headed?

Notice the NASB’s word: faith without works is “useless.” Many Greek manuscripts here read argē, which means idle, inactive, not working. It is a play on words: faith without works (ergōn) is workless (a-ergos). That is exactly James’s point. Faith that does not work does not work.

Then James offers proof. He turns to two people every Jewish believer would know: one at the top of Israel’s history, and one at the bottom of society.

III. Proof One: Abraham (2:21–24)

“Was not Abraham our father justified by works when he offered up Isaac his son on the altar?”

James 2:21 (NASB95)

A. Abraham’s Background

Abraham is “our father,” the father of the Jewish nation, and the man whom Jews, Christians and Muslims all claim. But he began as a pagan in Ur of the Chaldees, in what is now Iraq. God called him, and he left Haran for Canaan when he was seventy-five years old (Genesis 12:4).

B. Justified by Faith Before God (Genesis 15)

Some time after he entered the land, God took Abraham outside and showed him the night sky:

“And He took him outside and said, ‘Now look toward the heavens, and count the stars, if you are able to count them.’ And He said to him, ‘So shall your descendants be.’”

Genesis 15:5 (NASB95)

“Then he believed in the Lord; and He reckoned it to him as righteousness.”

Genesis 15:6 (NASB95)

That was the moment Abraham was justified before God, declared righteous, by faith alone. He had not yet been circumcised, and Isaac had not yet been born. Paul builds his whole argument on this verse:

“For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, but not before God.”

Romans 4:2 (NASB95)

“For what does the Scripture say? ‘Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness.’”

Romans 4:3 (NASB95)

C. Justified by Works Before Men (Genesis 22)

Now notice the timing. Ishmael was born when Abraham was eighty-six (Genesis 16:16). Isaac was born when Abraham was one hundred (Genesis 21:5). And the offering of Isaac on Mount Moriah came when Isaac was old enough to carry the wood for the sacrifice up the mountain, a young man. Scripture does not give his exact age; some estimates, including Josephus, place him around twenty-five. Either way, the events of Genesis 22 came decades after Abraham was declared righteous in Genesis 15.

So when James says Abraham was “justified by works when he offered up Isaac,” he cannot mean that this was when Abraham first became righteous before God. That had happened many years earlier, by faith. James is speaking of another kind of justification: Abraham’s righteousness being shown, demonstrated and vindicated before others.

The word dikaioō can carry exactly that meaning. Jesus said, “wisdom is vindicated [justified] by all her children” (Luke 7:35). Wisdom is not made wise by her children; she is shown to be wise. In the same way, Abraham was not made righteous by offering Isaac; he was shown to be righteous. Paul himself hints at this: if Abraham was justified by works, “he has something to boast about, but not before God” (Romans 4:2). There is a justification by works, but it is not before God.

D. Faith Working and Perfected (2:22)

“You see that faith was working with his works, and as a result of the works, faith was perfected;”

James 2:22 (NASB95)

Two verbs are important. Faith was “working with” (synergeō) his works; it cooperated with them. And “as a result of the works, faith was perfected” (teleioō), brought to maturity, completed. This is the same family of words as the “perfect” believer of 1:4.

There is a two-way relationship here. James is not only saying that faith produces works, but also that works strengthen faith. Abraham’s faith grew as he obeyed. Paul says the same thing about Abraham: “he did not waver in unbelief but grew strong in faith, giving glory to God, and being fully assured that what God had promised, He was able also to perform” (Romans 4:20–21). Hebrews tells us what Abraham believed on Moriah:

“He considered that God is able to raise people even from the dead, from which he also received him back as a type.”

Hebrews 11:19 (NASB95)

E. Scripture Fulfilled (2:23a)

“The Scripture was fulfilled which says, ‘And Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness.’” James is not saying that Genesis 22 is the same event as Genesis 15. Genesis 15:6 is not a prophecy. But it held potential that was “filled full” when Abraham obeyed. The faith that justified him before God in Genesis 15 came to full expression in Genesis 22. In a sense, the potential for that kind of obedience is present whenever someone believes.

F. The Friend of God (2:23b)

“And he was called the friend of God.” This title is given to Abraham in the Old Testament (2 Chronicles 20:7; Isaiah 41:8). Notice the progression:

  • In Genesis 15, by faith, Abraham was declared righteous, the standing of every child of God.
  • In Genesis 22, by works, Abraham was called the friend of God, a special relationship of intimacy.

Every believer is a child of God by faith alone. But not every believer is God’s friend in this sense. Jesus said, “You are My friends if you do what I command you” (John 15:14). Friendship with God is the reward of obedience, not the condition of eternal life.

G. Two Justifications (2:24)

“You see that a man is justified by works and not by faith alone.”

James 2:24 (NASB95)

Notice that James now says “you see,” plural. He is speaking to his readers, not to the objector. And notice the word “alone” (monon). In Greek it is an adverb, not an adjective. It does not describe faith (“faith that is alone”); it describes the verb (“justified … not only by faith”). The sense is: “a man is justified by works [before men], and not only justified by faith [before God].” There are two kinds of justification:

Justification by faithJustification by works
Before whom?Before GodBefore people
When?The moment one believesAs faith is put to work over time
ExampleGenesis 15:6Genesis 22
ResultDeclared righteous; a child of GodShown righteous; a friend of God
Can it be lost?NeverA believer may fail to display it

But there is no such thing as justification before God by faith plus works. That would contradict Paul (Romans 3:28; 4:5). What there is, is a sanctification, a maturing, that involves both faith and works.

Here is a sobering question: if Abraham had not offered Isaac, would he still have been justified before God? Yes; that was settled in Genesis 15. Is it possible for believers to fail in obedience the way Abraham did not? Yes; Scripture gives many examples. That is why James writes. He wants believers to be not only children of God, but His friends, and their faith to be not only real, but useful.

IV. Proof Two: Rahab (2:25)

“In the same way, was not Rahab the harlot also justified by works when she received the messengers and sent them out by another way?”

James 2:25 (NASB95)

James’s second example could hardly be more different from the first. Abraham was a patriarch; Rahab was a pagan, a Gentile and a prostitute. If the principle holds for both, it holds for everyone.

A. Her Background

Rahab lived in Jericho, the first city Israel faced in Canaan. She was a harlot. Yet she appears in Hebrews’ “hall of faith” (Hebrews 11:31), and in the genealogy of the Lord Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:5). Grace reaches the most unlikely people.

B. Her Faith

Before the spies ever arrived, Rahab had heard what the Lord had done, and she believed:

“When we heard it, our hearts melted and no courage remained in any man any longer because of you; for the Lord your God, He is God in heaven above and on earth beneath.”

Joshua 2:11 (NASB95)

That was her faith: “the Lord your God, He is God in heaven above and on earth beneath.” Hebrews confirms it: “By faith Rahab the harlot did not perish” (Hebrews 11:31).

C. Her Works

Then her faith went to work. She “received the messengers and sent them out by another way.” She hid the spies on her roof, lied to the king’s men to protect them, and helped them escape. It was dangerous; if discovered, she would have been executed as a traitor.

D. Her Deliverance

Her faith at work saved her life, and her family’s:

“However, Rahab the harlot and her father’s household and all she had, Joshua spared; and she has lived in the midst of Israel to this day, for she hid the messengers whom Joshua sent to spy out Jericho.”

Joshua 6:25 (NASB95)

Notice the kind of salvation: physical deliverance from death when Jericho fell. That is exactly the kind of “saving” James has been talking about (1:21; 2:14; 5:20). Her faith, put to work, rescued her life.

V. The Final Picture: A Body Without the Spirit (2:26)

“For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so also faith without works is dead.”

James 2:26 (NASB95)

James ends with a picture everyone understands. A body without the spirit is dead. It is still a real body; you can see it and touch it. But it cannot do anything. It does not walk, work or help anyone.

In the same way, faith without works is dead. James does not say it does not exist. He does not say the person who has it was never saved. He says it is dead, inactive, useless. The objector said faith and works are unrelated. James says they are related the way spirit and body are related: works are what make faith alive and active in the world.

VI. The Same Pattern in Hebrews 11

Abraham and Rahab both appear again in the great chapter on faith, Hebrews 11, and so do many others. Read the chapter with James 2 in mind and a pattern stands out. Again and again, “by faith” is followed by an action:

“By faith Noah, being warned by God about things not yet seen, in reverence prepared an ark for the salvation of his household, by which he condemned the world, and became an heir of the righteousness which is according to faith.”

Hebrews 11:7 (NASB95)

“By faith Abraham, when he was called, obeyed by going out to a place which he was to receive for an inheritance; and he went out, not knowing where he was going.”

Hebrews 11:8 (NASB95)

“By faith Moses, when he had grown up, refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter,”

Hebrews 11:24 (NASB95)

“choosing rather to endure ill-treatment with the people of God than to enjoy the passing pleasures of sin,”

Hebrews 11:25 (NASB95)

“considering the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures of Egypt; for he was looking to the reward.”

Hebrews 11:26 (NASB95)

Noah believed God’s warning, and then he built. Abraham believed God’s call, and then he went. Moses believed God’s promise, and then he chose. In each case, faith came first and works followed. The works did not make these people God’s children; they showed what their faith looked like when it went to work. And notice Moses: “he was looking to the reward.” Faith at work keeps an eye on the reward Christ will give.

Hebrews 11 does not say these people were perfect. Noah got drunk; Abraham lied about his wife, twice; Moses struck the rock in anger. Faith that works is not sinless faith. But at the decisive moments, their faith went to work, and God has recorded it for us. James is calling us to the same kind of faith: real faith, put to real use, in ordinary obedience and in the hard moments when God asks us to trust Him with something precious.

Conclusion: Child by Faith, Friend by Obedience

The objector said faith and works have nothing to do with each other. James answered with two lives. Abraham believed God and was declared righteous, freely, by faith alone. Decades later, his faith went to work on Mount Moriah, grew to maturity, and earned him the title “friend of God.” Rahab believed God and, when the moment came, put her faith to work and saved her family.

Every believer has the first part already: eternal life, received by faith alone, never to be lost (Galatians 3:7; John 6:47). James calls us to the second part: faith that works, grows, is seen by others, and enjoys friendship with God. The choice is not between heaven and hell; it is between a dead, useless faith and a living, useful one.

Lord, thank You.

1. Why is it important to see 2:18–19 as the objector’s words rather than James’s?

Show Leader Answer

Otherwise James 2:19 seems to teach that faith without works is “demon faith.” In context, the objector argues faith and works are unrelated, and James refutes him.

2. How does the timing between Genesis 15 and Genesis 22 help us understand James 2:21?

Show Leader Answer

Abraham was declared righteous by faith decades before Moriah, so James cannot mean he became righteous there. He means Abraham’s righteousness was shown before others.

3. What is the difference between being a child of God and being a friend of God?

Show Leader Answer

Every believer is a child by faith alone. Friendship is an intimate relationship enjoyed by those who obey (John 15:14), as Abraham did.

4. How do faith and works strengthen each other (2:22; Romans 4:20–21)?

Show Leader Answer

Faith leads to obedience, and obedience in turn matures and strengthens faith. Abraham grew strong in faith as he acted on God’s promise.

5. Rahab’s “salvation” was physical deliverance from Jericho’s destruction. How does that fit James’s use of “save” in the rest of the letter?

Show Leader Answer

James consistently speaks of rescuing life from death (1:21; 5:15, 20). Rahab’s faith at work saved her life and her family’s.

Many people believe faith and conduct have nothing to do with each other. Believers can explain gently why faith is meant to work.

Reflection: Have I ever used “I’m saved by grace” as an excuse for careless living?

Practical Step: Write a two-sentence answer to someone who says, “It doesn’t matter how I live as long as I believe.”

Trust God on Your Moriah

James 2:21–22; Hebrews 11:17–19

Source: Commentary ③ — III.C–D

Abraham’s faith matured when he trusted God with what he loved most. Our faith grows the same way.

Reflection: What is God asking me to trust Him with right now?

Practical Step: Name it in prayer, and take one concrete step of obedience this week.

Pursue Friendship with God

James 2:23; John 15:14–15

Source: Commentary ③ — III.F

Every believer is God’s child. Those who obey Him enjoy an intimacy Jesus calls friendship.

Reflection: Is my relationship with God more like a distant child or a close friend?

Practical Step: Spend 15 minutes this week simply talking with God about what He has commanded, and do one thing He asks.

Risk Something for God

James 2:25; Joshua 2:11

Source: Commentary ③ — IV

Rahab put her faith to work at great personal risk. Useful faith often costs something.

Reflection: Where is fear keeping my faith idle?

Practical Step: Do one thing this week that requires you to trust God more than your comfort.
