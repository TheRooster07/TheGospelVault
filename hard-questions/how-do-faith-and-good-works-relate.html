50 Hard Questions · Question 18 of 50

How Do Faith and Good Works Relate?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Eternal life is received by faith alone, apart from works. Good works are the calling of every believer and are rewarded at the Judgment Seat of Christ, but they are never the condition for salvation.

Key versesEphesians 2:8–10 · Romans 4:5 · James 2:14–17

Confusion here is almost always caused by collapsing two distinct categories: the gift of eternal life, received once by faith alone, and the ongoing life of discipleship, in which works matter enormously but for entirely different reasons. Paul states the first category plainly: “by grace you have been saved through faith ... not of works, lest anyone should boast” (Ephesians 2:8–9). Lewis Sperry Chafer pressed the same point a century ago: grace, by its very nature, excludes every form of human merit.1

The very next verse refuses to let this become an excuse for indifference to holiness: “we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works” (Ephesians 2:10). Works are not the means by which a person becomes God’s workmanship; they are the purpose for which someone who already is God’s workmanship was created anew — salvation, then good works, never the reverse.

James 2:14–26 is the passage most often raised as a challenge. Read carefully, James is addressing believers already within the church (“my brethren,” 2:14), and his question — “Can faith save him?” — concerns deliverance from the deadly consequences of sin in a believer’s life, the same “saving” James speaks of in 1:21 and 5:19–20, not entrance into eternal life. The needy brother of 2:15–16 illustrates the point: kind words without deeds help no one. John Hart’s study argues that James’s “faith without works” describes a faith that is useless and unproductive — dead in the sense of inert — not a faith that never existed.2 Abraham’s justification “by works” when he offered Isaac (2:21) is a demonstration, decades after Genesis 15:6, of a faith already reckoned as righteousness — a vindication before men, not a second justification before God. Verses 18–19 are best read as the words of an objector rather than James’s own conclusion, and the demons who “believe — and tremble” (2:19) believe only that God is one; they have not believed Christ’s promise of eternal life.3

Paul and James answer two different questions: Paul answers how a person is declared righteous before God — by faith alone; James answers what a living faith should look like in practice — it should produce works, or it is functionally useless. This distinction guards against both legalism, which quietly reintroduces works as a condition for salvation, and antinomianism, which treats grace as license for indifference to holiness (Titus 2:11–12).

The healthy answer holds both truths together: a person receives eternal life the instant they believe, entirely apart from works (Romans 4:5), and that same person is called into a lifelong discipleship in which good works are the expected fruit of gratitude, evaluated for reward at the Judgment Seat of Christ, not for entrance into heaven (2 Corinthians 5:10). Works are neither the root of salvation nor an optional accessory — they are its intended fruit, and Scripture takes seriously both that fruit sometimes fails to appear and that its absence does not undo a gift never conditioned on it.
