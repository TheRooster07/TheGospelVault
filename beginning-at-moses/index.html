HOW THIS STUDY FITS

This is the practical companion to Opening the Scriptures, which studies how the apostles proved that Jesus is the Christ. Here we walk through where it is written: the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms, as Jesus did on the Emmaus road (Luke 24:27, 44–47), ending with a seven-step outline for sharing the gospel from the Old Testament.

For the historical question of whether these claims matched what first-century Jews expected of the Messiah, see The Apostolic Case for Jesus as Messiah.

② Devotional

Drawn from the commentary · For personal reading

“Then beginning with Moses and with all the prophets, He explained to them the things concerning Himself in all the Scriptures.”
— Luke 24:27 (NASB95)

Two tired disciples walked home thinking their hope had died on a cross. A Stranger joined them and, instead of showing them His hands, opened their Bible. From Moses through the prophets He showed them that the Christ had to suffer and then enter His glory. Later they said their hearts burned while He spoke.

The same Old Testament is in our hands. In the first promise of a wounded Victor, in the lamb God provided, in the serpent lifted up so that whoever looked would live, in the Servant pierced for our transgressions, the Lord was speaking of His Son. And the response God asked for has never changed. Abraham believed, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness.

Read the Old Testament with Jesus as the key, and it will not stay closed. It will lead you to Him, and through you, it can lead others.

For Reflection

  1. Which Old Testament picture of Christ (the Passover lamb, the bronze serpent, the Suffering Servant) speaks most clearly to you, and why?
  2. How does Genesis 15:6 help you explain that eternal life is received by faith and not earned by works?
  3. Who could you walk through the “Emmaus Road” outline with this month?

“Were not our hearts burning within us while He was… explaining the Scriptures to us?”

I. The Key Jesus Gave (Luke 24:25–27, 44–47)

On the day He rose, Jesus taught two Bible studies. The first was on the road to Emmaus with two discouraged disciples. The second was that evening in Jerusalem with the Eleven and those gathered with them. Both had the same subject: Himself in the Old Testament. Luke records them so that we can learn to read the Old Testament the way Jesus taught His own disciples to read it.

The Emmaus disciples were not short on facts. They knew Jesus had been crucified, and they had heard the women’s report of the empty tomb (24:19–24). What they lacked was understanding. They did not see how a suffering Messiah fit the Scriptures, so the cross looked like the end of their hope. Jesus called them “slow of heart to believe in all that the prophets have spoken” (24:25). The cure was not a new revelation but the old one, opened and explained.1

A. Three Sections: the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms

In 24:27 Luke says Jesus began “with Moses and with all the prophets.” In 24:44 Jesus names the three parts of the Hebrew Bible as the Jewish people arranged it: “the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms.” The Psalms was the first and largest book of the third section, the Writings. In other words, Jesus claimed that the whole Old Testament, not just a few scattered verses, “must be fulfilled” in Him. We will follow His order in sections II–IV.

B. Three Points: Suffer, Rise, Proclaimed to All Nations

Jesus then summarized what all three sections say about Him (24:46–47):

  1. The Christ would suffer.
  2. He would rise from the dead on the third day.
  3. Repentance for forgiveness of sins would be proclaimed in His name to all the nations.

These three points give us a framework for the passages that follow. Some show the Christ’s suffering, some His resurrection, and some the worldwide offer of forgiveness. A few, like Psalm 22 and Isaiah 53, show all three at once.

C. One Response: Believe

Luke 24:47 speaks of “repentance for forgiveness of sins.” In the Gospel context this repentance is a change of mind about Jesus, the kind of change that the Emmaus disciples themselves needed, which leads a person to believe in Him. Peter, preaching to the Gentile Cornelius, states the same offer in terms of faith: “Of Him all the prophets bear witness that through His name everyone who believes in Him receives forgiveness of sins” (Acts 10:43). Notice the scope of his claim: all the prophets bear witness. Notice also the condition: everyone who believes.

This matters for how we read the Old Testament. It is not only a book of laws that were later replaced by grace. Running through it is the same gospel and the same response, faith in what God promised. That is why Paul could say the righteousness of God by faith was “witnessed by the Law and the Prophets” (Rom 3:21).

II. Moses: The Gospel in the Law

“Beginning with Moses.” The five books of Moses are where Jesus started, and so do we. Paul could even say that the Scripture “preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham” (Gal 3:8).

A. The First Promise (Genesis 3:15)

“And I will put enmity
Between you and the woman,
And between your seed and her seed;
He shall bruise you on the head,
And you shall bruise him on the heel.”

— Genesis 3:15 (NASB95)

Moments after sin entered the world, God promised a Deliverer. He would be the woman’s “seed,” a human descendant. He would be wounded (“bruise him on the heel”) and He would win (“bruise you on the head”). In the very first gospel promise we already see suffering and victory together. Many have noted that the promise speaks of the woman’s seed, which fits well with the virgin birth.2 The rest of the Old Testament narrows the line: through Seth, Shem, Abraham, Judah and David.

B. Blessing for All Families (Genesis 12:3)

“…And in you all the families of the earth will be blessed.”

— Genesis 12:3 (NASB95)

God’s plan was never only for one nation. Through Abraham and his Seed, blessing would reach “all the families of the earth.” Paul calls this verse the gospel preached ahead of time, “foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith” (Gal 3:8). Here is Jesus’ third point, “to all the nations,” planted in the first book of the Bible.

C. Abraham Believed (Genesis 15:6)

Then he believed in the Lord; and He reckoned it to him as righteousness.

— Genesis 15:6 (NASB95)

This may be the single most important verse in the Old Testament for understanding the gospel. Abraham was childless and old. God promised him a seed as countless as the stars. Abraham believed God’s promise, and God credited righteousness to his account. The Hebrew verb “believed” (ʾāman, Hiphil) means to consider something reliable, to rely on it.3 No ritual is mentioned. Circumcision came later (Gen 17; Rom 4:10). The Law came 430 years later (Gal 3:17). Abraham was declared righteous by faith alone.

Paul builds his whole argument in Romans 4 on this verse: “to the one who does not work, but believes in Him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is credited as righteousness” (Rom 4:5). Bob Wilkin lists Genesis 15:6 first among his “four verses” for faith alone, precisely because it settles the question before the Law was ever given.4 When we share the gospel from the Old Testament, this verse shows from Moses himself that God has always asked for faith, not works.

D. The Lamb God Provides (Genesis 22)

God told Abraham, “Take now your son, your only son, whom you love, Isaac… and offer him there as a burnt offering” (22:2). On the third day (22:4) they reached the mountain. When Isaac asked where the lamb was, Abraham answered, “God will provide for Himself the lamb for the burnt offering, my son” (22:8). At the last moment God stopped Abraham’s hand, and Abraham offered a ram “in the place of his son” (22:13). He named the place “The Lord Will Provide” (22:14).

Hebrews tells us that Abraham “considered that God is able to raise people even from the dead, from which he also received him back as a type” (Heb 11:19). An only son, a three-day journey, a substitute dying in his place, a son received back as from the dead: the picture of the gospel is hard to miss. And John the Baptist would one day point to Jesus and say, “Behold, the Lamb of God” (John 1:29).

E. The Passover Lamb (Exodus 12)

On the night of judgment in Egypt, each household took an unblemished male lamb (12:5), killed it, and put its blood on the doorposts. God said, “when I see the blood I will pass over you” (12:13). Safety did not depend on how good the family inside was, but on the blood outside. God also commanded, “nor are you to break any bone of it” (12:46). John saw this fulfilled when the soldiers did not break Jesus’ legs: “Not a bone of Him shall be broken” (John 19:36). Paul says plainly, “Christ our Passover also has been sacrificed” (1 Cor 5:7). For more on this picture, see the site’s study The Feasts of the Lord Fulfilled in Christ.

F. Atonement by Blood (Leviticus 16–17)

“For the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you on the altar to make atonement for your souls; for it is the blood by reason of the life that makes atonement.”

— Leviticus 17:11 (NASB95)

On the Day of Atonement the high priest laid both hands on the head of a live goat and confessed over it “all the iniquities of the sons of Israel,” and the goat carried them away into the wilderness (Lev 16:21–22). Two truths stand out: sin requires the death of a substitute, and sin can be carried away. Hebrews 9–10 explains that these sacrifices were pictures, repeated year after year, of the one sacrifice Christ offered once for all (Heb 10:10–14).

G. Look and Live (Numbers 21:8–9)

Then the Lord said to Moses, “Make a fiery serpent, and set it on a standard; and it shall come about, that everyone who is bitten, when he looks at it, he will live.” And Moses made a bronze serpent and set it on the standard; and it came about, that if a serpent bit any man, when he looked to the bronze serpent, he lived.

— Numbers 21:8–9 (NASB95)

Israel was dying from snake bites because of their sin. God did not tell them to make medicine, offer a sacrifice, or promise to do better. He told them to look. Anyone who looked lived. Jesus applied this to Himself in the most famous gospel conversation in the Bible:

“As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of Man be lifted up; so that whoever believes will in Him have eternal life.”

— John 3:14–15 (NASB95)

Looking was not a work. It was simply trusting God’s provision. In the same way, believing in Jesus is not a work we offer God; it is receiving what He offers. This is one of the clearest pictures of faith alone in all of Scripture, and Jesus Himself drew it from Moses.4

H. The Prophet and the Tree (Deuteronomy 18:15; 21:23)

Moses promised, “The Lord your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among you… you shall listen to him” (Deut 18:15). Peter applied this to Jesus (Acts 3:22–23). Moses also wrote that “he who is hanged is accursed of God” (Deut 21:23). This verse made the cross a stumbling block for many Jews: how could the Messiah be cursed? Paul answers that this was exactly the point: “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the Law, having become a curse for us” (Gal 3:13).

III. The Prophets

“And with all the prophets.” The prophets fill in the picture Moses began: who the Deliverer would be, how He would suffer, and how far His salvation would reach.

A. The Child Who Is Mighty God (Isaiah 7:14; 9:6; Micah 5:2)

Isaiah promised a sign: “a virgin will be with child and bear a son, and she will call His name Immanuel” (7:14), which means “God with us.” Two chapters later he names the Child: “Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Eternal Father, Prince of Peace” (9:6). Micah said He would be born in Bethlehem, yet His “goings forth are from long ago, from the days of eternity” (Mic 5:2). The Deliverer would be truly human and truly God. For the virgin birth and David’s line, see the site’s white paper The Curse of Jeconiah and the Virgin Birth.

B. The Suffering Servant (Isaiah 52:13–53:12)

No passage in the Old Testament is more important for showing the gospel. Philip began right here with the Ethiopian (Acts 8:35). In one passage Isaiah shows all of Jesus’ points in Luke 24.

Surely our griefs He Himself bore,
And our sorrows He carried;
Yet we ourselves esteemed Him stricken,
Smitten of God, and afflicted.
But He was pierced through for our transgressions,
He was crushed for our iniquities;
The chastening for our well-being fell upon Him,
And by His scourging we are healed.
All of us like sheep have gone astray,
Each of us has turned to his own way;
But the Lord has caused the iniquity of us all
To fall on Him.

— Isaiah 53:4–6 (NASB95)

What Isaiah saysVersesFulfilled in Jesus
He suffers: despised, a man of sorrows53:3Rejected by His own (John 1:11)
He suffers in our place: pierced for our transgressions; our iniquity laid on Him53:5–6Christ died for our sins (1 Cor 15:3)
He dies and is buried: “with a rich man in His death”53:8–9Buried in the tomb of Joseph of Arimathea, a rich man (Matt 27:57–60)
He lives again: “He will see His offspring, He will prolong His days”53:10Raised on the third day
He justifies others: “the Righteous One, My Servant, will justify the many”53:11Justified by faith (Rom 5:1)

The word “pierced through” (53:5) translates a form of ḥālal, “to bore, to wound.”5 Notice the logic of 53:10–11: the Servant renders Himself as a guilt offering and dies, yet afterward He sees His offspring and prolongs His days. A man who has died cannot prolong his days unless he rises. And the result of His suffering is that “by His knowledge” the Servant “will justify the many, as He will bear their iniquities” (53:11). Justification comes through knowing Him, that is, through believing in Him.

C. Light to the Nations, Free to All (Isaiah 49:6; 55:1)

God told His Servant that restoring Israel was “too small a thing”: “I will also make You a light of the nations so that My salvation may reach to the end of the earth” (49:6). Paul and Barnabas quoted this to explain why they turned to the Gentiles (Acts 13:47). And the invitation is free:

“Ho! Every one who thirsts, come to the waters;
And you who have no money come, buy and eat.
Come, buy wine and milk
Without money and without cost.”

— Isaiah 55:1 (NASB95)

“Without money and without cost.” The Bible ends with the same invitation: “let the one who wishes take the water of life without cost” (Rev 22:17). From Isaiah to Revelation, the gift is free.

D. A New Covenant (Jeremiah 31:31–34)

Jeremiah promised a new covenant with Israel and Judah, not like the one at Sinai. Its final promise is the heart of the gospel: “for I will forgive their iniquity, and their sin I will remember no more” (31:34). Jesus inaugurated this covenant in His blood (Luke 22:20), and Hebrews quotes the promise twice (Heb 8:12; 10:17). Its full national fulfillment for Israel awaits the Lord’s return (Rom 11:26–27), while believers today already enjoy its forgiveness through the Mediator of the covenant.

E. Messiah Cut Off (Daniel 9:26)

Daniel was told that “after the sixty-two weeks the Messiah will be cut off and have nothing,” and that afterward the city and sanctuary would be destroyed (Dan 9:26). The Messiah would come and be killed before Jerusalem and the temple fell. The temple was destroyed in A.D. 70. Whatever else one says about Daniel’s seventy weeks, this verse places the Messiah’s death before that date. (For the full prophetic timeline, see the site’s study God’s Prophetical Program for the Ages.)

F. The Sign of Jonah (Jonah 1:17)

Jonah was “in the stomach of the fish three days and three nights” (Jonah 1:17). Jesus Himself chose this as the sign of His death and resurrection: “just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the sea monster, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth” (Matt 12:40). Paul says Christ “was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures” (1 Cor 15:4). Jonah, Abraham’s three-day journey to Moriah, and the resurrection promises of the Psalms all stand behind that phrase.

G. Faith, Calling and the Pierced One (Habakkuk 2:4; Joel 2:32; Zechariah 12:10)

Three short texts round out the prophets. Habakkuk: “the righteous will live by his faith” (Hab 2:4), the verse Paul places at the head of Romans (Rom 1:17). Joel: “whoever calls on the name of the Lord will be delivered” (Joel 2:32), which Paul applies to all who believe in Jesus (Rom 10:13). And Zechariah, looking to Israel’s future repentance: “they will look on Me whom they have pierced; and they will mourn for Him, as one mourns for an only son” (Zech 12:10). John cites this verse at the cross (John 19:37).

IV. The Psalms

“And the Psalms.” The apostles quoted the Psalms more than any other Old Testament book when they preached Christ.

A. The Son (Psalm 2)

“He said to Me, ‘You are My Son, today I have begotten You’” (Ps 2:7). The Lord’s Anointed (2:2) is also His Son. Paul applied this verse to the resurrection, the day God publicly declared His Son (Acts 13:33; Rom 1:4).

B. No Decay (Psalm 16:10)

For You will not abandon my soul to Sheol;
Nor will You allow Your Holy One to undergo decay.

— Psalm 16:10 (NASB95)

Both Peter (Acts 2:25–32) and Paul (Acts 13:35–37) made the same argument from this verse. David died, was buried, and his body decayed; his tomb was still in Jerusalem. So David was not speaking of himself. He “looked ahead and spoke of the resurrection of the Christ” (Acts 2:31).

C. From Forsaken to the Ends of the Earth (Psalm 22)

Psalm 22 moves through all three of Jesus’ points in a single psalm. It opens with the cry Jesus spoke from the cross: “My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?” (22:1). It describes the scene in detail: “They pierced my hands and my feet” (22:16); “They divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots” (22:18; John 19:24). Then, suddenly, the sufferer is alive and praising: “I will tell of Your name to my brethren; in the midst of the assembly I will praise You” (22:22; Heb 2:12). And the psalm ends with the nations: “All the ends of the earth will remember and turn to the Lord” (22:27). Suffering, resurrection, and the gospel to all nations, all in one psalm.

D. At the Right Hand (Psalm 110)

“The Lord says to my Lord: ‘Sit at My right hand until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet’” (110:1). David calls his own descendant “my Lord.” Jesus used this verse to show that the Messiah is more than David’s son (Matt 22:41–45), and Peter used it at Pentecost to prove that God had made Jesus “both Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:34–36). Psalm 110:4 adds that He is “a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek,” the theme of Hebrews 7.

E. The Rejected Stone (Psalm 118:22)

“The stone which the builders rejected has become the chief corner stone” (118:22). Jesus applied it to Himself (Matt 21:42), and Peter told the Sanhedrin that Jesus was that stone, adding, “there is salvation in no one else” (Acts 4:11–12).

V. Walking the Emmaus Road: A Seven-Step Presentation

With about fifteen minutes and only an Old Testament, you can follow the same two steps Paul used in Acts 17:2–3: show what the Messiah must be, then show that Jesus is He. Here is one simple path.

StepPassagePoint
1. The problem and the promiseGenesis 3:15Sin entered, and God promised a Deliverer who would be wounded and win.
2. A substituteGenesis 22; Exodus 12God provides a lamb, and the lamb dies in place of the son.
3. Who He isIsaiah 9:6; Micah 5:2The Deliverer is born in Bethlehem, and He is Mighty God.
4. He dies for sinsIsaiah 53:4–6Pierced for our transgressions; our iniquity laid on Him.
5. He risesPsalm 16:10; Isaiah 53:10–11; Jonah 1:17No decay; His days prolonged; the sign of the third day.
6. What God asksNumbers 21:8–9; Genesis 15:6Look and live; believe and be counted righteous.
7. It is offered to anyoneIsaiah 55:1; Joel 2:32Without money and without cost; whoever calls.

Then make the turn Paul made in Thessalonica: “This Jesus whom I am proclaiming to you is the Christ” (Acts 17:3). The Gospels supply the second half of the case. Jesus was born in Bethlehem (Matt 2:1), was crucified, was buried in a rich man’s tomb, and rose on the third day, seen by many witnesses (1 Cor 15:3–8). And He makes the same offer the Old Testament made: “Truly, truly, I say to you, he who believes has eternal life” (John 6:47).

Close where John closed: these things are written “so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing you may have life in His name” (John 20:31).6 Then ask the question Jesus asked Martha: “Do you believe this?” (John 11:26).

VI. Guardrails for Using the Old Testament

A few guidelines will keep our use of the Old Testament honest and persuasive.

1. Start where the apostles started

The strongest passages are the ones the New Testament itself quotes: Isaiah 53, Psalm 16, Psalm 22, Psalm 110, Numbers 21 (in John 3), Genesis 15:6 (in Romans 4). No one can dismiss these as later Christian imagination when the apostles used them in the first generation.

2. Know whether you are using a prophecy or a picture

Isaiah 53, Micah 5:2 and Psalm 16:10 are direct predictions. Isaac on Moriah, the Passover lamb and the bronze serpent are types, real historical events that God designed to picture Christ, and the New Testament itself tells us so (Heb 11:19; 1 Cor 5:7; John 3:14). Both kinds are valid, but they make different kinds of arguments. Be clear about which one you are using.

3. Some texts are debated

Hosea 6:2 (“He will raise us up on the third day”) is sometimes cited for the resurrection, but many read it as about Israel’s national restoration. Lead with Jonah, which Jesus Himself cited, and use Hosea, if at all, as supporting background.

4. Do not drop the response

The Old Testament does not only point to Christ’s work; it shows how a person receives its benefit. Genesis 15:6 and Numbers 21 show from Moses himself that God has always asked for faith, not works. That makes the Old Testament presentation the same gospel as John 3:16, not just background to it.

Lord, thank You.

Study Questions

What three sections of Scripture did Jesus name in Luke 24:44?The Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms.
What three points did He say they teach (24:46–47)?The Christ would suffer; He would rise on the third day; forgiveness would be proclaimed in His name to all nations.
What does Acts 10:43 say all the prophets bear witness to?That everyone who believes in Him receives forgiveness of sins.
Why is Genesis 15:6 so important?Abraham was declared righteous by believing, before circumcision and before the Law.
How did Jesus use Numbers 21?As a picture of believing in Him: whoever looks lives; whoever believes has eternal life (John 3:14–15).
What five things does Isaiah 53 show?He suffers, suffers in our place, dies and is buried, lives again, and justifies many.
What three points does Psalm 22 cover?Suffering (vv. 1–18), deliverance and praise (v. 22), and the nations turning to the Lord (v. 27).

Part 2 — Discussion

1. Why did the Emmaus disciples need the Scriptures more than a sight of the risen Jesus?

Show Leader Note

Their problem was not missing facts but misunderstanding. They did not see that the Christ had to suffer. Only the opened Scriptures could show them that the cross fulfilled God’s plan rather than ending it.

2. How does Genesis 15:6 answer the claim that people in the Old Testament were saved by keeping the Law?

Show Leader Note

Abraham was credited with righteousness by believing God’s promise, before circumcision (Gen 17) and 430 years before the Law (Gal 3:17). Paul builds Romans 4 on this verse.

3. What is the difference between a prophecy and a type? Give an example of each.

Show Leader Note

A prophecy directly predicts (Isaiah 53; Micah 5:2). A type is a real event God designed as a picture, confirmed by the New Testament (the Passover lamb, 1 Cor 5:7; the bronze serpent, John 3:14).

4. How does Isaiah 53:10–11 point to the resurrection?

Show Leader Note

The Servant dies as a guilt offering, yet afterward “He will see His offspring, He will prolong His days.” A man who has died can only prolong his days by rising.

5. Why does the commentary say not to “drop the response”?

Show Leader Note

The Old Testament shows not only Christ’s work but how a person receives it: by faith (Gen 15:6; Num 21:8–9). Leaving that out turns the gospel into background information rather than an offer.

⑤ Application

Living and sharing what we have studied

Application 1 — Read the Old Testament With the Key in Hand

Luke 24:27, 44–45

From ③ I.A–B; cf. Constable, Notes on Luke.

Jesus said the whole Old Testament speaks of Him. When we read Genesis, Leviticus or the Psalms, we can ask: how does this point to the Christ who suffered, rose, and offers forgiveness to all? The Old Testament becomes a book about Jesus, not only a book of history and law.

Reflection: Do I treat the Old Testament as a closed book, or as a book about Christ?

Practical Step: This week, read Isaiah 52:13–53:12 slowly and list every phrase fulfilled in Jesus.

Application 2 — Rest Where Abraham Rested

Genesis 15:6; Romans 4:5

From ③ II.C; Wilkin, “Faith Alone in Four Verses.”

Abraham was counted righteous the moment he believed God’s promise. Nothing he did afterward added to it, and nothing he failed to do took it away. Eternal life is the same: received by faith alone in Christ alone, a free gift that can never be lost (John 10:28–29).

Reflection: Am I still trying to earn what God has already credited to those who believe?

Practical Step: Write Genesis 15:6 and John 6:47 on a card, and read them each morning this week.

Application 3 — Learn One Path, and Use It

Acts 17:2–3

From ③ V.

You do not need to master every prophecy to share the gospel from the Old Testament. Learn the seven steps, mark the passages in your Bible, and practice them until you can walk someone through them in fifteen minutes.

Reflection: What keeps me from sharing the gospel: lack of knowledge or lack of practice?

Practical Step: Mark the seven passages in your Bible with a small number (1–7) in the margin, then walk a friend or family member through them.

Application 4 — Offer It Freely

Isaiah 55:1; Revelation 22:17

From ③ III.C.

God’s offer is “without money and without cost.” When we share the gospel, we must not add conditions God did not set. We offer living water to anyone who is thirsty and invite them to take it.

Reflection: Have I ever made the gospel sound harder to receive than God made it?

Practical Step: Practice saying the gospel in one sentence: Jesus, the Christ, died for your sins and rose again, and whoever believes in Him has eternal life.
