② Devotional

Drawn from the commentary · For personal reading

We Have Found Him

“We have found Him of whom Moses in the Law and also the Prophets wrote—Jesus of Nazareth.”
— John 1:45 (NASB95)

Philip did not tell Nathanael that he had found a good teacher. He said he had found the One the Scriptures had been describing for centuries. The first disciples came to Jesus with their Bibles open, and they recognized Him because the prophets had already told them what to look for.

Faith is still born that way. On the road to Emmaus, Jesus began “with Moses and with all the prophets” and explained the things concerning Himself. In Thessalonica, Paul first showed what the Christ had to be, and only then said, “This Jesus … is the Christ.” The prophets are not an extra in our witness. They are the witnesses God placed ahead of time.

And the point of their witness is life. John wrote so that we may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing we may have life in His name. Be sure of who He is, and you can rest in what He promises.

For Reflection

  1. Which Old Testament promise about the Messiah has done the most to strengthen your own confidence in Jesus?
  2. Who in your life might be helped by hearing what the prophets said before hearing your conclusion?
  3. Are you resting today in Jesus’ promise of life because you are sure of who He is?

The prophets drew the portrait; only Jesus fits the frame.

I. John’s Purpose: That You May Believe Jesus Is the Christ

A. A Book Written to Persuade

Most books leave us to guess why they were written. John does not. Near the end of his Gospel he lays his purpose on the table: Jesus did many other signs, “but these have been written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing you may have life in His name.” Everything in the book serves that sentence. The seven signs, the “I am” sayings, the witnesses, the cross and the empty tomb were selected and arranged to persuade.

John writes as a witness building a case. He calls witness after witness: John the Baptist, the Father, the works of Jesus, and the Scriptures themselves. Jesus told His critics, “You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; it is these that testify about Me” (John 5:39). He went further: “For if you believed Moses, you would believe Me, for he wrote about Me” (John 5:46). In John’s courtroom the Old Testament takes the stand for Jesus.

We see it from the first chapter. Andrew runs to his brother and says, “We have found the Messiah” (John 1:41). Philip tells Nathanael, “We have found Him of whom Moses in the Law and also the Prophets wrote—Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph” (John 1:45). These men did not invent a new category for Jesus. They recognized Him from a description they already had. They had been waiting for Someone, and the prophets had told them what He would be like.

B. What “the Christ, the Son of God” Means in John

“Christ” is not Jesus’ last name. It is a title, the Greek Christos, which translates the Hebrew mashiach, “anointed one.” John is the only New Testament writer who uses the Hebrew-based word “Messiah” and then translates it for his readers (John 1:41; 4:25). He wants Gentile readers to understand that Jesus is the One Israel’s Scriptures promised. “Son of God” sits beside “Christ” because the two were already joined in the Old Testament. God promised David a descendant and said, “I will be a father to him and he will be a son to Me” (2 Samuel 7:14), and in Psalm 2 the LORD says to His anointed King, “You are My Son” (Psalm 2:7).

In John’s Gospel, though, believing that Jesus is the Christ is never merely agreeing with a title. The clearest example is Martha at the grave of Lazarus:

25 Jesus said to her, “I am the resurrection and the life; he who believes in Me will live even if he dies,

26 and everyone who lives and believes in Me will never die. Do you believe this?”

27 She said to Him, “Yes, Lord; I have believed that You are the Christ, the Son of God, even He who comes into the world.”

— John 11:25–27 (NASB95)

Jesus asks whether she believes His promise of life that can never be lost. She answers by confessing who He is. In John’s Gospel the two cannot be separated. Bob Wilkin of the Grace Evangelical Society puts it plainly: “In John’s Gospel, as well as 1 John 5:1, to believe that Jesus is the Christ is to believe that He guarantees everlasting life to all who believe in Him.”1 Shawn Lazar observes that, for Martha, “the title ‘the Christ’ was shorthand for the One Who gives eternal life through faith.”2 John Niemelä makes the same connection from the other side: “No ordinary man could be the guarantor of everlasting life. Her faith in the promise rests on knowing who He is.”3

So John 20:31 has two clauses that belong together. The first gives the content of faith: Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God. The second gives the result: by believing, you have life in His name. Wilkin summarizes the verse this way: “The sole condition of everlasting life, according to the purpose statement of John’s Gospel, is believing that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God. Nothing else is required.”4 Charles Bing reaches the same conclusion from the whole Gospel: “In John, faith is trustful reliance on Christ’s promise to give eternal life to those who believe.”5

C. A Neglected Half of the Message

When we share the gospel today, we usually explain the work of Christ: He died for our sins and rose again. That is right and necessary. But John’s purpose statement puts weight on the person as well: who Jesus is. In a culture that knows the name “Jesus Christ” mainly as a phrase, many people have never considered that “Christ” is a claim. To say “Jesus is the Christ” is to say that a specific Person, born in Bethlehem and crucified in Jerusalem, is the One God promised through Moses and the prophets.

That claim can be tested. The prophets wrote centuries before Jesus was born, and their words are in the Hebrew Scriptures that Jewish communities have preserved with great care. Pointing to them lets a listener see that the gospel is not a story someone assembled after the fact. God announced His plan beforehand and then kept His word. Paul says the gospel is the message God “promised beforehand through His prophets in the holy Scriptures, concerning His Son” (Romans 1:2–3). When we leave the prophets out, we leave out one of God’s chosen witnesses.

II. The Apostolic Method: Reasoning from the Scriptures

A. Jesus Taught It First

The apostles did not invent this approach. They learned it from Jesus on the day He rose. Two disappointed disciples were walking to Emmaus, sure their hopes had died on the cross. Jesus did not first show them His hands. He opened the Bible:

25 And He said to them, “O foolish men and slow of heart to believe in all that the prophets have spoken!

26 Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things and to enter into His glory?”

27 Then beginning with Moses and with all the prophets, He explained to them the things concerning Himself in all the Scriptures.

— Luke 24:25–27 (NASB95)

Later that evening He did the same for the eleven, naming all three sections of the Hebrew Bible: “the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms” (Luke 24:44). Then “He opened their minds to understand the Scriptures” (Luke 24:45). Notice the word “necessary” in verse 26. The suffering of the Christ was not a tragic surprise that needed explaining away. It was written, so it had to happen. The Greek verb (dei) expresses divine necessity.

John admits that the disciples did not grasp this at first: “For as yet they did not understand the Scripture, that He must rise again from the dead” (John 20:9). Their faith became settled when the risen Lord connected His suffering and resurrection to what had been written. Ours does too.

B. Step One: What the Christ Must Be

Luke gives us a short but complete picture of Paul’s method in the synagogue at Thessalonica:

2 And according to Paul’s custom, he went to them, and for three Sabbaths reasoned with them from the Scriptures,

3 explaining and giving evidence that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, “This Jesus whom I am proclaiming to you is the Christ.”

— Acts 17:2–3 (NASB95)

There are two steps here, and their order matters. First, Paul reasoned from the Scriptures that “the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead.” At this stage he is not yet talking about Jesus. He is building the job description from the Bible his listeners already trusted. What must the Messiah be? Where would He come from? When would He come? What would happen to Him?

The verbs are worth noticing. Paul was “explaining” (dianoigō, to open fully, the same verb Luke uses for Jesus opening the Scriptures and the disciples’ minds in Luke 24:32 and 45) and “giving evidence” (paratithēmi, to set something alongside). He laid the texts side by side and let them build a portrait.6

C. Step Two: This Jesus Is the Christ

Only after the portrait was clear did Paul say, “This Jesus whom I am proclaiming to you is the Christ.” Now the question is no longer abstract. The listener has agreed to the description and must ask, “Who in history fits it?”

This order is persuasive for a simple reason. When we open with our conclusion, a skeptical listener braces against everything that follows. When we begin with what the prophets said, the listener weighs the evidence before facing the claim. By the time Jesus’ name comes up, the description is already on the table, and the fit speaks for itself.

D. The Pattern Repeated

The same method runs all through Acts. At Pentecost Peter quoted Psalm 16 and argued that David “looked ahead and spoke of the resurrection of the Christ” (Acts 2:31). Philip found the Ethiopian official reading Isaiah 53, “and beginning from this Scripture he preached Jesus to him” (Acts 8:35). At Pisidian Antioch Paul worked through Psalm 2, Isaiah 55 and Psalm 16 (Acts 13:33–35). Apollos “powerfully refuted the Jews in public, demonstrating by the Scriptures that Jesus was the Christ” (Acts 18:28). Even Paul’s summary of the gospel twice says “according to the Scriptures” (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).

The apostles never treated the Old Testament as a museum piece. It was their main evidence. If the early church needed the prophets to make its case, so do we.

III. The Messiah’s Portrait in the Prophets

One prophecy can be explained away. A dozen independent lines that meet in one Person are much harder to dismiss. Arnold Fruchtenbaum, who has written extensively on messianic prophecy from a Jewish-believer and dispensational perspective, shows how the Old Testament builds its picture of the Messiah in layers, each narrowing the field.7 Below are six strands of that portrait. Each one can be read with a listener straight from the Hebrew Scriptures.

A. His Line and His Birthplace

The Messiah would come from Judah: “The scepter shall not depart from Judah, nor the ruler’s staff from between his feet, until Shiloh comes” (Genesis 49:10). Ancient Jewish interpreters read this verse messianically. The Aramaic Targum Onkelos renders “Shiloh” as the Messiah. Within Judah, God narrowed the line to David’s house and promised David, “I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever” (2 Samuel 7:13). Isaiah called the coming King “a shoot … from the stem of Jesse,” David’s father (Isaiah 11:1).

Micah even named the town:

2 “But as for you, Bethlehem Ephrathah,

Too little to be among the clans of Judah,

From you One will go forth for Me to be ruler in Israel.

His goings forth are from long ago,

From the days of eternity.”

— Micah 5:2 (NASB95)

Notice the final line. This ruler is born in a small town, yet His “goings forth are from long ago, from the days of eternity.” The prophet hints that the coming King existed before His birth.

B. His Time

This is the most underused strand in modern witness, and one of the strongest. Several prophecies require the Messiah to come while the second temple was still standing, and that temple was destroyed by Rome in AD 70.

25 So you are to know and discern that from the issuing of a decree to restore and rebuild Jerusalem until Messiah the Prince there will be seven weeks and sixty-two weeks; it will be built again, with plaza and moat, even in times of distress.

26 Then after the sixty-two weeks the Messiah will be cut off and have nothing, and the people of the prince who is to come will destroy the city and the sanctuary. And its end will come with a flood; even to the end there will be war; desolations are determined.

— Daniel 9:25–26 (NASB95)

Read the order carefully. The Messiah is “cut off and [has] nothing,” and after that “the people of the prince who is to come will destroy the city and the sanctuary.” So the Messiah must come and be cut off before the temple is destroyed. Dispensational interpreters such as John Walvoord and J. Dwight Pentecost have shown how the sixty-nine weeks of years bring us to the time of Christ, with the seventieth week still future.8 Even apart from exact date calculations, the order of events in the text is plain.

Malachi agrees: “The Lord, whom you seek, will suddenly come to His temple” (Malachi 3:1). He must come to a standing temple. Haggai, speaking to the people who rebuilt that second temple, promised, “The latter glory of this house will be greater than the former” (Haggai 2:9). The second temple had no ark and no visible glory cloud, so how could its glory surpass Solomon’s? The answer is that the Lord Himself walked in its courts.

This gives us a simple, powerful question to ask: if the Messiah had to come before AD 70, and Jesus is not He, then who was? For nearly two thousand years there has been no temple for the Messiah to come to.

C. His Suffering

Isaiah’s fourth Servant Song (Isaiah 52:13–53:12) is the most important single text for this conversation. It begins with exaltation, “Behold, My servant will prosper, He will be high and lifted up and greatly exalted” (Isaiah 52:13), and then describes that same Servant despised, wounded and killed for the sins of others:

5 But He was pierced through for our transgressions,

He was crushed for our iniquities;

The chastening for our well-being fell upon Him,

And by His scourging we are healed.

6 All of us like sheep have gone astray,

Each of us has turned to his own way;

But the Lord has caused the iniquity of us all

To fall on Him.

— Isaiah 53:5–6 (NASB95)

The Servant suffers as a substitute. He is “pierced through for our transgressions,” and the LORD causes “the iniquity of us all to fall on Him.” The Aramaic Targum of Jonathan, an ancient Jewish paraphrase, reads Isaiah 52:13 as “My servant the Messiah,” which shows that this passage was understood messianically long before the medieval period.9

Psalm 22 describes a righteous sufferer surrounded by enemies who divide his garments and cast lots for his clothing (Psalm 22:18), and the Gospels record that very scene at the cross (John 19:23–24). Zechariah adds a surprising detail: the LORD Himself says, “They will look on Me whom they have pierced; and they will mourn for Him” (Zechariah 12:10). John quotes this text at the cross (John 19:37). The One pierced is in some sense the LORD who speaks, a hint of the Messiah’s deity woven into the description of His suffering.

D. His Vindication

The suffering Servant does not stay dead. Isaiah says that after His life is made “a guilt offering, He will see His offspring, He will prolong His days” (Isaiah 53:10). A dead man does not see his offspring or prolong his days unless he lives again. David wrote, “You will not abandon my soul to Sheol; nor will You allow Your Holy One to undergo decay” (Psalm 16:10). Peter pointed out that David died, was buried and decayed, so David “looked ahead and spoke of the resurrection of the Christ” (Acts 2:29–31). The resurrection was not an unexpected twist. It was written.

E. His Identity: More Than a Man

The prophets also prepared Israel to expect a Messiah who would be more than a man. Isaiah announced a child who would be called “Mighty God” (Isaiah 9:6), the same title Isaiah uses for the LORD a chapter later (Isaiah 10:21). Jeremiah says the righteous Branch of David will be called “The LORD our righteousness” (Jeremiah 23:6). Daniel saw “One like a Son of Man” come with the clouds of heaven and receive an everlasting kingdom that all peoples serve (Daniel 7:13–14), and Jesus took that title as His favorite self-designation.

Jesus Himself used Psalm 110 to press the point. He asked the Pharisees whose son the Christ is. When they answered, “The son of David,” He asked how David could then call Him “Lord” (Matthew 22:42–45; Psalm 110:1). The answer they could not give is the one John gives: the Christ is David’s son and David’s Lord, the Son of God.

F. His Reach: A Light to the Nations

The prophets also said the Messiah’s salvation would not stop at Israel’s borders. The LORD says to His Servant, “It is too small a thing that You should be My Servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob … I will also make You a light of the nations so that My salvation may reach to the end of the earth” (Isaiah 49:6). That a crucified Jewish teacher is worshiped today among nearly every people on earth is itself a fulfillment a listener can see. It also keeps Israel’s distinct future in view: the same prophets who promised blessing to the nations promised that Israel would one day look on the One they pierced (Zechariah 12:10).

IV. John’s Own Way: Patterns That Point

John uses predictive prophecy, but his favorite method is pattern, what Bible students call typology. He shows that the people, events and institutions of Israel’s history were shaped by God to point ahead to Christ. This kind of evidence moves people who are not persuaded by a single prediction. It shows that the whole story of the Old Testament leans toward Jesus.

A. The Lamb and the Lifted Serpent

John the Baptist introduces Jesus with the words “Behold, the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!” (John 1:29). At the cross John notes that the soldiers did not break Jesus’ legs, “to fulfill the Scripture, ‘Not a bone of Him shall be broken’” (John 19:36). That is the rule for the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:46). John wants us to see Jesus as the true Passover Lamb, dying at Passover, whose blood shelters those who trust in Him.

In John 3, Jesus pointed Nicodemus to an even stranger picture:

14 As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of Man be lifted up;

15 so that whoever believes will in Him have eternal life.

— John 3:14–15 (NASB95)

In Numbers 21 the dying Israelites were told simply to look at the bronze serpent on the pole, and “when he looked to the bronze serpent, he lived” (Numbers 21:9). They did not have to clean themselves up or promise to do better; they looked and lived. Jesus chose that picture to explain believing in Him for eternal life. The pattern carries the very gospel John wants his readers to believe.

B. Manna, Tabernacle, Temple and Feasts

John fills his Gospel with these echoes. The Word “became flesh, and dwelt [literally, tabernacled] among us” (John 1:14). Jesus calls His body the temple (John 2:19–21). After the feeding of the five thousand, He contrasts the manna in the wilderness with Himself, “the bread of life” (John 6:31–35). At the Feast of Booths, with its water-pouring and great lamps, He announces Himself as the source of living water and “the Light of the world” (John 7:37–38; 8:12). Each of Israel’s great institutions turns out to be a signpost.

C. One Messiah, Two Comings

Careful readers of the prophets have always noticed two portraits that seem hard to reconcile. One Messiah reigns in glory over a world at peace. Another is despised, pierced and cut off. Some later rabbinic writers proposed two different Messiahs, a suffering Messiah son of Joseph and a reigning Messiah son of David. The New Testament gives a better answer: one Messiah, two comings. Jesus came first to suffer and rise, and He will come again to reign on David’s throne.

This is also the answer to one of the most common objections: “If Jesus is the Messiah, why is there no world peace?” The prophets themselves set the suffering before the glory. Jesus said it on the Emmaus road: “Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things and to enter into His glory?” (Luke 24:26). The glory has begun at the Father’s right hand, and the kingdom on earth awaits His return. Our premillennial reading of the prophets lets those promises keep their plain meaning.

V. Using the Prophets Well Today

A. Let the Text Speak First

Follow Paul’s order. With a willing friend, read Isaiah 52:13–53:12 aloud without saying who it describes, and then ask, “Who do you think this is about?” Many people, including Jewish readers who have never been shown the passage, are surprised by how clearly it describes Jesus. The Scripture does work that our arguments cannot. Philip simply began from the passage the official was already reading (Acts 8:35).

It also helps to keep a short list of anchor texts in mind, one for each strand of the portrait: Micah 5:2 for the place, Daniel 9:26 for the time, Isaiah 53 for the suffering, Psalm 16:10 for the resurrection, Isaiah 9:6 for His identity, and Isaiah 49:6 for His reach. Six passages are enough to start a long conversation.

B. Be Honest with the Hard Texts

Credibility matters, especially with thoughtful skeptics. A few cautions will keep our witness trustworthy:

  • Respect context. Not every “fulfilled” text is a direct prediction. Matthew applies Hosea 11:1 to Jesus: “Out of Egypt I called My Son” (Matthew 2:15), although Hosea is speaking of Israel’s exodus. Matthew is drawing a pattern: Jesus relives Israel’s story and succeeds where Israel failed. Say so plainly rather than claiming more than the text says.
  • Know the textual questions. In Psalm 22:16 the standard Hebrew text reads “like a lion my hands and feet,” while the ancient Greek translation and a Hebrew manuscript from the Judean desert read a verb meaning “they dug” or “pierced.” The NET Bible notes discuss the evidence.10 The point of Psalm 22 does not rest on one word; the whole psalm portrays a righteous sufferer who is surrounded, stripped and vindicated.
  • Be ready for common counter-readings. Many modern Jewish readers take the Servant of Isaiah 53 to be the nation of Israel, a view associated with medieval commentators such as Rashi. Isaiah himself distinguishes the Servant from the people: He is wounded “for the transgression of my people” (Isaiah 53:8), and He suffers innocently, while Isaiah calls Israel a sinful nation (Isaiah 1:4). Isaiah 7:14 (Hebrew ʿalmâ, a young woman of marriageable age) and the counting of Daniel’s seventy weeks also deserve careful, humble handling.
  • Do not overclaim. Popular probability calculations, which assign huge odds against one man fulfilling many prophecies, often backfire with educated listeners because the inputs are debatable. The cumulative portrait is persuasive on its own terms.

C. Fit the Conversation to the Listener

Paul reasoned differently in the synagogue (Acts 17:2–3) than at the Areopagus (Acts 17:22–31). The prophets serve every audience, but we start in different places:

  • Jewish friends: Start where Paul did, with the Hebrew Scriptures they already honor. Isaiah 53, Daniel 9:26 and Zechariah 12:10 are natural starting points. Avoid treating the conversation as a debate to be won; we are sharing the hope of Israel (Acts 28:20).
  • Muslim friends: Islamic teaching already calls Jesus al-Masih, the Messiah (Qur’an 3:45; 4:171), but it denies that He was crucified (4:157) and that He is the Son of God (4:171). These denials come from a document written six centuries after the events, while Isaiah 53, Psalm 22 and Psalm 2 were written centuries before them. The question becomes what “Messiah” means, and the prophets answer that the Messiah suffers, dies, rises and is God’s Son.
  • Secular listeners: The timing argument and the cumulative portrait are especially useful. Ask, “If someone wrote this description centuries in advance, who in history fits it?”
  • Nominal Christians: Many have heard “Jesus Christ” all their lives without realizing that “Christ” is a claim. Luke 24:27 and 44, where Jesus Himself teaches from the prophets, can awaken them to what they have been saying.

D. Land on the Promise

Everything in this study serves one goal. The prophets establish the credentials of the Christ; the promise is what a person is asked to believe. If we prove that Jesus fulfilled prophecy but never bring a listener to His promise of eternal life, we have stopped short of John’s purpose. John did not write merely to prove that Jesus is the Messiah. He wrote “so that you may believe … and that believing you may have life in His name.”

That is why Martha’s confession is the model. Jesus said, “Everyone who lives and believes in Me will never die. Do you believe this?” She answered, “I have believed that You are the Christ, the Son of God.” Believing that Jesus is the Christ meant believing that He is the One who guarantees resurrection and life to all who believe in Him. And the moment she believed, she had that life as a settled gift, not contingent on what she did afterward (John 5:24; 6:47).

Even within the Reformed tradition, which differs from the Free Grace position on the nature of saving faith, John Calvin defined faith as a firm and certain knowledge of God’s kindness toward us, “founded upon the truth of the freely given promise in Christ.”11 On this point Calvin’s definition and John’s Gospel agree: faith rests on God’s promise in Christ, and assurance belongs to faith itself.12

So when we open the prophets with a friend, we are not running a seminar. We are introducing the One whom Moses and the prophets wrote about, so that our friend can hear Him say, “He who believes in Me will live even if he dies,” and answer as Martha did.

Jesus is the Christ. The prophets said He would come from David’s line, be born in Bethlehem, come before the temple fell, be pierced for our sins, rise from the dead, and reign forever. He did and He will. Whoever believes in Him has eternal life.

Lord, thank You.

Study Questions

Part 1 — Review from the Commentary

QuestionAnswer from the Commentary
Why did John write his Gospel? (John 20:31)So that readers may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and by believing have life in His name.
What does “Christ” mean?“Anointed one,” the Greek for the Hebrew mashiach. It is a title, not a surname.
What are the two steps of Paul’s method? (Acts 17:2–3)First, show from the Scriptures what the Christ must be and do; second, show that Jesus is that Christ.
Which prophecies require the Messiah to come before AD 70?Daniel 9:26, Malachi 3:1 and Haggai 2:9; Genesis 49:10 is often added.
How does the New Testament resolve the “two portraits” of the Messiah?One Messiah, two comings: first to suffer and rise, then to reign.
Why is Martha’s confession the model of saving faith?She believed Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, the One who guarantees life to all who believe in Him (John 11:25–27).

Part 2 — Discussion Questions

1. John says the Scriptures “testify” about Jesus (John 5:39). How does seeing the Old Testament as a witness change the way you use it in conversation?

Show Leader Note

The prophets are evidence God planted ahead of time, not decoration. Because they were written centuries before Jesus and preserved by the Jewish community, they show that the gospel was announced beforehand (Romans 1:2–3). We can invite a friend to examine that witness rather than simply accept our conclusion.

2. Why does the order of Paul’s two steps matter (Acts 17:2–3)?

Show Leader Note

Beginning with the Scriptures lets the listener agree to the description of the Christ before facing the claim about Jesus. A skeptic who hears the conclusion first tends to resist everything after it; one who sees the portrait first must then ask who fits it.

3. Of the six strands in the Messiah’s portrait, which seems most persuasive for people you know, and why?

Show Leader Note

Answers will vary. The timing strand (Daniel 9:26; Malachi 3:1; Haggai 2:9) is often the most striking because it puts a deadline on the Messiah’s coming. Isaiah 53 is the most powerful single text. The strength of the case is cumulative: many independent lines meeting in one Person.

4. How does John 3:14–15 with Numbers 21 picture the simplicity of believing in Jesus for eternal life?

Show Leader Note

The dying Israelites only had to look at the bronze serpent, and they lived. They did not have to fix themselves first. Jesus uses that picture for believing in Him: whoever believes has eternal life. The pattern carries the content of the gospel.

5. Why must a prophecy-based witness “land on the promise”?

Show Leader Note

John’s purpose is not only that people agree Jesus is the Messiah but that by believing they have life (John 20:31). Martha shows that believing Jesus is the Christ means believing He guarantees life to the believer (John 11:25–27). The prophets establish His credentials; His promise is what a person trusts for eternal life.

⑤ Application

Living and sharing what we have studied

Application 1 — Learn the Portrait

Luke 24:27

From ③ III (the six strands); cf. Fruchtenbaum, Messianic Christology.

Jesus began “with Moses and with all the prophets.” You do not need to master every messianic text to follow Him. Learn one anchor passage for each strand: Micah 5:2 (place), Daniel 9:26 (time), Isaiah 53 (suffering), Psalm 16:10 (resurrection), Isaiah 9:6 (identity), and Isaiah 49:6 (reach).

Reflection: Which strand of the portrait do you know least well?

Practical Step: Write the six anchor references on a card and read one passage each day this week.

Application 2 — Read Before You Reveal

Acts 8:35

From ③ II.B–C and V.A (the two-step method).

Philip began from the passage the official was already reading. Let the prophets speak before you name Jesus. Reading Isaiah 53 aloud and asking “Who is this about?” often opens a conversation that arguments cannot.

Reflection: Do you tend to lead with your conclusion or with the evidence?

Practical Step: Practice reading Isaiah 52:13–53:12 aloud with a believing friend this week, then ask the question together.

Application 3 — Be Honest with Hard Texts

1 Peter 3:15

From ③ V.B; cf. NET Bible note on Psalm 22:16.

Peter tells us to be ready “to make a defense … with gentleness and reverence.” Gentleness includes honesty. When a text works by pattern rather than prediction, or carries a textual question, say so. Truthfulness about small points earns a hearing on the large one.

Reflection: Have you ever claimed more for a verse than it says?

Practical Step: Study one hard text this month (Isaiah 7:14, Psalm 22:16 or Daniel 9:24–27) until you can explain it simply and fairly.

Application 4 — Point to the Promise

John 11:25–27

From ③ I.B and V.D; Wilkin, Lazar, Niemelä, Bing (GES).

The goal of the prophetic case is not agreement about a title. It is trust in the One who says, “Everyone who lives and believes in Me will never die.” Make sure every conversation about prophecy ends with Jesus’ own promise of eternal life to the one who believes.

Reflection: Can you state Jesus’ promise of eternal life in one sentence, from memory?

Practical Step: Memorize John 11:25–27 and use Jesus’ question, “Do you believe this?”, to close your next conversation about Him.
