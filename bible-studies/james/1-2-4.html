Joy in the Furnace

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

Source: The Gospel Vault · Developed from teaching outlines by Dr. C. Norman Sellers, ThD, used by permission

① The Biblical Text

James 1:2–4 · New American Standard Bible (NASB95)

2 Consider it all joy, my brethren, when you encounter various trials,

3 knowing that the testing of your faith produces endurance.

4 And let endurance have its perfect result, so that you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing.

Everyone reads before anything else.

② Devotional

“Consider it all joy, my brethren, when you encounter various trials.” — James 1:2

Trials rarely make an appointment. One moment we are walking along, and the next we have fallen into a hole we never saw coming. James does not say “if” trials come but “when,” and he warns that they come in every color: money, health, family, work, grief.

So how can he say, “Consider it all joy”? He is not asking us to feel happy about pain or to paste on a smile. He is asking us to think. “Consider” means to weigh something up and reach a settled judgment. We know something the trial cannot tell us: our Father is using it. Like gold in the refiner’s fire, our faith is being proved, and the result is endurance, the staying power that makes us mature.

Our part is to let Him finish the work. We stop resisting, keep praying, and keep trusting. The trial is temporary; what God builds in us through it will last, and He will reward it when Christ appears.

Reflection Questions

  1. What trial am I facing right now, and how have I been “counting” it so far?
  2. What do I know about God that my feelings in this trial are not telling me?
  3. Where might I be resisting the work God wants to do through this hardship?

Father, I cannot always feel the joy, but I choose to count on what You are doing.

Introduction: Where James Begins

James has just greeted his scattered readers with a single word: chairein, “rejoice” (1:1). Now, in the very next breath, he tells them what to rejoice about, and it is the last thing we would expect: their trials. “Consider it all joy” uses chara, a word from the same family as the greeting. James is not changing the subject. He is explaining how a hard-pressed believer can actually live out the greeting.

It is no accident that trials come first. These believers had been driven from their homes by persecution (Acts 8:1; 11:19). Some had lost property and work; many were poor (2:5–6; 5:4–6). James is a pastor, and a good pastor starts where his people are hurting. The whole prologue of the letter (1:2–18) is about meeting trials well. Verses 2–4 give the right attitude toward trials themselves; verses 5–8 show how to pray for wisdom in them; verses 9–11 give the long view of rich and poor; and verses 12–18 turn our eyes to the good God who stands behind every trial and never tempts anyone to sin.

In this study we will see three things about trials. They are certain, they are valuable, and, by God’s grace, they are manageable.

I. Trials Are Certain (1:2b)

A. “When,” Not “If”

James writes, “when you encounter various trials,” not “if.” Trials are not a sign that something has gone wrong in the Christian life. They are part of it. Long before James, Eliphaz said to Job:

“For man is born for trouble, As sparks fly upward.”

Job 5:7 (NASB95)

Jesus was even more direct:

“These things I have spoken to you, so that in Me you may have peace. In the world you have tribulation, but take courage; I have overcome the world.’”

John 16:33 (NASB95)

Some trials come simply because we live in a fallen world. Bodies wear out, cars break down, roofs leak, and the people we love grow old. Some come because we live in a world of sowing and reaping; our own poor choices have consequences. And some come precisely because we belong to Christ. The believers James wrote to were scattered because they followed Jesus. Peter says plainly that such fiery ordeals should not catch us off guard:

“Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery ordeal among you, which comes upon you for your testing, as though some strange thing were happening to you;”

1 Peter 4:12 (NASB95)

Anyone who promises that faith in Christ will remove all hardship from our lives has not read the New Testament. What Christ promises is not a life without trouble, but His peace in the middle of it.

B. Trials Come Suddenly

The word translated “encounter” is peripiptō. It means to fall into something, to light upon it unexpectedly. Jesus used the same word in the parable of the Good Samaritan:

“Jesus replied and said, ‘A man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and fell among robbers, and they stripped him and beat him, and went away leaving him half dead.”

Luke 10:30 (NASB95)

The traveler was walking along the road, minding his own business, and suddenly he was in the middle of an attack. That is how trials usually come. One moment we are upright; the next we are flat on our backs. There is often no warning.

The word also carries the idea of being surrounded (peri, “around”). Trials have a way of arriving in clusters. The car will not start on the same morning the boss calls you in, and the diagnosis comes in the same week as the bill. When it rains, it pours. James knows this, and he does not pretend otherwise.

C. Trials Come in Many Colors

James calls them “various” trials. The Greek word, poikilos, means many-colored or variegated. Trials come in every shade: financial, physical, emotional, relational, spiritual. Each believer has his own combination. That is one reason we should never envy someone else’s life. We see the outside; we do not see their trials. And their trials would not fit us any better than ours would fit them.

Here is a wonderful thing. Peter uses the same word twice. He speaks of “various trials” (1 Peter 1:6), and then, a few chapters later, he speaks of God’s grace with the very same word:

“As each one has received a special gift, employ it in serving one another as good stewards of the manifold grace of God.”

1 Peter 4:10 (NASB95)

“Manifold” is poikilos. God’s grace is many-colored too. For every color of trial, there is a matching color of grace. There is no shade of hardship that His grace cannot meet.

II. Trials Are Valuable (1:2–3)

A. Two Ways to See a Trial

How we look at a trial largely decides what it does to us. There is a natural way to see it:

  • “This is not right.” Why me? I don’t deserve this.
  • “This is useless.” Nothing good can come of it; it is pure loss.
  • “God is unfair.” Look at all the people who live as they please and seem to have no trouble at all.

James gives us God’s way of seeing it. A trial is a test, and a test has a purpose. It is not random, and it is not wasted.

B. Enrolled in God’s School

Think of the Christian life as God’s school. The Bible is the textbook. The Holy Spirit is the Teacher. And the circumstances of life, including its trials, are the tests. Nobody enjoys tests, and seeing them this way will not make them fun. But it does help to know that the tests are given by a Teacher who wants us to learn, and that every one of them is intended for our spiritual good.

The writer of Hebrews says the same about God’s discipline:

“All discipline for the moment seems not to be joyful, but sorrowful; yet to those who have been trained by it, afterwards it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness.”

Hebrews 12:11 (NASB95)

C. A Trial Is Not a Temptation

The word James uses for “trials” is peirasmos. The same word can mean “temptation,” and the King James Version translates it that way here. But the context shows that James means outward trials in verses 2 and 12, and he will make clear in verse 13 that God never tempts anyone to do evil. God sends or allows trials to strengthen us; temptation to sin rises from our own desires. Study 04 (1:12–18) will develop this. For now, the point is simple: when hardship comes from God’s hand, its aim is our good.

III. Trials Are Manageable: Count, Know, Let (1:2–4)

James gives three verbs that turn a trial from a threat into a tool: count, know, and let. The first concerns our attitude, the second our understanding, the third our will.

A. Count It All Joy: A Settled Evaluation (1:2a)

“Consider” is hēgeomai. It means to think something through, weigh it, and reach a judgment. It is a word of the mind, not of the emotions. Paul uses it the same way:

“But whatever things were gain to me, those things I have counted as loss for the sake of Christ.”

Philippians 3:7 (NASB95)

Paul looked at his former religious credentials, weighed them against Christ, and reached a settled judgment: loss. James asks us to do the same kind of weighing with our trials, and to reach the judgment: joy.

This is not the same as popular “positive thinking,” which offers a cheerful attitude with no reason behind it. James is not asking us to manufacture a feeling. He gives reasons, and they are found in what God is doing. Nor is he asking for a stoic, forced smile. We are not told to be joyful for the trial, as if pain were pleasant, but to count it joy in the trial, because of what God intends through it.

The verb is also in the aorist imperative, a decisive command. When the trial arrives, make the call. Do not wait to see how you feel. Decide how you will count it.

Notice, too, the little word “all.” James does not say “some joy” or “joy mixed with complaint.” He says “all joy,” pure joy. That is only possible when the joy rests on something outside the trial itself.

B. Knowing: What Faith Rests On (1:3a)

James continues, “knowing that…” Joy in trials rests on knowledge, not on feelings. In a trial, our emotions will tell us all kinds of things: that God is absent, that He does not care, that He is angry, that we are being punished. Emotions are real, but they are not reliable guides to the truth. If we follow them, they can make a practical unbeliever out of any of us.

Faith rests on what God has said. Feelings will follow faith in time, but faith must not wait for feelings. Paul uses the same pattern of “knowing” in a parallel passage:

“And not only this, but we also exult in our tribulations, knowing that tribulation brings about perseverance;”

Romans 5:3 (NASB95)

And again:

“And we know that God causes all things to work together for good to those who love God, to those who are called according to His purpose.”

Romans 8:28 (NASB95)

We may not know why a particular trial has come. But we can know what God is doing through it, because He has told us.

C. The Testing of Your Faith (1:3a)

What we know is this: “the testing of your faith produces endurance.” The word for “testing” is dokimion. It describes the process of proving something, as metal is proved in a furnace. The refiner heats gold until the dross rises to the top and can be skimmed away. What is left is purer and stronger. The Old Testament uses the picture often:

“The refining pot is for silver and the furnace for gold, But the Lord tests hearts.”

Proverbs 17:3 (NASB95)

“‘But He knows the way I take; When He has tried me, I shall come forth as gold.”

Job 23:10 (NASB95)

Peter applies it directly to believers:

“so that the proof of your faith, being more precious than gold which is perishable, even though tested by fire, may be found to result in praise and glory and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ;”

1 Peter 1:7 (NASB95)

It is important to notice whose faith is being tested: “your faith,” the faith of “my brethren.” The test is not designed to find out whether these people are really saved. They already have eternal life, received by faith in Christ (1:18). Gold in the refiner’s fire is already gold. The fire does not make it gold; it purifies and proves it. In the same way, trials do not decide whether a believer belongs to God. They prove and strengthen the faith he already has, so that it is “found to result in praise and glory and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ” (cf. Zane C. Hodges, The Epistle of James: Proven Character Through Testing [Irving, TX: Grace Evangelical Society, 1994]).

D. What Testing Produces: Endurance (1:3b)

The product of tested faith is “endurance,” hypomonē. The word is built from hypo, “under,” and menō, “to remain.” It means to remain under a load without running from it. It is not passive resignation (“I guess I just have to take it”), but active staying power, the strength to keep going and keep trusting when the weight stays on.

Endurance is exactly what the Christian life requires, because the Christian life is a race, and not a sprint:

“Therefore, since we have so great a cloud of witnesses surrounding us, let us also lay aside every encumbrance and the sin which so easily entangles us, and let us run with endurance the race that is set before us,”

Hebrews 12:1 (NASB95)

We cannot get endurance from a book or a seminar. God builds it in us, and trials are one of His primary tools. He also grows our faith through His Word (Romans 10:17) and through the gifts of His grace. But there are some things only a trial can produce.

IV. Surrendering to the Process (1:4a)

“And let endurance have its perfect result.” Verse 3 told us what God is doing; verse 4 tells us what we must do. And our part turns out to be, in large measure, getting out of His way.

A. God Is at Work

Every believer is God’s project:

“For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand so that we would walk in them.”

Ephesians 2:10 (NASB95)

“for it is God who is at work in you, both to will and to work for His good pleasure.”

Philippians 2:13 (NASB95)

God works on us all the time. He works through His Word, which “performs its work in you who believe” (1 Thessalonians 2:13). And He works through the circumstances of our lives: the good, the bad, and the painful. He is not finished with any of us yet.

“For I am confident of this very thing, that He who began a good work in you will perfect it until the day of Christ Jesus.”

Philippians 1:6 (NASB95)

B. Our Part: “Let”

“Let endurance have” translates a third-person imperative, echetō. It is a command, but a command to allow. The process is God’s; our responsibility is to cooperate with it, to stop fighting the work He is doing. We can, in fact, hinder that work. A believer who resists God during a trial tends to:

  • stop praying, because God seems far away;
  • stop drawing near, pulling back from fellowship and the Word;
  • stop trusting, and start trying to escape the trial on his own terms; and so
  • stop growing, cutting short the very maturity God intended.

The trial still comes, but it does not produce what it could have. That is the tragedy of a wasted trial.

C. The Surrendered Life (Romans 12:1–2)

Paul describes the same surrendered attitude in Romans 12:

“Therefore I urge you, brethren, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies a living and holy sacrifice, acceptable to God, which is your spiritual service of worship.”

Romans 12:1 (NASB95)

“And do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind, so that you may prove what the will of God is, that which is good and acceptable and perfect.”

Romans 12:2 (NASB95)

Notice the shape of these verses. The appeal is made to “brethren,” people who are already believers. It rests on “the mercies of God,” not on fear. It calls for a single decisive act, presenting ourselves to God as a living sacrifice, followed by an ongoing pattern: stop being conformed to this world, and keep on being transformed. And the result is that we come to know by experience that God’s will is “good and acceptable and perfect.” Paul’s word for “perfect” there is teleios, the same word James uses in verse 4. The surrendered believer, and the believer who lets endurance finish its work, are headed toward the same goal.

V. The Goal of the Process (1:4b)

James now tells us where it all leads: “so that you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing.” Three phrases describe the goal.

A. “Perfect”: Grown Up

Teleios does not mean sinless. James himself will say, “we all stumble in many ways” (3:2). The word means mature, full-grown, having reached its goal. It is the difference between a child and an adult.

Think of visiting a home with a new baby. The baby cries during dinner, and everyone smiles. She throws a fit, and someone says, “Isn’t she precious?” On the drive home, nobody even mentions it; that is what babies do. But if a grown man throws a temper tantrum at the dinner table, the evening is over, and that is all anyone talks about on the way home. Spiritual immaturity is cute in a new believer. In a believer of many years, it is not cute at all. Trials, accepted with endurance, are one of God’s means of growing us up.

“But solid food is for the mature, who because of practice have their senses trained to discern good and evil.”

Hebrews 5:14 (NASB95)

B. “Complete”: Whole in Every Part

Holoklēros means whole, with every part present and in good order. It is the picture of a life that is not lopsided, strong in one area and missing in another, but well-rounded. Peter describes the same kind of growth, with endurance as one of its parts:

“Now for this very reason also, applying all diligence, in your faith supply moral excellence, and in your moral excellence, knowledge,”

2 Peter 1:5 (NASB95)

“and in your knowledge, self-control, and in your self-control, perseverance, and in your perseverance, godliness,”

2 Peter 1:6 (NASB95)

“and in your godliness, brotherly kindness, and in your brotherly kindness, love.”

2 Peter 1:7 (NASB95)

C. “Lacking in Nothing”

Finally, James says the mature believer is “lacking in nothing.” The verb is leipō, to lack or fall short. And here James does something clever. In the very next verse he uses the same word: “But if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God” (1:5). The goal is to lack nothing. But in the middle of a trial, we often discover what we do lack, and the first thing we usually lack is wisdom to know how to handle it. James will take that up in Study 03. The point here is that God does not leave us to supply our own lack. He supplies it.

VI. Joy That Looks Ahead

Why, finally, can a believer count a trial as joy? Because the trial has value, both now and forever.

A. The Value Now

In the present, God uses trials to further His work in our lives, to make us more like Christ, to teach us lessons we would never learn in comfort, and to give us compassion for others who are hurting. Many believers can look back and say that their hardest seasons were also the ones in which they grew the most.

B. The Value Forever

But James also looks ahead. A few verses later he writes:

“Blessed is a man who perseveres under trial; for once he has been approved, he will receive the crown of life which the Lord has promised to those who love Him.”

James 1:12 (NASB95)

Bob Wilkin puts the point well: “We are to handle trials in a way that honors God and that results in being approved by Him.” As for the crown itself, he suggests, “The crown of life here might refer to ruling with Christ in the life to come” (Robert N. Wilkin, “What Is the Crown of Life in James 1:12?” Grace in Focus blog, Grace Evangelical Society, October 3, 2022, https://faithalone.org/?p=104436). The crown is a reward for faithful endurance, not the gift of eternal life itself. Eternal life is already ours, freely and forever, the moment we believe. The reward is something more, granted at the Judgment Seat of Christ to believers who have endured.

Paul shares the same long view:

“For momentary, light affliction is producing for us an eternal weight of glory far beyond all comparison,”

2 Corinthians 4:17 (NASB95)

Jesus taught it too: “Rejoice and be glad, for your reward in heaven is great” (Matthew 5:12). The apostles took Him at His word. After being flogged by the Council, they went away “rejoicing that they had been considered worthy to suffer shame for His name” (Acts 5:41).

C. Our Example

Above all, we have the example of the Lord Himself:

“fixing our eyes on Jesus, the author and perfecter of faith, who for the joy set before Him endured the cross, despising the shame, and has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God.”

Hebrews 12:2 (NASB95)

Jesus did not enjoy the cross. He despised its shame. But He endured it “for the joy set before Him.” He counted the cost, looked past the suffering to what it would accomplish, and endured. That is exactly what James calls us to do.

Conclusion: Count, Know, Let

James has given us three simple words to carry into every trial:

  • Count. Make a settled decision to regard the trial as an occasion for joy, because of what God is doing (1:2).
  • Know. Rest your faith on what God has said, not on what your feelings tell you: your faith is being proved, and endurance is being built (1:3).
  • Let. Surrender to the process. Keep praying, keep trusting, and let endurance finish its work (1:4).

And remember where it leads: to a mature, complete believer who lacks nothing, and who will one day hear the Lord’s approval and receive the reward He has promised to those who love Him.

Lord, thank You.

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. How is “count it all joy” different from simply trying to feel happy in hard times?

Show Leader Answer

It is a decision of the mind based on what God is doing, not an emotion. We count the trial as joy because of its purpose and its reward, even while we feel its pain.

2. Why are our emotions an unreliable guide in a trial? What should our faith rest on instead?

Show Leader Answer

Emotions may tell us God is absent, angry or uncaring. Faith rests on what God has said: that He is proving our faith and building endurance (1:3; Romans 5:3; 8:28).

3. The text speaks of “the testing of your faith.” Does this mean trials show whether a person is really saved? Why or why not?

Show Leader Answer

No. James writes to “brethren” who already have eternal life (1:18). Like gold in the fire, their faith is proved and purified, not created. Trials affect maturity and reward, not salvation.

4. What are some ways a believer might resist God’s work during a trial? What does surrender look like instead?

Show Leader Answer

He may stop praying, pull away from fellowship and the Word, stop trusting, and try to escape on his own terms. Surrender means continuing to pray, trust and obey, letting endurance finish its work (Romans 12:1–2).

5. How does looking ahead to the crown of life (1:12) and the example of Jesus (Hebrews 12:2) make joy in trials possible?

Show Leader Answer

Both look past present pain to future reward and joy. The crown is a reward for endurance, not eternal life itself; Jesus endured the cross “for the joy set before Him.”

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Make the Call Before the Feelings Do

James 1:2; Philippians 3:7

Source: Commentary ③ — III.A

Joy in trials begins with a decision. When the next hardship arrives, we can decide how we will count it before our emotions decide for us.

Reflection: When hard news comes, what is my first reaction usually: complaint, fear, or trust?

Practical Step: Write James 1:2–4 on a card and read it aloud the next time a trial lands, before you talk to anyone else about it.

Answer Your Feelings with the Truth

James 1:3; Romans 8:28

Source: Commentary ③ — III.B

Our feelings in a trial are real but not reliable. Faith answers them with what God has said.

Reflection: What lie do my feelings most often tell me in hard times?

Practical Step: List three things you know about God from Scripture and pray them back to Him this week.

Do Not Waste the Trial

James 1:4; Romans 12:1–2

Source: Commentary ③ — IV.B–C

We can cut short God’s work by pulling away from Him. Surrender means staying close to Him while the trial runs its course.

Reflection: Have I stopped praying, reading or gathering with other believers because of a current trial?

Practical Step: Choose one habit you have let slip during this hardship and restart it this week.

Look Ahead to the Crown

James 1:12; 2 Corinthians 4:17

Source: Commentary ③ — VI.B; cf. Wilkin, “What Is the Crown of Life in James 1:12?”

Our eternal life is secure. The trials we endure faithfully now will be rewarded when Christ appears. That future gives present hardship meaning.

Reflection: How would my attitude change if I believed this trial was producing an “eternal weight of glory”?

Practical Step: Thank God this week, by name, for one trial He has used to grow you, and ask Him for endurance in the one you face now.
