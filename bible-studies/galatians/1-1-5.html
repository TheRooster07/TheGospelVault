The Ground of Liberty

① The Biblical Text

Galatians 1:1–5 · New King James Version (NKJV)

1 Paul, an apostle (not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father who raised Him from the dead),

2 and all the brethren who are with me, To the churches of Galatia:

3 Grace to you and peace from God the Father and our Lord Jesus Christ,

4 who gave Himself for our sins, that He might deliver us from this present evil age, according to the will of our God and Father,

5 to whom be glory forever and ever. Amen.

Everyone reads before anything else.

② Devotional

“Grace to you” is not a wish — it is Paul’s reminder that everything good in your life with God begins as a gift, and stays a gift.

Before Paul says one more word to the Galatians, he has to settle a question: who sent him?

The Judaizers had spent months quietly chipping away at his authority, because they could not out-argue his message. So Paul opens with a parenthesis that is really the whole book in miniature — his apostleship, like your salvation, did not come from any man. It came “through Jesus Christ and God the Father who raised Him from the dead.” Before you can rest in what Paul teaches, rest first in the fact that his message carries the authority of the risen Christ Himself.

Then comes the greeting every one of Paul’s letters opens with — grace and peace. It would be easy to hear “grace” and think only of the moment you first believed. But Paul is not writing to unbelievers here; he is writing to Christians who already have eternal life and are being tempted to think they now need the law to finish what grace started. His greeting quietly answers them before the argument even begins: grace is not just how you got in, it is how you go on. And “peace” — the deep, Hebrew shalom kind of wholeness — is only ever the possession of someone standing on grace, not law.

Paul closes these five verses the way his heart always closes when he thinks about the cross — in worship. Christ “gave Himself for our sins” — an act He chose, not one He suffered — “that He might deliver us from this present evil age.” That deliverance did not stop at the empty tomb.

It reaches into this week, this struggle, this temptation to earn what has already been freely given. No wonder Paul cannot help but break into praise: “to whom be glory forever and ever. Amen.”

Reflection Questions

  1. Where in your life right now are you tempted to treat grace as only the doorway in, rather than the road you walk on every day?
  2. Paul’s authority rested entirely on Christ, not on human approval. Whose approval are you most tempted to seek instead of resting in what God has already said about you?
  3. “Deliver us from this present evil age” describes a rescue that is already underway. What would it look like this week to actually live like someone being delivered, rather than someone still trying to earn deliverance?

Lord, thank You that grace was never meant to stop at the door.

That is our destiny, ascribing glory to God the Father through the Lord Jesus Christ. We will not be alone. We will be joined by all the ones in heaven, in the earth, and under the earth. The confession we make will involve the words, “Lord Jesus Christ” - Philippians 2:8-11

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. Why does Paul spend so much of verse 1 defending his apostleship before he even gets to his main subject?

Show Leader Answer

The Judaizers could not answer Paul's doctrine, so they attacked his authority instead. Paul settles the question of his commission immediately — it comes directly from Christ and the Father, not from any human source — so that everything he says afterward carries full apostolic weight.

2. What does the Greek word for "apostle" (ἀπόστολος) tell us about the nature of Paul's calling?

Show Leader Answer

Apostolos means "one sent with a commission." The Greek word study shows Paul's whole argument turns on who did the sending — and he insists it was Christ and the Father alone, not any group or individual in Jerusalem or Antioch.

3. How does Paul's use of "grace" in verse 3 differ from how the word is often used today?

Show Leader Answer

Today "grace" is often reduced to the moment of initial salvation. The commentary and Greek word study both show that Paul's grace-greeting is aimed at believers already saved — grace as the ongoing, sustaining favor of God for the whole Christian life, not only the doorway into it.

4. According to verse 4 and the word ἐξέληται, in what two senses does Christ "deliver" believers from "this present evil age"?

Show Leader Answer

Both a future, final deliverance when Christ returns and judges the world, and a present, ongoing deliverance from the evil already surrounding believers now — the same present rescue Jesus prayed for in John 17.

5. Why does Paul break into a doxology in verse 5, and what does this reveal about his purpose in writing?

Show Leader Answer

Paul cannot describe the cross and its purpose without worship. The doxology reveals that his ultimate aim — even in a letter written to correct error — is the glory of God, the same aim believers will share forever.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Resting in a Settled Identity

Galatians 1:1

Source: Commentary ③ — The Writer

Paul's confidence never rested on human approval — it rested on the fact that Christ Himself had commissioned him. Much of our own instability comes from measuring ourselves by what others think, rather than by what God has already declared true of us in Christ.

Reflection: Where this week are you tempted to seek human validation instead of resting in what God has already said about you?

Practical Step: Write down one identity truth Scripture gives you (e.g., "sent/called by God," "accepted in the Beloved") and read it aloud each morning this week.

Living Under Grace, Not Under Law

Galatians 1:3

Source: Self-Study Guide ④ (χάρις) and FGR.docx

The Greek word study shows that Paul's grace is not merely the grace that saves but the grace that sustains daily obedience. Zane Hodges makes this same distinction the centerpiece of Free Grace theology — that a settled, gift-based salvation is the very foundation on which grace-empowered discipleship is built, not a truth in tension with it (Zane C. Hodges, Absolutely Free: A Biblical Reply to Lordship Salvation [Grand Rapids: Zondervan, 1989]).

Reflection: Is there an area of your walk with God where you have quietly slipped from grace back into performance?

Practical Step: Name one "rule" you've been keeping to feel acceptable to God, and replace it this week with a specific act of grateful obedience instead.

Walking in Present Deliverance

Galatians 1:4

Source: Self-Study Guide ④ (ἐξέληται)

Deliverance from "this present evil age" is not only a future promise but a present reality Christ is already accomplishing in you. Sanctification, like salvation, flows from what has already been given — not from what still must be achieved.

Reflection: What specific pull of "this present evil age" do you most need to remember you are already being delivered from?

Practical Step: Choose one recurring temptation and, instead of fighting it in your own strength this week, ask God specifically to make His ongoing deliverance real in that moment.

Living for God's Glory Alone

Galatians 1:5

Source: Devotional ②

Paul cannot speak of the cross without worship. Every doctrine in Galatians ultimately serves this one end — that God the Father would receive glory through the Lord Jesus Christ, and that believers would join that chorus now, not only in eternity.

Reflection: What would change this week if every decision were run through the simple question, "Does this give God glory?"

Practical Step: Close this week's study together in a short time of spoken praise, each person completing the sentence: "God, I give You glory because..."
