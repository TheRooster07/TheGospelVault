Opening the Scriptures

How the Apostles Proved Jesus Is the Christ (Messiah)

HOW THIS STUDY FITS

This study looks at the apostles’ method: how Paul “reasoned with them from the Scriptures” in the synagogue at Thessalonica (Acts 17:2–3), how he changed his starting point when he stood before pagans at Athens (Acts 17:22–31), and how John wrote his Gospel “so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God” (John 20:31).

It is one of three companion studies. For whether the New Testament’s claims matched what first-century Jews expected of the Messiah, see The Apostolic Case for Jesus as Messiah. For a walk through the Old Testament passages themselves, see Beginning at Moses and All the Prophets. The three answer three questions: Did He fit? How did they show it? Where is it written?

② Devotional

Drawn from the commentary · For personal reading

“This Jesus whom I am proclaiming to you is the Christ.”
— Acts 17:3 (NASB95)

Paul did not walk into Thessalonica with a new idea. He walked in with an old Book and opened it. For three Sabbaths he showed from the Scriptures what the Christ had to be: One who would suffer and then rise from the dead. Then he set Jesus beside those Scriptures and said, “This is He.”

The risen Lord had done the same thing on the road to Emmaus, and the disciples’ hearts burned as He opened the Scriptures to them (Luke 24:32). The truth has not changed, and neither has the goal. John wrote his whole Gospel for one reason: that we would believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and by believing have life in His name.

When the audience changed, Paul started in a different place, at an altar in Athens or with the rain at Lystra. But he always arrived at the same Person. You do not need to be clever to share the gospel. You need to know where it is written, and to point to Him.

For Reflection

  1. Which Old Testament passage would you open first to show someone that the Christ had to suffer and rise?
  2. Who in your life starts from “no Bible at all,” and what common ground could lead them to the risen Christ?
  3. Have you yourself believed that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and do you know that you have life in His name (1 John 5:13)?

“He opened the Scriptures to them, and their hearts burned within them.”

I. Paul’s Method in Thessalonica (Acts 17:1–4)

Luke rarely pauses to describe how Paul preached. In Acts 17:2–3 he does, and in a few words he gives us the clearest picture in the New Testament of the apostolic method. Thessalonica was the capital of Macedonia, a busy port on the Via Egnatia, the great Roman road running east to west. Paul and Silas had just come from Philippi, where they had been beaten and jailed (Acts 16:22–24; 1 Thess 2:2). They traveled about a hundred miles through Amphipolis and Apollonia, and Luke notes why they stopped in Thessalonica: “there was a synagogue of the Jews” (17:1).

A. A Settled Custom (v. 2a)

“According to Paul’s custom, he went to them.” Wherever there was a synagogue, Paul went there first (Acts 13:5, 14; 14:1; 17:10, 17; 18:4, 19; 19:8). This was not mere convenience. It reflected his conviction that the gospel is “to the Jew first and also to the Greek” (Rom 1:16). It also reflected a practical wisdom: in the synagogue he found people who already had the Scriptures, already read Moses and the Prophets every Sabbath, and already waited for the Messiah. He did not have to build a foundation. He only had to open the one God had already laid.

For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek.

— Romans 1:16 (NASB95)

B. Three Verbs: Reasoned, Explained, Gave Evidence (vv. 2b–3a)

Luke uses three words that together describe the method.

1. “Reasoned” (dialegomai)

The word behind our English “dialogue” describes discussion, not a monologue.1 Paul spoke, listened, answered questions, and spoke again, and he did this “for three Sabbaths.” The gospel was not presented as a mystery to be accepted without thought. It was argued from evidence that anyone in the room could check. The same word describes Paul in the marketplace at Athens (17:17) and in the school of Tyrannus at Ephesus (19:9).

2. “Explaining” (dianoigō)

The word literally means “to open completely.” It is the very word Luke used three times at the end of his Gospel: the disciples’ eyes “were opened” at Emmaus (Luke 24:31); they said their hearts burned “while He was explaining [opening] the Scriptures to us” (24:32); and in the upper room “He opened their minds to understand the Scriptures” (24:45).2 Luke is telling us that Paul did in Thessalonica exactly what the risen Jesus had done. He took a closed book and opened it.

3. “Giving evidence” (paratithēmi)

The third word means “to set before” or “to place beside.” It was used of setting food before a guest (Luke 9:16) and of laying out a case. Paul placed the facts about Jesus alongside the Scriptures so his hearers could compare them for themselves.3 Prophecy on one side, history on the other: do they match?

C. Two Steps: What the Christ Must Be, and Who He Is (v. 3)

The content of Paul’s argument came in two steps, and the order matters.

Step one: from the Scriptures, the Christ “had to” suffer and rise from the dead. The words “had to” translate the Greek dei, “it is necessary.” Paul did not begin with Jesus. He began with God’s plan as written. Many in the synagogue expected a Messiah who would conquer, not one who would suffer. So Paul first had to show that suffering and resurrection were not a problem for the Messiah’s identity but part of His job description. He would have used passages such as Isaiah 53, Psalm 22 and Psalm 16, which the apostles used again and again.

Step two: “This Jesus whom I am proclaiming to you is the Christ.” Only after the Scripture had set the standard did Paul present the Person who met it. Jesus of Nazareth suffered under Pontius Pilate, was buried, and rose on the third day, before many witnesses (1 Cor 15:3–8). He is the One the Scriptures described.

This two-step pattern is worth learning. If we begin with Jesus, some listeners will hear only our opinion. If we begin with what God promised and then show that Jesus fulfilled it, the authority rests on the Word, not on us.

For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received, that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, and that He was buried, and that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures,

— 1 Corinthians 15:3–4 (NASB95)

D. The Response: Persuaded (v. 4)

“Some of them were persuaded.” The verb peithō in the passive means to be convinced, to come to trust. Luke uses it here for what Paul elsewhere calls receiving the word and believing: the Thessalonians “received the word of God… which also performs its work in you who believe” (1 Thess 2:13). Notice what is absent. Paul did not ask them to clean up their lives first, to promise future obedience, or to prove their sincerity. He asked them to be persuaded of the truth about Jesus. Those who were persuaded believed, and at that moment they received eternal life as a free and permanent gift (John 3:16; 6:47). Their changed lives followed, and Paul praised them for it (1 Thess 1:3–10), but the changed life was the fruit of new life, not its price.

Luke also notes who was persuaded: some Jews, “a large number of the God-fearing Greeks and a number of the leading women.” We will return to that detail in section IV.

II. The Method Was Jesus’ Before It Was Paul’s (Luke 24)

Paul did not invent this method. Luke wrote both the Gospel and Acts, and he deliberately ties them together. The end of the Gospel shows the risen Christ teaching from the Scriptures; the book of Acts shows His apostles doing the same thing.

A. Emmaus: “Was It Not Necessary?” (Luke 24:25–27, 32)

Two disappointed disciples walked toward Emmaus on the day of the resurrection. “We were hoping that it was He who was going to redeem Israel” (24:21), they said. Their problem was not a lack of facts. They knew about the cross and had even heard the report of the empty tomb. Their problem was that they did not see how a crucified Messiah fit the Scriptures.

“O foolish men and slow of heart to believe in all that the prophets have spoken! Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things and to enter into His glory?” Then beginning with Moses and with all the prophets, He explained to them the things concerning Himself in all the Scriptures.

— Luke 24:25–27 (NASB95)

Notice the same “necessary” (dei) that Paul used in Acts 17:3. Notice too that Jesus rebuked them for being slow “to believe.” The remedy for their unbelief was the Scripture, opened and explained.

B. Moses, the Prophets and the Psalms (Luke 24:44–47)

That evening in Jerusalem, Jesus gave the whole group a summary of the Old Testament’s message about Him. He named the three sections of the Hebrew Bible, the Law of Moses, the Prophets and the Psalms (the first book of the Writings), and said all three spoke of Him. Then He “opened their minds to understand the Scriptures” and summed up what they say:

“Thus it is written, that the Christ would suffer and rise again from the dead the third day, and that repentance for forgiveness of sins would be proclaimed in His name to all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem.”

— Luke 24:46–47 (NASB95)

Here are the three points the apostles would preach for the rest of their lives: the Christ suffers, the Christ rises, and forgiveness is offered in His name to all nations. In Luke’s usage, the repentance proclaimed is a change of mind about Jesus that leads a person to believe in Him; Peter states the same promise in terms of faith: “everyone who believes in Him receives forgiveness of sins” (Acts 10:43). The companion study, Beginning at Moses and All the Prophets, walks through the Old Testament passages under each of these three points.

III. The Same Pattern Across Acts

Once we see the pattern in Acts 17, we find it everywhere in Acts. The setting changes, the speaker changes, but the method and the message stay the same.

A. Peter in Jerusalem (Acts 2–3)

On the day of Pentecost Peter argued from Psalm 16:10 that David, whose tomb was still in Jerusalem, could not have been speaking of himself when he wrote, “Nor will You allow Your Holy One to undergo decay.” David “looked ahead and spoke of the resurrection of the Christ” (Acts 2:31). A few days later Peter told another crowd that “the things which God announced beforehand by the mouth of all the prophets, that His Christ would suffer, He has thus fulfilled” (3:18). The same two steps: this is what the Christ must be; Jesus is He.

B. Philip and the Ethiopian (Acts 8:30–35)

The Ethiopian official was reading Isaiah 53 aloud in his chariot and asked whether the prophet spoke of himself or of someone else. “Then Philip opened his mouth, and beginning from this Scripture he preached Jesus to him” (8:35). Philip did not need a different starting point. The man was already holding it. When we meet someone who is already reading the Bible, the best place to begin is often the very page they are on.

C. Paul from Damascus to Pisidian Antioch (Acts 9; 13)

Paul’s first preaching, just days after his conversion, used both titles that John would later join in John 20:31. In the synagogues of Damascus he proclaimed Jesus, “He is the Son of God” (9:20), and confounded his opponents “by proving that this Jesus is the Christ” (9:22). The word “proving” (symbibazō) means to put things together, the way a builder fits joints. Paul fit the Scriptures and the facts together until the case held.

Acts 13:16–41 gives us the fullest sample of one of Paul’s synagogue sermons. He walked through Israel’s history to David, then to David’s promised Descendant, then to the cross and empty tomb, citing Psalm 2, Isaiah 55:3 and Psalm 16. He ended with the offer: “through Him everyone who believes is freed from all things, from which you could not be freed through the Law of Moses” (13:39).

D. Berea, Corinth and Apollos (Acts 17:11; 18)

The Bereans are commended because they “received the word with great eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so” (17:11). The apostolic method invites exactly this kind of checking. In Corinth, Paul “was reasoning in the synagogue every Sabbath and trying to persuade Jews and Greeks,” solemnly testifying “that Jesus was the Christ” (18:4–5). Shortly afterward Apollos, “mighty in the Scriptures,” powerfully refuted his opponents, “demonstrating by the Scriptures that Jesus was the Christ” (18:24, 28).

E. Before Agrippa and in Rome (Acts 26; 28)

Near the end of Acts, standing before King Agrippa, Paul summarized his entire ministry in one sentence:

“…stating nothing but what the Prophets and Moses said was going to take place; that the Christ was to suffer, and that by reason of His resurrection from the dead He would be the first to proclaim light both to the Jewish people and to the Gentiles.”

— Acts 26:22–23 (NASB95)

And the last scene of the book shows Paul under house arrest in Rome, “trying to persuade them concerning Jesus, from both the Law of Moses and from the Prophets, from morning until evening” (28:23). From the first chapter of his ministry to the last, the method never changed.

IV. Was It Only for Jews?

It is natural to ask whether “reasoning from the Scriptures” was a tactic for Jewish audiences only. The answer from Acts is: mostly no, but the starting point changed with the audience.

A. Gentiles in the Synagogue (Acts 13:16, 26; 17:4)

First-century synagogues were not filled with Jews alone. They also held proselytes, Gentiles who had fully converted to Judaism, and “God-fearers,” Gentiles who worshiped Israel’s God and attended the synagogue without becoming proselytes. Cornelius was one (Acts 10:2). Paul addressed both groups at Pisidian Antioch: “Men of Israel, and you who fear God, listen” (13:16; cf. 13:26).

In Thessalonica, Luke tells us that among those persuaded were “a large number of the God-fearing Greeks” (17:4). The church that grew there was largely Gentile. Paul later reminded them that they had “turned to God from idols to serve a living and true God” (1 Thess 1:9), something no Jew would need to do. So the Scripture-based method, used in a synagogue, reached a great many Gentiles.

SIDEBAR · THE PHILIPPIAN JAILER (ACTS 16:25–34): THE GOSPEL IN SHORT FORM TO A GENTILE

Between the synagogue at Thessalonica and the Areopagus at Athens, Luke has already shown us a third scene, one chapter earlier. Philippi was “a Roman colony” (16:12), and it seems to have had no synagogue at all. On the Sabbath Paul’s company went “outside the gate to a riverside, where we were supposing that there would be a place of prayer” (16:13). The jailer was a Roman official in a Roman city, with no Scriptures behind him.

He had not come to the question cold. For days a slave girl had followed the missionaries, “crying out, saying, ‘These men are bond-servants of the Most High God, who are proclaiming to you the way of salvation’” (16:17). And on the night of the earthquake, “Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns of praise to God, and the prisoners were listening to them” (16:25). So when he asked, “Sirs, what must I do to be saved?” (16:30), the answer came in one sentence: “Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household” (16:31).

The command was a summary, and the content followed. “And they spoke the word of the Lord to him together with all who were in his house” (16:32). As in Thessalonica, the call to believe came with an explanation of who Jesus is and what He has done.

Each one believed. “…rejoiced greatly, having believed in God with his whole household” (16:34). “You and your household” does not mean the family was saved through the jailer’s faith; each member heard the word and believed for himself.

Faith alone, at the plainest moment in Acts. Asked directly what he must do, the apostles named no work, no promise of reform, and no rite. They named one condition: believe in the Lord Jesus.

A note on the text. The NASB95, following the oldest manuscripts, reads “Believe in the Lord Jesus.” Some manuscripts add “Christ” (so the KJV, NKJV and WEB). Either way the Messiah claim is present, because in Acts “Lord” and “Christ” belong together: “God has made Him both Lord and Christ—this Jesus whom you crucified” (2:36), and Paul preached “faith in our Lord Jesus Christ” (20:21).

Here, then, is the bridge between the two scenes on either side of it. In the synagogue the gospel was argued from the Scriptures over three Sabbaths. In a Philippian jail it was given in short form, in the middle of the night, to a Gentile in crisis. At Athens it began with an altar and ended with the risen Man. The doors differ; the call is the same.

B. When the Audience Had No Scriptures: Lystra and Athens

Luke places a striking contrast in the very same chapter. A few verses after Thessalonica and Berea, Paul stands in the Areopagus at Athens before philosophers who had no Bible at all (17:22–31). He does not quote Moses. He begins with an altar inscribed “TO AN UNKNOWN GOD” (17:23), speaks of the Creator who made the world and gives life to all, and even quotes their own poets: “For we also are His children” (17:28).

But notice where he ends. He moves from creation to accountability, and from accountability to a Man:

“…God is now declaring to men that all people everywhere should repent, because He has fixed a day in which He will judge the world in righteousness through a Man whom He has appointed, having furnished proof to all men by raising Him from the dead.”

— Acts 17:30–31 (NASB95)

The call to repent here is a call for pagans to change their minds about God and their idols and to reckon with the risen Man. Luke reports the result in terms of faith: “some men joined him and believed” (17:34). The philosophers had already noticed the center of Paul’s message: “he was preaching Jesus and the resurrection” (17:18). At Lystra, speaking to villagers, Paul likewise began with the “living God, who made the heaven and the earth,” who “did not leave Himself without witness” in rain and harvests (14:15–17).3

So the entry point adapted to the audience. With people who had the Scriptures, Paul opened the Scriptures. With people who had only creation and conscience, he started there. Either way, he arrived at the same place: Jesus, crucified and risen.

C. One Message, Many Doors (1 Cor 9:22; 1:23–24)

Paul explained his flexibility: “I have become all things to all men, so that I may by all means save some” (1 Cor 9:22). He changed his approach, never his message. To the Corinthians he wrote, “we preach Christ crucified, to Jews a stumbling block and to Gentiles foolishness, but to those who are the called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God” (1 Cor 1:23–24). The door differed; the room was the same.

D. Gentile Believers Were Taught the Scriptures

Once Gentiles believed, Paul did not leave them without the Old Testament. Romans and Galatians, written to largely Gentile churches, are full of Old Testament argument: Abraham believing God (Gen 15:6), Habakkuk 2:4, and the promise to bless all nations (Gen 12:3; Gal 3:8). The righteousness of God, Paul wrote, was “witnessed by the Law and the Prophets” (Rom 3:21). So the Scriptures were not only a Jewish tactic. They were the foundation for every church once people came in.

V. John’s Gospel: The Same Claim for the Whole World (John 20:30–31)

If Acts shows the apostolic method in action, John’s Gospel shows it in writing, and written for readers far beyond the synagogue.

A. A Book With a Stated Purpose

John is the only book of the Bible that states, in so many words, that it was written to bring readers to faith for eternal life. John says he chose certain signs out of many and recorded them “so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing you may have life in His name” (20:31). Notice what John names as the condition. It is not believing plus baptism, believing plus obedience, or believing plus perseverance. It is believing, and the result is life. Bob Wilkin notes that “believing” here is the means by which life is received, and that the object of that faith is Jesus as the Christ, the Son of God.4

There is a small textual question in 20:31. Some manuscripts read a present-tense verb (“that you may continue to believe”), and others an aorist (“that you may come to believe”).5 Either way the condition for life is the same, faith in Jesus, and the book’s many invitations to “whoever believes” (3:16; 5:24; 6:47; 11:25–26) show that John had unbelievers in view.

B. Two Titles: the Christ, the Son of God

John joins two titles. “The Christ” is the Jewish category: the Anointed One, the Messiah promised to Israel. “The Son of God” draws on Psalm 2 and 2 Samuel 7, where the Davidic King is God’s Son, but John fills it with more: the Son who shares the Father’s nature, the Word who “was God” and “became flesh” (1:1, 14). Thomas’s confession, “My Lord and my God!” (20:28), comes just before the purpose statement. The same pairing of titles appears in Peter’s confession (Matt 16:16), in Martha’s (John 11:27), and in Paul’s first sermons (Acts 9:20, 22).

C. Signs and Witnesses, With Scripture Beside Them

Acts 17 moves from Scripture to Jesus. John moves mainly from signs and witnesses to Jesus, with Scripture standing beside them. He records seven signs, from water turned to wine (2:11) to the raising of Lazarus (11:43–44), and then the greatest sign of all, the resurrection. He calls a series of witnesses: John the Baptist, Jesus’ own works, the Father, and the Scriptures themselves (5:31–47).

“You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; it is these that testify about Me;… For if you believed Moses, you would believe Me, for he wrote about Me.”

— John 5:39, 46 (NASB95)

John also notes Scripture fulfilled at key points: Isaiah 53:1 at the people’s unbelief (12:37–38), Psalm 22:18 at the dividing of His garments (19:24), and Exodus 12:46 at the soldiers’ choice not to break His legs: “Not a bone of Him shall be broken” (19:36). The signs and the Scriptures together build one case.

D. Written So Outsiders Could Follow

John writes so that readers with no Jewish background can follow. He translates: “We have found the Messiah (which translated means Christ)” (1:41); “I know that Messiah is coming (He who is called Christ)” (4:25). He explains Jewish customs, such as the stone waterpots “for the Jewish custom of purification” (2:6) and Jewish burial practice (19:40). His prologue speaks of the Logos, the Word, a term that meant something to both Jewish and Greek readers.

The Samaritans of John 4 are a test case. They accepted only the five books of Moses and stood outside mainstream Judaism. The woman at the well asked, “This is not the Christ, is it?” (4:29). After hearing Jesus for themselves, the villagers said, “we have heard for ourselves and know that this One is indeed the Savior of the world” (4:42). Within John’s own story, the Jewish claim, “the Christ,” opens into a universal one, “the Savior of the world.”

E. What “the Christ” Means (John 11:25–27)

A reader unfamiliar with the word “Christ” might ask what exactly they are being asked to believe. John answers before he reaches chapter 20. At the tomb of Lazarus, Jesus said to Martha:

“I am the resurrection and the life; he who believes in Me will live even if he dies, and everyone who lives and believes in Me will never die. Do you believe this?” She said to Him, “Yes, Lord; I have believed that You are the Christ, the Son of God, even He who comes into the world.”

— John 11:25–27 (NASB95)

Martha’s answer matches John 20:31 almost word for word. To believe that Jesus is the Christ, in John’s sense, is to believe He is the One who guarantees resurrection and everlasting life to whoever believes in Him.4 That is why 1 John 5:1 can say, “Whoever believes that Jesus is the Christ is born of God,” and why John can write so that believers “may know that you have eternal life” (1 John 5:13).

VI. Putting It Together

Set side by side, the synagogue at Thessalonica, the Areopagus at Athens and the Gospel of John show one message reaching three kinds of listeners.

The method of opening the Scriptures and showing that Jesus fits them was used first and most fully with people who had the Scriptures, and that included many Gentiles. With people who had no Scripture background, the apostles started from common ground and arrived at the same conclusion. John wrote for both at once. And in every case the response God asked for was the same: believe in Jesus, the Christ, the Son of God, and by believing have life in His name.6

Lord, thank You.

Study Questions

Part 1 — Review

QuestionAnswer from the commentary
Where did Paul go first in Thessalonica, and why?The synagogue: his custom, the gospel “to the Jew first,” and the hearers already had the Scriptures.
What three verbs describe Paul’s method in Acts 17:2–3?Reasoned (discussion), explained (opened), gave evidence (set alongside).
What were the two steps of Paul’s argument?From Scripture the Christ had to suffer and rise; this Jesus is the Christ.
Where else does Luke use the verb “open” for the Scriptures?Luke 24:31, 32, 45, the Emmaus road and the upper room.
Who besides Jews was persuaded in Thessalonica?Many God-fearing Greeks and leading women (17:4).
Where did Paul start in Athens, and where did he end?An altar to an unknown god and creation; the risen Man appointed as Judge.
What is the stated purpose of John’s Gospel?That readers may believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and by believing have life (20:31).

Part 2 — Discussion

1. Why does the order of Paul’s two steps matter?

Show Leader Note

Beginning with what God promised puts the authority on the Word rather than on the speaker’s opinion. Once the standard is set, listeners can see for themselves that Jesus meets it.

2. How does Luke 24 show that Paul’s method came from Jesus?

Show Leader Note

Luke uses the same “necessary” (dei) and the same “open” (dianoigō) in Luke 24 and Acts 17. Jesus opened Moses, the Prophets and the Psalms; Paul did the same in Thessalonica.

3. Was the Scripture-based method only for Jews? Support your answer.

Show Leader Note

No. God-fearing Gentiles sat in synagogues (13:16; 17:4), and the Thessalonian church was largely Gentile (1 Thess 1:9). Pure pagans heard a different starting point (17:22–31), but Gentile churches were then taught the Old Testament (Romans, Galatians).

4. What did Paul ask his hearers to do, and what did he not ask?

Show Leader Note

To be persuaded and believe that Jesus is the Christ. He did not make cleaning up their lives or promising future obedience a condition; changed lives followed as fruit (1 Thess 1:3–10).

5. According to John 11:25–27, what does it mean to believe Jesus is “the Christ”?

Show Leader Note

To believe He is the resurrection and the life, the One who guarantees everlasting life to whoever believes in Him. Martha’s confession matches John 20:31.

⑤ Application

Living and sharing what we have studied

Application 1 — Let the Scripture Set the Standard

Acts 17:2–3

From ③ I.C; cf. Constable, Notes on Acts.

When we share the gospel, we are not asking people to take our word. We are asking them to compare Jesus with what God promised long before He came. That frees us from needing to win arguments and lets the Bible do its own work.

Reflection: Do I tend to start with my opinion or with God’s Word?

Practical Step: Choose one passage (Isaiah 53 or Psalm 16) and practice explaining in two minutes how Jesus fulfills it.

Application 2 — Find the Door Your Friend Can Walk Through

Acts 17:22–31; 1 Corinthians 9:22

From ③ IV.B–C.

Some friends know the Bible; many do not. Paul met the Athenians at their altar and the villagers of Lystra in their harvest. We can begin with creation, with conscience, or with the questions people already ask, and still lead them to the risen Christ.

Reflection: Who in my life needs a different starting point than a Bible verse?

Practical Step: Write down one point of common ground with that person, and pray for an opening this week.

Application 3 — Invite People to Check for Themselves

Acts 17:11

From ③ III.D.

The Bereans were called noble because they searched the Scriptures daily to see if Paul was right. The gospel welcomes examination. An invitation to read the Gospel of John is often more powerful than a long conversation.

Reflection: Am I comfortable inviting someone to test what I believe?

Practical Step: Offer a friend a reading plan through John, a chapter a day for 21 days.

Application 4 — Make Sure of Your Own Life in His Name

John 20:31; 1 John 5:13

From ③ V.A, V.E; Wilkin, “By Believing You Have Life in His Name.”

John wrote so that his readers would believe and have life, and so that believers would know they have eternal life. That life is not earned or kept by our performance. It is received the moment we believe in Jesus as the Christ, the Son of God, and it can never be lost (John 10:28–29).

Reflection: Have I believed in Jesus for the eternal life He promises, and do I rest in that promise?

Practical Step: Read John 11:25–27 aloud, and answer Jesus’ question to Martha for yourself: “Do you believe this?”
