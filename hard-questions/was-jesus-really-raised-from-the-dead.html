50 Hard Questions · Question 13 of 50

Was Jesus Really Raised from the Dead?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

The empty tomb, early eyewitness testimony, and the transformed disciples make the resurrection the best explanation of the evidence — and it guarantees every other promise.

Key verses1 Corinthians 15:3–8 · 1 Corinthians 15:14–17 · John 14:19

Christianity stakes its entire truth claim on this single historical event more explicitly than any other major religion stakes its claims on a verifiable fact. Paul makes the wager unmistakable: “if Christ is not risen, then our preaching is empty and your faith is also empty ... you are still in your sins! ... If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men the most pitiable” (1 Corinthians 15:14, 17, 19). The resurrection is not a pious embellishment on Christianity; if it did not happen, nothing else in the New Testament’s claims about Jesus is worth believing either.

The evidence Paul cites is deliberately, publicly verifiable rather than private or mystical: Christ “was seen by Cephas, then by the twelve. After that He was seen by over five hundred brethren at once, of whom the greater part remain to the present, though some have fallen asleep” (1 Corinthians 15:5–6) — an explicit invitation, written within about twenty-five years of the event, for skeptical readers to go ask the eyewitnesses themselves while most of them were still alive to be questioned. This is not the language of legend accumulating gradually over centuries; it is the language of a claim staked publicly, early, and specifically, while it could still be checked and falsified if untrue.

The circumstantial evidence reinforces the direct testimony. The tomb was empty and its location was known to both friends and enemies of the movement in Jerusalem, the very city where a body could have been produced to end the movement immediately if one existed. The disciples, who fled and hid at Jesus’ arrest (Mark 14:50), were transformed within weeks into public proclaimers willing to suffer and die for the specific claim that they had seen Him risen — a transformation difficult to explain if they knew the claim to be false, since people do not typically endure torture and execution for what they know to be a lie they invented themselves. And the earliest Christian movement, made up overwhelmingly of Jews, abandoned or radically reworked deeply held convictions — Sabbath observance, the temple sacrificial system, strict monotheism’s application to Jesus — convictions that would only be worth abandoning in light of something they were convinced had actually, undeniably happened.

The resurrection also fulfilled specific prophecy rather than arising as an isolated, unanticipated claim: “You will not leave my soul in Sheol, nor will You allow Your Holy One to see corruption” (Psalm 16:10, quoted and applied to Jesus in Acts 2:27–31). Jesus Himself repeatedly predicted His own death and resurrection in advance to His disciples (Matthew 16:21; 17:22–23; 20:18–19), predictions that would have been an enormous, easily falsifiable liability to fabricate after the fact and attribute retroactively to Him.

For the believer, the resurrection is not merely a historical curiosity to be defended apologetically; it is the guarantee of everything else Scripture promises. “Because I live, you will live also” (John 14:19). The same power that raised Christ from the dead is the power that secures the believer’s own future resurrection (Romans 8:11) and stands behind every other promise this paper addresses — assurance, security, and the hope of life after death alike rest on the fact that the tomb, verifiably, was found empty.1
