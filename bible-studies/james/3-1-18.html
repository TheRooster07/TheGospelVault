Bits, Sparks, and the Wisdom from Above

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

① The Biblical Text

James 3:1–18 · New American Standard Bible (NASB95)

1 Let not many of you become teachers, my brethren, knowing that as such we will incur a stricter judgment.

2 For we all stumble in many ways. If anyone does not stumble in what he says, he is a perfect man, able to bridle the whole body as well.

3 Now if we put the bits into the horses’ mouths so that they will obey us, we direct their entire body as well.

4 Look at the ships also, though they are so great and are driven by strong winds, are still directed by a very small rudder wherever the inclination of the pilot desires.

5 So also the tongue is a small part of the body, and yet it boasts of great things. See how great a forest is set aflame by such a small fire!

8 But no one can tame the tongue; it is a restless evil and full of deadly poison.

9 With it we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse men, who have been made in the likeness of God;

10 from the same mouth come both blessing and cursing. My brethren, these things ought not to be this way.

11 Does a fountain send out from the same opening both fresh and bitter water?

12 Can a fig tree, my brethren, produce olives, or a vine produce figs? Nor can salt water produce fresh.

17 But the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits, unwavering, without hypocrisy.

② Devotional

“For we all stumble in many ways. If anyone does not stumble in what he says, he is a perfect man, able to bridle the whole body as well.” — James 3:2

A bit is small, but it turns a strong horse. A rudder is small, but it steers a great ship. James says the tongue is like that. It is a small part of the body, yet it sets the direction of a life. A careless word can start a fire that burns for years. A gracious word can turn a whole conversation, a whole home, toward God.

No one can tame the tongue by willpower. We all stumble, and most of our stumbling comes out of our mouths. But the Spirit who lives in every believer produces what we cannot: self-control, gentleness, peace. When He has control of us, He has control of our words.

James then asks, “Who among you is wise?” The answer is not the one who talks the most, but the one whose life is gentle, pure and peaceable. Wisdom from above does not shout. It sows peace, and it reaps a harvest of righteousness.

Reflection Questions

  1. What did my words build up, or burn down, this week?
  2. Where do I most need the Spirit to bridle my tongue?
  3. Is my “wisdom” gentle and peaceable, or bitter and self-seeking?

Lord, set a guard over my mouth, and fill my words with Your wisdom and peace.

Introduction: Slow to Speak

In Study 01 we saw that James 1:19 is the hinge of the letter: “everyone must be quick to hear, slow to speak and slow to anger.” Chapters 1 and 2 took up the first phrase: receive the Word and do it. Chapter 3 takes up the second: be slow to speak. It is no accident that it follows the call to a working faith. Where works are missing, words are usually plentiful.

Why does James go after the tongue instead of the heart? The tongue is the heart’s spokesman: “his mouth speaks from that which fills his heart” (Luke 6:45). When Paul wanted to show how deeply sin has gone into the human race, he went straight to the mouth:

“‘Their throat is an open grave, With their tongues they keep deceiving,’ ‘The poison of asps is under their lips’; ‘Whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness.’”

Romans 3:13–14 (NASB95)

Jeremiah called the tongue “a deadly arrow” (Jeremiah 9:8). A knife wounds up close; an arrow kills at a distance. Every one of us carries a concealed weapon. It stays concealed until we open our mouths.

I. A Word to Would-Be Teachers (3:1)

“Let not many of you become teachers, my brethren, knowing that as such we will incur a stricter judgment.”

James 3:1 (NASB95)

James begins with a surprising word: “Let not many of you become teachers.” His point is not “never teach.” It is “don’t rush into it.” He makes the case by raising and answering a series of questions.

A. Should Not Every Believer Teach?

At first this sounds strange. Moses said, “Would that all the Lord’s people were prophets” (Numbers 11:29). Jesus sent His disciples out “teaching them to observe all that I commanded you” (Matthew 28:20). Paul said, “woe is me if I do not preach the gospel” (1 Corinthians 9:16), and wrote that the office of overseer, a teaching office, is “a fine work” (1 Timothy 3:1).

So James is not forbidding teaching. He is saying: do not begin without understanding how serious it is. Sinning with your tongue in private is one thing. Sinning with it in public, as a teacher of God’s Word, is another.

B. Official and Unofficial Teachers

The word is didaskalos, a teacher, the Greek equivalent of “Rabbi.” Israel had official teachers, like Nicodemus, “the teacher of Israel” (John 3:10), and many loved the title, “being called Rabbi by men” (Matthew 23:7), more than the task.

The church has official teachers too: “some as pastors and teachers” (Ephesians 4:11). But its meetings were also informal. Visitors could be invited to speak, as Paul was at Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:15), and “each one has a psalm, has a teaching” (1 Corinthians 14:26). Some who spoke up had nothing to say; others had error to say. Paul warned of men “wanting to be teachers of the Law, even though they do not understand either what they are saying or the matters about which they make confident assertions” (1 Timothy 1:7).

It is not so different today: pastors, Bible study and Sunday school teachers, small group leaders, and countless voices online. James is not warning those God has gifted, called and prepared. He is warning the unprepared, the careless and the self-appointed.

C. A Stricter Judgment

“Knowing that as such we will incur a stricter judgment.” Notice the word “we.” James includes himself. Every teacher stands under this warning, the apostle and pastor of Jerusalem included.

What is this judgment? The word krima is neutral; context gives it its meaning. James writes to “my brethren,” born again by the word of truth (1:18). Their eternal destiny is not in question. Every believer will appear before the Judgment Seat of Christ to have his life and service evaluated for reward:

“For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may be recompensed for his deeds in the body, according to what he has done, whether good or bad.”

2 Corinthians 5:10 (NASB95)

“If any man’s work is burned up, he will suffer loss; but he himself will be saved, yet so as through fire.”

1 Corinthians 3:15 (NASB95)

Teachers will be evaluated more strictly because they have been given more: “From everyone who has been given much, much will be required” (Luke 12:48). Leaders “keep watch over your souls as those who will give an account” (Hebrews 13:17). So Paul urged Timothy to be “a workman who does not need to be ashamed” (2 Timothy 2:15), and John urged believers to abide in Christ so that we may “not shrink away from Him in shame at His coming” (1 John 2:28). The danger is not loss of salvation. It is shame and loss of reward before the Lord who called us, and that is no small thing.

II. The Power of a Small Member (3:2–5a)

A. We All Stumble (3:2)

“For we all stumble in many ways. If anyone does not stumble in what he says, he is a perfect man, able to bridle the whole body as well.”

James 3:2 (NASB95)

Again James says “we.” The word ptaiō means to trip or stumble, and we all do, “in many ways.” John wrote, “If we say that we have no sin, we are deceiving ourselves,” and then gave the remedy: “If we confess our sins, He is faithful and righteous to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness” (1 John 1:8–9).

Of all the ways we stumble, the tongue is the chief. So the man who does not stumble in what he says is “a perfect man.” The word teleios means mature, full-grown, as in 1:4, not sinless. James already said that the one who does not bridle his tongue has a worthless religion (1:26). Now he gives the positive side: bridle the tongue, and you can bridle the whole body.

B. The Bit and the Horse (3:3)

“Now if we put the bits into the horses’ mouths so that they will obey us, we direct their entire body as well.” James is probably not thinking of a plow horse, but of a spirited war horse. In battle you want strength and spirit, under control. A small piece of metal turns a thousand-pound animal wherever the rider wants. Control the mouth, and you control the whole body.

C. The Rudder and the Ship (3:4–5a)

Next James points to ships, great vessels driven by strong winds, yet “directed by a very small rudder wherever the inclination of the pilot desires.” Then the application: “So also the tongue is a small part of the body, and yet it boasts of great things” (3:5a). Small, but it sets the direction of a life, for good or for harm.

III. The Destructiveness of the Tongue (3:5b–6)

A. A Small Fire, a Great Forest

“See how great a forest is set aflame by such a small fire!” (3:5b). Anyone who has watched the news during fire season understands. One spark, one careless match, and thousands of acres are gone. The tongue must be controlled because it is so destructive. A rumor, a sharp remark, a half-truth repeated to a friend, and a family, a friendship or a church is in flames.

“And the tongue is a fire, the very world of iniquity; the tongue is set among our members as that which defiles the entire body, and sets on fire the course of our life, and is set on fire by hell.”

James 3:6 (NASB95)

B. A World of Iniquity

“The tongue is a fire, the very world of iniquity.” There is a whole world of sin it can commit: lying, slander, gossip, flattery, boasting, cursing, complaining, filthy talk. Look at Paul’s list of the deeds of the flesh:

“Now the deeds of the flesh are evident, which are: … enmities, strife, jealousy, outbursts of anger, disputes, dissensions, factions, envying …”

Galatians 5:19–21 (NASB95)

Many of these are carried out mainly with the mouth. Strife, outbursts of anger, disputes and factions rarely happen in silence.

C. It Defiles the Whole Body

The tongue “defiles the entire body.” When the Pharisees asked why Jesus’ disciples did not wash their hands before eating, He answered that it is “what proceeds out of the mouth” that defiles (Matthew 15:11), and explained:

“But the things that proceed out of the mouth come from the heart, and those defile the man. For out of the heart come evil thoughts, murders, adulteries, fornications, thefts, false witness, slanders.”

Matthew 15:18–19 (NASB95)

Notice that “false witness” and “slanders” sit in the same list as murders and adulteries. What comes out of the mouth reveals the heart and stains the whole person.

D. It Sets on Fire the Course of Life

The tongue “sets on fire the course of our life.” The phrase is literally “the wheel of birth” or “the wheel of existence” (trochos and genesis): the whole cycle of life from beginning to end. A word spoken in a moment can affect the rest of a life, ours and others’. An old saying puts it well: you are the master of the unspoken word, but the spoken word is master of you.

E. Set on Fire by Hell

Finally, the tongue “is set on fire by hell.” The word is geenna, Gehenna, used everywhere else in the New Testament by Jesus Himself. It was the valley outside Jerusalem where refuse burned, a picture of the place of final judgment. James is not saying the believer is headed there. He is naming the source of the fire. The destructive tongue is lit by Satan and his kingdom. When our words burn others, we are doing the devil’s work.

IV. The Untamable Tongue (3:7–8)

A. Every Creature but One

“Every species of beasts and birds, of reptiles and creatures of the sea, is tamed.” James names the four classes of animals from creation (Genesis 1:26; 9:2). Think of trained horses, falcons, elephants, even dolphins. But there is one creature no one has ever tamed:

“But no one can tame the tongue; it is a restless evil and full of deadly poison.”

James 3:8 (NASB95)

The most untamable beast in the world lives in a cage of teeth, right behind your lips.

B. A Restless Evil, Full of Deadly Poison

James gives two pictures. First, the tongue is a wild animal that cannot be broken: “a restless evil.” The NASB follows manuscripts that read akatastatos, restless, unstable; the Majority Text reads akataschetos, “unruly” (KJV, NKJV). Either way, it will not stay in its place. Second, it is a snake that cannot be trusted: “full of deadly poison” (cf. Psalm 140:3).

C. Is There No Hope?

If no one can tame the tongue, is there no hope? Of course there is. James says no human being can tame it. He does not say God cannot. We will get control of our tongues when the Holy Spirit gets control of us. The fruit of the Spirit includes “gentleness, self-control” (Galatians 5:23), and the believer who walks by the Spirit “will not carry out the desire of the flesh” (Galatians 5:16).

V. The Double Tongue (3:9–12)

A. Blessing and Cursing from One Mouth

“With it we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse men, who have been made in the likeness of God; from the same mouth come both blessing and cursing. My brethren, these things ought not to be this way.”

James 3:9–10 (NASB95)

The same tongue that sings praise on Sunday morning can curse the driver in the parking lot an hour later. We bless God, and we curse people made in His likeness (Genesis 1:27). The word homoiōsis means likeness, resemblance. To curse a person is to attack someone who bears the image of the God we just praised. James’s verdict is short: “My brethren, these things ought not to be this way.”

B. Fountain, Fig Tree and Vine

James turns to nature: “Does a fountain send out from the same opening both fresh and bitter water?” A fig tree does not grow olives, and a vine does not grow figs. Nature is consistent. The human mouth that produces both blessing and cursing is unnatural.

C. Why It Happens: Two Natures

If it should not be this way, why is it? Because the believer still lives with two natures: the old, what we were in Adam, and the new, what we are in Christ. The flesh and the Spirit “are in opposition to one another” (Galatians 5:17; cf. Romans 7:22–23). The double tongue is the old nature talking. The answer is not to try harder, but to walk by the Spirit and let the new nature speak.

VI. Who Is Wise? (3:13)

James told would-be teachers that they need two things. First, a controlled tongue (3:1–12). Second, true wisdom (3:13–18). It has been said that there are two kinds of preachers: those who have something to say, and those who have to say something. James wants the first kind.

“Who among you is wise and understanding? Let him show by his good behavior his deeds in the gentleness of wisdom.”

James 3:13 (NASB95)

A. What Wisdom Is

The Greeks loved philosophia, the love of wisdom, often speculation about invisible things. The Hebrews were practical: wisdom meant knowing how to live in a way that pleases God. Keeping God’s statutes “is your wisdom and your understanding in the sight of the peoples” (Deuteronomy 4:6). The psalmist wrote:

“The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom; A good understanding have all those who do His commandments; His praise endures forever.”

Psalm 111:10 (NASB95)

So we may define it this way: wisdom is a God-given sense of spiritual understanding and discernment that results in God-pleasing decisions and behavior. James has already told us where to get it: “if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God” (1:5).

James pairs “wise” with “understanding.” The word epistēmōn describes someone with expertise, one who knows his field well enough to teach it. Would you rather be a surgeon’s first patient or his five hundredth? Expertise comes with time.

B. Show It by Your Life

Many claimed to be wise. How would anyone know? “Let him show by his good behavior his deeds in the gentleness of wisdom.” The word for behavior is anastrophē, a way of life. The King James rendered it “conversation,” which in 1611 meant conduct, not talk. Wisdom is proven by how a person lives.

Solomon is the great example, and the great warning. He asked God for “an understanding heart to judge Your people to discern between good and evil” (1 Kings 3:9), and God gave him wisdom unlike anyone before him. Yet “when Solomon was old, his wives turned his heart away after other gods” (1 Kings 11:4). Wisdom must be lived out to the end.

True wisdom shows itself “in the gentleness of wisdom.” The word prautēs is meekness: strength under control, like the war horse with the bit in its mouth. Such wisdom takes time. A young man once asked a retiring bank president the key to success. “Good decisions.” “How do I learn to make them?” “Experience.” “And how do I get experience?” “Bad decisions.”

VII. Wisdom from Below (3:14–16)

A. Its Traits

“But if you have bitter jealousy and selfish ambition in your heart, do not be arrogant and so lie against the truth.” The first trait is “bitter jealousy” (zēlos), zeal turned sour. The second is “selfish ambition” (eritheia), a word once used of those who sought office for their own gain. A teacher with these in his heart should not boast; his boasting is a lie against the truth he claims to teach.

B. Its Source

This wisdom “is not that which comes down from above, but is earthly, natural, demonic.” It is “earthly” (epigeios), bounded by this world. It is “natural” (psychikos), the reasoning of the natural man apart from the Spirit (1 Corinthians 2:14). And it is “demonic” (daimoniōdēs). It is the world, the flesh and the devil in one verse.

C. Its Results

“For where jealousy and selfish ambition exist, there is disorder and every evil thing.” The word akatastasia belongs to the same family as the “restless” tongue (3:8) and the “unstable” double-minded man (1:8). Where jealousy and ambition rule, a church becomes chaotic, and chapter 4 will show where that leads: quarrels and conflicts.

VIII. Wisdom from Above (3:17–18)

“But the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits, unwavering, without hypocrisy.”

James 3:17 (NASB95)

A. Eight Marks of Heavenly Wisdom

In contrast, here is the wisdom that comes down from God. James lists its qualities like a portrait:

Notice that it is “first pure.” Purity comes first; peace does not mean peace at any price. But once it is pure, heavenly wisdom is gentle, open to reason, merciful and real. It is a portrait of the Lord Jesus Himself, “who became to us wisdom from God” (1 Corinthians 1:30).

B. A Harvest Sown in Peace

“And the seed whose fruit is righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace.” Peacemakers sow in peace, and the harvest is righteousness, in their own lives and in others’. Jesus said, “Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God” (Matthew 5:9). A teacher with wisdom from above leaves behind him not a burned forest but a growing field.

Conclusion: Bridled Tongues and Peaceful Seeds

Someone may say, “Then I will play it safe and never teach.” That is not James’s point. If God has gifted you, given you the opportunity and called you, then teach, with your eyes open: to the seriousness of the calling, the danger of an unbridled tongue, and the reward when you stand before the One who called you.

Every believer already has eternal life, received by faith alone and never to be lost (John 5:24). The question James raises is how we will use our words and what we will hear at the Judgment Seat. A tongue under the Spirit’s control, and a life of pure, gentle, peaceable wisdom, will sow a harvest that lasts. “He who guards his mouth and his tongue, Guards his soul from troubles” (Proverbs 21:23).

Lord, thank You.

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. Why does James warn would-be teachers instead of forbidding teaching?

Show Leader Answer

Teaching is good and commanded (Matthew 28:20), but it carries greater accountability. James wants teachers to enter it with their eyes open, not to stop teaching.

2. How does the Judgment Seat of Christ help us understand the “stricter judgment” of 3:1?

Show Leader Answer

Believers are not judged for eternal destiny but for reward (1 Corinthians 3:13–15). Teachers will be evaluated more strictly because they have been given more (Luke 12:48).

3. Which of James’s pictures (bit, rudder, fire, poison, fountain) best describes your own speech this week? Why?

Show Leader Answer

Answers will vary. Encourage honesty and point to confession (1 John 1:9) and the Spirit’s control (Galatians 5:16, 23).

4. If no one can tame the tongue, how can a believer have victory in speech?

Show Leader Answer

Not by willpower but by walking by the Spirit, who produces self-control. Victory comes as the Spirit controls us.

5. How do the marks of wisdom from above (3:17) differ from the way the world defines wisdom?

Show Leader Answer

The world prizes cleverness, ambition and winning. Heavenly wisdom is pure, peaceable, gentle, reasonable, merciful and sincere, and it is proven by conduct.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Take Teaching Seriously

James 3:1; 2 Timothy 2:15

Source: Commentary ③ — I

Whether you lead a class, a small group, your children or a conversation over coffee, you are teaching. Do it carefully, prepared and accurate, as one who will give an account.

Reflection: Am I handling God’s Word accurately when I share it?

Practical Step: Before you next teach or share Scripture, read the passage in context three times and check one thing you plan to say.

Put a Bit in Your Mouth

James 3:2–6; Proverbs 21:23

Source: Commentary ③ — II–III

Small words steer big lives. Before you speak, ask whether your words will build up or burn down.

Reflection: Where did my words start a fire recently?

Practical Step: Go to one person you hurt with your words this month and make it right.

Let the Spirit Tame Your Tongue

James 3:7–12; Galatians 5:16, 22–23

Source: Commentary ③ — IV–V

No one can tame the tongue by effort. Depend on the Spirit moment by moment, and confess quickly when you stumble.

Reflection: Do I bless God and curse people with the same mouth?

Practical Step: For one week, pray Psalm 141:3 each morning: “Set a guard, O Lord, over my mouth.”

Seek Wisdom from Above

James 3:13–18; James 1:5

Source: Commentary ③ — VI–VIII

True wisdom is gentle, pure and peaceable, and it is shown by how we live. Ask God for it, and sow peace.

Reflection: Is there bitter jealousy or selfish ambition in my heart?

Practical Step: Choose one relationship where there is tension and take one step to make peace this week.
