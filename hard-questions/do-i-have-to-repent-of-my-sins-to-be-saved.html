50 Hard Questions · Question 17 of 50

Do I Have to Repent of My Sins to Be Saved?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

No. Believing in Jesus is the one condition for eternal life. Whether repentance is understood as a change of mind that comes with believing, or as turning from sin for fellowship with God, it is not an added condition.

Key versesJohn 3:16 · Acts 16:31 · John 20:31

This is one of the most sharply contested questions between the Free Grace and Reformed/Lordship traditions, and the Free Grace answer requires precision rather than a simple yes or no, because the word “repent” carries more than one biblical meaning. The New Testament uses metanoia (a change of mind) most fundamentally, and Scripture’s plainest statements of the condition for eternal life name believing, not a separate act of turning from sin, as what secures it: “whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life” (John 3:16); “believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and you will be saved” (Acts 16:31).

Strikingly, the Gospel of John — the one New Testament book that explicitly states its own purpose as evangelistic, “that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in His name” (John 20:31) — never once uses the word “repent” or “repentance” in that purpose statement or anywhere in its evangelistic appeals, using instead some form of “believe” roughly one hundred times. Free Grace teachers explain this pattern in two ways, and both reach the same conclusion. Some, following Lewis Sperry Chafer and Charles Ryrie, understand repentance in a salvation context as a change of mind that happens as a person believes — not a separate step added to faith.1 Others, following Zane Hodges and Robert Wilkin, understand repentance as turning from one’s sins: a matter of fellowship and harmony with God, and of escaping His temporal discipline, never a condition for receiving eternal life.2 On either reading, the one condition Jesus states for eternal life is believing in Him.

This does not mean the word “repent” never refers to turning from sin elsewhere in Scripture — it plainly does, especially in calls to Israel’s national repentance (Acts 3:19) and in the ongoing discipleship of believers confessing specific sins (1 John 1:9; Revelation 2:5, addressed to a church already saved). On either reading the Free Grace distinction holds: whatever change of mind accompanies believing belongs to the moment of faith itself, while turning from particular sins belongs to the ongoing life of discipleship and fellowship that follows salvation, not to the single condition — believe — that secures it.

Why does this distinction matter practically? Because if turning from particular sins were a condition for receiving eternal life, assurance would immediately become uncertain — how much turning, and how consistently maintained, would be enough? The gospel invitation as Scripture states it removes that uncertainty by naming a single, clear, completable condition: “he who believes in the Son has everlasting life” (John 3:36). This is also the point on which Reformed and Lordship theology differ most explicitly from the Free Grace position, treating repentance from sin as integral to saving faith itself — a genuine and significant disagreement not resolved in this article.

None of this diminishes the seriousness of sin or the necessity of a transformed life following genuine faith; Scripture expects exactly that transformation as the fruit of salvation (Ephesians 2:10; 2 Corinthians 5:17). It simply insists that the fruit not be relocated to the root — the sole condition for receiving eternal life is believing in Jesus Christ, and turning from sin is the Spirit’s ongoing work in the life that follows.
