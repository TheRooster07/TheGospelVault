What Does It Mean to Walk in the Spirit — the Spirit-Filled Life?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Walking in the Spirit is moment-by-moment dependence on Him rather than on the flesh. It concerns fellowship and growth, not the security of salvation.

Key versesGalatians 5:16 · Ephesians 5:18 · 1 John 1:9

Paul commands believers, “walk in the Spirit, and you shall not fulfill the lust of the flesh” (Galatians 5:16), and separately, “be filled with the Spirit” (Ephesians 5:18) — both commands addressed to people already indwelt by the Spirit permanently at salvation (see Q45, “What Is the Role of the Holy Spirit in a Believer’s Life?”), describing not a one-time event to be sought but an ongoing, repeatable, moment-by-moment posture of yielding practical control of one’s choices, thoughts, and reactions to the Spirit’s influence rather than to the competing pull of the sinful nature Scripture calls “the flesh.”

Charles Ryrie’s treatment of the Spirit-filled life describes this as fundamentally a matter of daily, continuous surrender and dependence rather than a dramatic, once-for-all experience separate from ordinary sanctification — the Greek verb tense in Ephesians 5:18 indicates an ongoing, repeated filling, not a single completed event, which is why Paul can command it as a continuous practice rather than describing it as an achievement already secured.1 This is consistent with the Free Grace distinction (see Q22, “What Is the Difference Between Salvation and Discipleship?”) between the believer’s permanent, unconditional standing in Christ and the believer’s variable, day-to-day walk and fellowship with Him — being filled with the Spirit belongs entirely to the second category, a matter of present experience and discipleship rather than of salvation’s security.

Paul describes the visible result of this Spirit-directed life as “fruit” rather than as a list of achieved behaviors: “love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control” (Galatians 5:22–23) — an organic outgrowth of the Spirit’s ongoing work rather than a checklist accomplished through sheer willpower or self-effort, which is precisely why Paul frames the entire passage as a contrast between “walking in the Spirit” and human effort exerted through “the flesh” (Galatians 5:17–18), even when that effort is aimed at good behavior.

This does not mean the Spirit-filled life is passive or requires no effort from the believer; Scripture consistently pairs dependence on the Spirit with active, deliberate choices — putting off specific sinful patterns and putting on specific godly ones (Ephesians 4:22–24), setting the mind on “things above” rather than earthly things (Colossians 3:2), and actively resisting temptation (James 4:7). The Spirit-filled life is neither self-effort alone nor passive waiting alone, but active cooperation with the Spirit’s ongoing work — yielding control repeatedly, especially in the specific moments temptation or frustration arise, rather than relying on human willpower to produce what only the Spirit’s influence can actually sustain.

Practically, walking in the Spirit means a believer who stumbles — as every believer does — is never thereby disqualified or newly at risk of losing what grace has already secured (see Q20, “Can a Genuine Christian Lose Their Salvation?”); it means instead confessing the specific failure honestly (1 John 1:9), yielding control afresh in that moment, and continuing the same daily, repeated dependence Scripture describes as the ordinary, expected shape of the Christian life rather than an advanced achievement reserved for exceptionally disciplined believers.
