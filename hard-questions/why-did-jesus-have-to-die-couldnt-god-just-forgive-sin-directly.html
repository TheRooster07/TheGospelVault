50 Hard Questions · Question 12 of 50

Why Did Jesus Have to Die — Couldn’t God Just Forgive Sin Directly?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

God’s justice could not simply overlook sin, so He paid its penalty Himself. At the cross, justice and mercy were both fully satisfied.

Key versesRomans 3:25–26 · 2 Corinthians 5:21 · Romans 5:8

This question assumes forgiveness is a simple matter of God’s willingness to overlook wrongdoing, the way a person might simply decide to let an offense go. Scripture presents something more demanding: God is not only loving but perfectly just, and His justice cannot simply set aside sin without addressing it, or it would cease to be justice at all. “The soul who sins shall die” (Ezekiel 18:20) is not an arbitrary threat but a statement about the actual moral weight of sin against an infinitely holy God.

Scripture’s answer is that God did not choose between justice and mercy — He satisfied both simultaneously at the cross. “God set forth [Christ] as a propitiation by His blood, through faith, to demonstrate His righteousness ... that He might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus” (Romans 3:25–26). Propitiation means a wrath-satisfying sacrifice: the penalty sin actually deserved was not waived but paid, in full, by a substitute qualified to pay it. “He made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in Him” (2 Corinthians 5:21). This is why the cross was necessary rather than one option among several God might have chosen — a debt owed cannot be forgiven by pretending it was never incurred; it must be paid, by someone.

Why did the payment require the death of the eternal Son of God specifically, rather than any other sacrifice? Because the penalty for sin against an infinite, holy God is itself infinite in weight, and “it is not possible that the blood of bulls and goats could take away sins” (Hebrews 10:4) — animal sacrifices under the Old Covenant pointed forward to, but could never themselves accomplish, a sufficient payment. Only a sacrifice of infinite worth could satisfy an infinite debt, which is why the substitute had to be nothing less than God Himself, and why He also had to be fully human to represent humanity and to actually die a real death on humanity’s behalf (Hebrews 2:14–17).

This is also why the cross displays God’s love and God’s justice together rather than one at the expense of the other. “God demonstrates His own love toward us, in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us” (Romans 5:8). It would not have been loving for God to overlook sin at the cost of His own justice, since a God who is not fully just could not be fully trusted; and it would not have been just for God to punish sin without also providing, at infinite cost to Himself, a way of escape. The cross is not a compromise between these two attributes; it is their full, simultaneous satisfaction.

The practical upshot for the individual believer is enormous: forgiveness offered through the cross is not God pretending sin does not matter. It is God declaring, in the clearest possible terms, that sin matters so much that only His own Son’s death could deal with it adequately — and that the debt has already, actually, completely been paid for anyone who will simply receive it by faith (Colossians 2:13–14).
