Why Does God Allow Evil and Suffering?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

God is not the author of evil; it entered through Adam’s sin. God permits it for a time, has already defeated it at the cross, and will end it when Christ returns.

Key versesJames 1:13 · Romans 8:18 · Revelation 21:4

No question is raised more often than this one. A parent loses a child, a tyrant murders millions, and the believer is left asking how a perfectly good, perfectly powerful God permits what He could prevent. Scripture does not manage this with a formula — it devotes an entire book, Job, to sitting inside the question honestly rather than resolving it quickly.

The starting point must be clear: God is not the author of evil. “Let no one say when he is tempted, ‘I am tempted by God’; for God cannot be tempted by evil, nor does He Himself tempt anyone” (James 1:13). Evil entered through the choice of a real moral agent: Adam’s free, culpable disobedience in Genesis 3, from which Paul traces the entire history of sin and death — “through one man sin entered the world, and death through sin, and thus death spread to all men, because all sinned” (Romans 5:12). Evil has no origin in God’s own being; it enters through the creature’s culpable choice.1

Why would a good God create beings capable of choosing evil at all? Because love that cannot be withheld is not love. God made Adam and Eve genuine moral agents, and the possibility of the Fall was the necessary shadow cast by the reality of that gift — which does not make evil good; Scripture calls it an intrusion to be finally destroyed (1 Corinthians 15:26). Natural suffering, too, traces to the Fall’s effect on creation itself: “the creation was subjected to futility ... because the creation itself also will be delivered from the bondage of corruption” (Romans 8:20–21) — a temporary groaning aimed at future liberation, not left as the final word.

The cross is God’s own definitive answer to evil — not an explanation offered from a distance, but evil absorbed from the inside. The Son of God did not exempt Himself from the world’s pain; He bore betrayal, injustice, and agony, and turned it into the means of redemption (Acts 2:23; 1 Peter 2:24). For the believer, present suffering is never pointless even when its purpose is undisclosed: “I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us” (Romans 8:18), and “all things work together for good to those who love God” (Romans 8:28) — a promise about God’s use of hardship, not a claim that hardship is itself good.

Job never receives an explanation for his suffering; he receives instead an overwhelming encounter with the wisdom and freedom of God (Job 38–41), and that proves sufficient. Ultimately Scripture’s answer is eschatological: evil is permitted for a time, under a God who has already secured its final defeat and who will one day “wipe away every tear” (Revelation 21:4). The God who could have prevented suffering chose instead to conquer it from within, and has promised to finish the work at His return.
