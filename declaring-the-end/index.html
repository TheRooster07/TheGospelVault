Introduction

Thesis

The Bible stakes its claim to be God’s word on something no ordinary book would risk: public, checkable predictions of the future. God Himself presents fulfilled prophecy as evidence that He alone is God. Examined honestly — including the hardest cases and the strongest objections — that evidence holds. And because the God who has kept His word in history “cannot lie,” His promise of eternal life to everyone who believes in His Son can be known and trusted.

Most defenses of the faith begin with philosophy or with the resurrection. This paper begins where the prophet Isaiah begins: with a challenge. In the courtroom scenes of Isaiah 41–48, the LORD calls the idols of the nations to prove their divinity by declaring the future, and then meets the test Himself.1 The argument that follows has four parts: God’s own test (Section I); prophecies of named persons, nations and cities (Sections II–III); the prophecies concerning Israel and the Messiah (Sections IV–V); and an honest treatment of the objections, including a Reformed counterpoint (Sections VI–VII). It closes where Scripture closes the matter: with a promise (Section VIII).

Two cautions govern the whole paper. First, it aims to claim no more than the evidence shows; where a fulfillment is partial, debated or still future, it says so. Second, it does not treat evidence as the ground of salvation. Fulfilled prophecy can remove barriers and steady faith; eternal life is received by believing in Jesus Christ for it.

I. God’s Own Test

A. The Idols on Trial

Isaiah 41 opens a trial. “Present your case,” the Lord says. “Bring forward your strong arguments” (41:21). The test is not wealth, power or the number of worshipers, but knowledge of the future: “Declare the things that are going to come afterward, that we may know that you are gods” (41:23). The scene sharpens in chapter 46, where Babylon’s gods Bel and Nebo are loaded onto carts as baggage when the city falls (46:1–2). The gods that were meant to carry Babylon must be carried. By contrast the LORD says to Israel, “You who have been borne by Me from birth … even to your graying years I will bear you!” (46:3–4).

9“Remember the former things long past,

For I am God, and there is no other;

I am God, and there is no one like Me,

10Declaring the end from the beginning,

And from ancient times things which have not been done,

Saying, ‘My purpose will be established,

And I will accomplish all My good pleasure’;

— Isaiah 46:9–10 (NASB95)

God takes His own test. He declares “the end from the beginning” and gives His reason for doing so in advance: “so that you would not say, ‘My idol has done them’” (48:5). Prophecy is His signature on history. Bloom observes that God Himself thereby endorses fulfilled prophecy as an apologetic tool, and that this is precisely what distinguishes biblical faith from religions that make no testable claims about the future.2

B. The Prophet’s Standard

Israel received the same standard for anyone claiming to speak for God: “When a prophet speaks in the name of the Lord, if the thing does not come about or come true, that is the thing which the Lord has not spoken” (Deuteronomy 18:22). The standard is absolute. Micaiah staked his life on it before King Ahab: “If you indeed return safely the Lord has not spoken by me” (1 Kings 22:28). Ahab died that evening. Jeremiah applied it to the false prophet Hananiah, who died within the year as Jeremiah foretold (Jeremiah 28:9, 16–17). A book written to deceive does not hand its readers the test that could expose it.

C. A More Sure Word

Peter, an eyewitness of the transfiguration, nevertheless points his readers to “the prophetic word made more sure” (2 Peter 1:19) and explains its source: “no prophecy was ever made by an act of human will, but men moved by the Holy Spirit spoke from God” (1:21). Fulfilled prophecy is not what makes Scripture God’s word. It is one of the ways God has shown that it is.3 Jesus used it the same way with His disciples: “I am telling you before it comes to pass, so that when it does occur, you may believe that I am He” (John 13:19).

II. Named Before They Were Born

Some prophecies are general. The strongest are specific enough to be falsified. Two name persons not yet born.4

A. Josiah

While Jeroboam I stood at his rival altar at Bethel in the late tenth century BC, a man of God from Judah cried out: “Behold, a son shall be born to the house of David, Josiah by name; and on you he shall sacrifice the priests of the high places who burn incense on you, and human bones shall be burned on you” (1 Kings 13:2). About three centuries later Josiah broke down that altar, took bones from the nearby graves and burned them on it, “according to the word of the Lord which the man of God proclaimed” (2 Kings 23:16).

B. Cyrus

28“It is I who says of Cyrus, ‘He is My shepherd!

And he will perform all My desire.’

And he declares of Jerusalem, ‘She will be built,’

And of the temple, ‘Your foundation will be laid.’”

— Isaiah 44:28 (NASB95)

Isaiah prophesied in the eighth century BC, when Assyria dominated the region.5 He names Cyrus, calls him the LORD’s shepherd and “anointed” (45:1), and says he will declare that Jerusalem and the temple will be rebuilt. Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BC and in his first year authorized the Jews to return and rebuild the house of the LORD (Ezra 1:1–3). A clay cylinder from his reign records his policy of returning deported peoples and their sanctuaries; it does not name the Jews, but it matches the policy Ezra describes.6 The text states the purpose of the naming: “so that you may know that it is I, the Lord, the God of Israel, who calls you by your name” (Isaiah 45:3).

C. Why Specificity Matters

Ancient oracles were often unfalsifiable. Herodotus tells of King Croesus, who was told that if he attacked Persia a great empire would fall; one did, and it was his own. The prophecies of Josiah and Cyrus are the opposite: they name a person, a lineage, a place, a task and a result. Bloom notes that some biblical prophecies were simply too risky for a later editor to have invented.7

III. Empires and Cities

A. The Succession of Empires

Daniel interpreted Nebuchadnezzar’s dream of a statue of gold, silver, bronze and iron as a succession of kingdoms beginning with Babylon (“You are the head of gold,” Daniel 2:38) and ending with a kingdom God will set up that “will never be destroyed” (2:44). The book itself identifies the next two: Babylon falls to “the Medes and Persians” (5:28), and Gabriel names “the kings of Media and Persia” and “the kingdom of Greece,” whose “first king” is broken and succeeded by four kingdoms “not with his power” (8:20–22). Conservative interpreters identify the four empires as Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome.8

20The ram which you saw with the two horns represents the kings of Media and Persia.

21The shaggy goat represents the kingdom of Greece, and the large horn that is between his eyes is the first king.

22The broken horn and the four horns that arose in its place represent four kingdoms which will arise from his nation, although not with his power.

— Daniel 8:20–22 (NASB95)

History followed the sequence. Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BC; Alexander overthrew Persia in the 330s BC, moving with a speed captured in the image of a goat that crossed the earth “without touching the ground” (8:5); he died in 323 BC, and his empire settled into four kingdoms under his generals; Rome absorbed them. Daniel dates the vision to the third year of Belshazzar (8:1), more than two centuries before Alexander’s birth. The critical dating of Daniel is addressed in Section VI.9

The fulfilled parts of the dream also carry an argument for the unfulfilled part. Daniel concludes, “the dream is true and its interpretation is trustworthy” (2:45). Dispensational interpreters understand the stone that fills the earth as Christ’s kingdom established at His return.10

B. Nineveh, Babylon and Tyre

Nahum foretold the fall of Assyria’s capital: “Nineveh is devastated! Who will grieve for her?” (Nahum 3:7). It fell to Babylonians and Medes in 612 BC and lay as mounds until nineteenth-century excavations. Isaiah and Jeremiah foretold Babylon’s ruin (Isaiah 13:19–20; Jeremiah 51:37). Honesty requires noting that Babylon fell in 539 BC without destruction and declined over centuries before it was abandoned; because Isaiah 13 speaks of “the day of the Lord” (13:6, 9), many dispensational interpreters anticipate a further fulfillment.

Ezekiel 26 is the most detailed city prophecy and the one most often challenged. Ezekiel announces that “many nations” will come against Tyre “as the sea brings up its waves” (26:3). The first wave is Nebuchadnezzar (“he,” 26:7–11), who destroyed the mainland city in a thirteen-year siege but did not take the island; Ezekiel himself records that he received “no wages from Tyre” (29:18). The pronoun then shifts to “they” (26:12): “they will … throw your stones and your timbers and your debris into the water.” In 332 BC Alexander did exactly that, scraping the ruins of the mainland city into the sea to build a causeway to the island. The hardest phrase, “you will be built no more” (26:14), is treated in Section VI.11

C. Jerusalem and the Temple

Jesus foretold the siege and destruction of Jerusalem in specific terms: a barricade, encirclement, “they will not leave in you one stone upon another” (Luke 19:43–44; cf. Matthew 24:2), armies surrounding the city, and a command to flee (Luke 21:20–21). In AD 70 Titus besieged and destroyed the city and the temple, as Josephus, an eyewitness, describes.12 Eusebius records that the Jerusalem believers fled beforehand to Pella.13 Jesus added that “Jerusalem will be trampled under foot by the Gentiles until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled” (Luke 21:24), a phrase that looks beyond AD 70 to God’s purposes for Israel.

IV. Israel: Scattered, Preserved and Regathered

A. Scattered

Before Israel entered the land, Moses foretold its dispersion: “the Lord will scatter you among all peoples, from one end of the earth to the other end of the earth” (Deuteronomy 28:64), where it would find “no resting place” (28:65). Assyria (722 BC), Babylon (586 BC) and Rome (AD 70 and 135) fulfilled those words, and the Jewish people lived dispersed for nearly nineteen centuries, repeatedly expelled (England in 1290, Spain in 1492) and, in the Holocaust, targeted for annihilation. Hosea described the long condition precisely: “many days without king or prince, without sacrifice or sacred pillar and without ephod or household idols,” followed by a return (Hosea 3:4–5).

B. Preserved

35Thus says the Lord,

Who gives the sun for light by day

And the fixed order of the moon and the stars for light by night,

Who stirs up the sea so that its waves roar;

The Lord of hosts is His name:

36“If this fixed order departs

From before Me,” declares the Lord,

“Then the offspring of Israel also will cease

From being a nation before Me forever.”

37Thus says the Lord,

“If the heavens above can be measured

And the foundations of the earth searched out below,

Then I will also cast off all the offspring of Israel

For all that they have done,” declares the Lord.

— Jeremiah 31:35–37 (NASB95)

God promised that Israel would not be destroyed (Leviticus 26:44) and that the nations which scattered it would be (Jeremiah 30:11). Assyria, Babylon, Philistia, Edom, Moab and Ammon have disappeared as peoples; Israel has not. The promise rests on God’s unconditional covenant with Abraham, ratified by God alone passing between the pieces (Genesis 15:17–18), and it holds “for all that they have done” (Jeremiah 31:37).14 Paul draws the conclusion: “God has not rejected His people whom He foreknew” (Romans 11:2). Notably, the people scattered across the world preserved the very Scriptures that foretold their scattering; they were “entrusted with the oracles of God” (Romans 3:2).

C. Regathered

The prophets foretold a worldwide regathering, “the second time … from the four corners of the earth” (Isaiah 11:11–12), into one nation in the land (Ezekiel 37:21–22), never again to be uprooted (Amos 9:15), for the sake of God’s name (Ezekiel 36:22). Return began in the late nineteenth century; the State of Israel was declared on May 14, 1948; Jerusalem came under Jewish control in 1967; Hebrew was revived as a national language. Dispensational interpreters regard this as a stage, not the final fulfillment: in Ezekiel 37 the bones come together before the breath enters (37:8–10), and Israel’s national turning to the Messiah remains future (Zechariah 12:10; Romans 11:26).15 This paper therefore makes no claim that any modern government is beyond criticism; it claims only that scattering, preservation and regathering have occurred as foretold.16

V. The Messiah at the Center

Jesus told His disciples that “all things which are written about Me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled” (Luke 24:44). The messianic prophecies are treated fully in other Gospel Vault papers; a summary suffices here to show that independent lines, written centuries apart, converge on one Person:17

VI. Answering the Objections

A. A Fair Test

Believers are to give a defense “with gentleness and reverence” (1 Peter 3:15) and to examine claims as the Bereans did (Acts 17:11). A fair test cuts both ways: believers should not overclaim, and critics should not exclude predictive prophecy before examining it. Much critical dating begins from the assumption that prediction is impossible and concludes that accurate prophecies must be later than the events. That is a premise, not a finding.18

B. “Written After the Fact”

Daniel. Critical scholarship dates Daniel to about 165 BC, a view traceable to the third-century critic Porphyry. Against it: copies of Daniel were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls and treated as Scripture; Jesus called Daniel a prophet (Matthew 24:15); and even a second-century date leaves the iron kingdom (Rome) and the Messiah’s death before the sanctuary’s destruction (Daniel 9:26; AD 70) as genuine predictions.19

Isaiah. John attributes both Isaiah 53 and Isaiah 6 to the one prophet (John 12:38–41), and the complete Isaiah scroll from Qumran presents the book as a single work. Josephus records a tradition that Cyrus read Isaiah’s prophecy about himself; whatever its historical value, it shows that first-century Jews regarded the prophecy as prior to Cyrus.20

Jesus and AD 70. Acts ends with Paul alive in Rome, preaching “unhindered” (Acts 28:30–31), about AD 62, with no mention of the deaths of Paul or Peter or the fall of Jerusalem; Luke preceded Acts (Acts 1:1).21 Finally, the prophecies of Israel’s dispersion, preservation and regathering were fulfilled over the two millennia after every proposed date of composition.

C. “Vague,” “Self-Fulfilled”

The prophecies examined here name persons, nations, places, sequences and timing; they could have failed. Nor were they self-fulfilled: Alexander built his causeway to take Tyre, not to fulfill Ezekiel; Titus did not destroy the temple to fulfill Jesus’ words (Josephus even reports that Titus wished to spare it); and no one arranges his own birthplace or the actions of the soldiers who crucify him (Luke 2:1–7; John 19:23–24).

D. “Some Prophecies Failed”

Bloom concedes that it is “no simple matter to refute the charge that Ezekiel’s prophecy failed” until the whole history is considered.22 Considered whole, the prophecy announced waves of nations (26:3), not one conqueror; the proud, independent sea power of Ezekiel’s day was never restored; much of the ancient site lies in ruins where nets have been spread; and Ezekiel’s own admission (29:18) testifies to the prophet’s candor rather than to failure. Two further principles apply. Some prophecies of judgment carry an implied condition of repentance (Jeremiah 18:7–8; Jonah 3:10). And “not yet fulfilled” is not “failed”: the stone kingdom, Israel’s national turning and the Messiah’s reign remain future.

E. A Caution Against Overclaiming

Popular probability figures, such as those of Peter Stoner, assign vast odds against chance fulfillment, but their inputs are debatable and they often backfire with educated hearers.23 Honesty also requires distinguishing pattern from prediction (Hosea 11:1 in Matthew 2:15) and acknowledging textual questions: at Psalm 22:16 the NASB’s “They pierced my hands and my feet” follows the Septuagint and a Dead Sea manuscript, while the Masoretic Text reads differently.24 The cumulative case does not depend on any one disputed text.

VII. A Reformed Counterpoint

Many Reformed theologians, especially in the presuppositional tradition, hold that evidence cannot serve as a neutral foundation for faith: Scripture is self-attesting and is received as God’s word through the inner testimony of the Holy Spirit. Covenant theology also tends to read Daniel’s seventieth week and the Olivet Discourse as fulfilled in the first century and many promises to Israel as fulfilled in the church.25

This paper affirms that the Spirit alone persuades the heart. It nevertheless holds, first, that Scripture itself presents fulfilled prophecy as evidence (“so that you may know,” Isaiah 45:3; “so that … you may believe,” John 13:19; Paul “reasoned … explaining and giving evidence,” Acts 17:2–3); and second, that the literal fulfillment of past prophecy — real kings, empires, cities, a real dispersion and regathering — gives good reason to expect the literal fulfillment of prophecies still future.26 For that future, see the Vault 5 study “God’s Prophetical Program for the Ages.”

VIII. Conclusion: God, Who Cannot Lie, Promised

2in the hope of eternal life, which God, who cannot lie, promised long ages ago,

— Titus 1:2 (NASB95)

Paul anchors his letter to Titus in the character of a God who is apseudēs, “without falsehood.” Moses said the same: “God is not a man, that He should lie … Has He said, and will He not do it?” (Numbers 23:19), and Hebrews calls it “impossible for God to lie” (Hebrews 6:18). Every fulfilled prophecy examined in this paper is a witness to that truth.

To that truth Paul attaches “the hope of eternal life,” promised before the ages began. John states the same promise in terms of testimony: “the testimony of God is this … that God has given us eternal life, and this life is in His Son” (1 John 5:9, 11), written “so that you may know that you have eternal life” (5:13). Jesus spoke of it as a present possession: “he who hears My word, and believes Him who sent Me, has eternal life, and does not come into judgment, but has passed out of death into life” (John 5:24).27 Assurance is not presumption; it is taking at His word a God whose word has never failed.28

The evidence of fulfilled prophecy is not an end in itself. It points to the One who spoke, and to the promise He has made. The God who declared the end from the beginning has declared what He gives to everyone who believes in His Son. Because He cannot lie, that promise can be trusted, and known.

Lord, thank You.
