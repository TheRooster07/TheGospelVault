How Should Christians Handle Doubt?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Doubt is common among believers, and Jesus meets it with patience. Bring it to God and to His Word, and rest on His promise that no one can snatch His sheep from His hand.

Key versesMark 9:24 · John 20:29 · John 10:28

Doubt is a common experience even among sincere, genuine believers, and Scripture treats it with far more patience and honesty than the popular assumption that doubt is simply a failure of faith to be hidden or suppressed. Thomas, doubting the resurrection despite the other disciples’ testimony, is not condemned by Jesus but is met directly, given the evidence he asked for, and gently redirected: “Because you have seen Me, you have believed. Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed” (John 20:29) — a correction offered with evident tenderness, not rejection.

It is worth distinguishing (as Q19, “How Can I Know I’m Truly Saved?” does) between honest intellectual or emotional doubt and settled unbelief. The father of a demon-possessed boy cries out to Jesus, “Lord, I believe; help my unbelief!” (Mark 9:24) — holding genuine faith and genuine struggle together in a single breath, a combination Jesus honors by healing the boy rather than first demanding the doubt be fully resolved. This models a posture available to every struggling believer: faith does not require the absence of every question, only continued trust in Christ even while wrestling honestly with what remains unclear.

James does address one particular kind of doubt with real seriousness — the double-mindedness of a person who asks God for wisdom while refusing to actually trust Him for it, “like a wave of the sea driven and tossed by the wind” (James 1:6–8) — but this passage addresses a specific, unstable refusal to commit to trusting God at all, not the ordinary experience of a believer working through difficult questions about theology, suffering, or God’s purposes while remaining anchored in Christ.

Scripture’s own remedy for doubt is consistently active rather than passive: returning to the truth already revealed, rather than waiting for doubt to dissipate on its own or demanding new, additional certainty before continuing to trust. The psalmist, in the midst of genuine spiritual distress, deliberately rehearses what he already knows to be true: “Why are you cast down, O my soul? ... Hope in God, for I shall yet praise Him” (Psalm 42:11) — addressing his own doubting soul with settled truth rather than simply describing his feelings and stopping there. Jude similarly instructs believers to actively “have compassion, making a distinction” toward others who are doubting, extending patience rather than pressure (Jude 22).

For the believer troubled by doubt about any of the doctrines in this paper — including doubt about one’s own salvation (see Q19, “How Can I Know I’m Truly Saved?”) — the consistent biblical pattern is neither to suppress the doubt with false certainty nor to treat it as evidence of spiritual failure, but to bring it honestly to God in prayer and to Scripture for answers, trusting the Lord’s own promise — “I give them eternal life, and they shall never perish; neither shall anyone snatch them out of My hand” (John 10:28) — even through, and sometimes especially through, seasons of real struggle.
