50 Hard Questions · Question 50 of 50

How Can I Grow Spiritually as a Believer?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Growth comes through ordinary means — Scripture, prayer, the local church, confession, and using your gifts — empowered by the Spirit. It never secures salvation; it flows from it.

Key verses1 Peter 2:2 · 2 Peter 3:18 · Philippians 2:12–13

Spiritual growth, or sanctification, is the lifelong process by which a believer becomes progressively more conformed to the character of Christ following salvation — entirely distinct from the instantaneous, complete gift of eternal life, which requires no growth or maturation to be fully and permanently possessed the moment a person believes. Growth is not what secures a believer’s standing before God; it is what a secure believer is invited into as the grateful, Spirit-empowered response to a salvation already fully received.

Scripture identifies specific, ordinary means by which this growth occurs, none of them mysterious or reserved for spiritually elite believers. Scripture itself is the primary instrument: “as newborn babes, desire the pure milk of the word, that you may grow thereby” (1 Peter 2:2); Peter’s closing exhortation names the goal directly — “grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ” (2 Peter 3:18). Prayer (see Q4, “How Should Christians Think About Unanswered Prayer?”) is a second consistent means, alongside fellowship with other believers within the local church (see Q48, “Why Do I Need to Go to Church if I Already Believe?”), since Scripture never envisions spiritual growth occurring in isolation from other Christians who teach, encourage, and hold a believer accountable.

Growth is also consistently described in Scripture as the Spirit’s own work rather than something achieved through willpower or human effort alone, even though it requires the believer’s active cooperation. Paul’s own description of his ministry aim — presenting “every man perfect in Christ Jesus,” for which he says, “I also labor, striving according to His working which works in me mightily” (Colossians 1:28–29) — holds both realities together: real human effort and striving, entirely empowered by God’s own work within, neither canceling out the other. Philippians 2:12–13 states the same pairing even more directly: “work out your own salvation with fear and trembling; for it is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure.”

Practically, spiritual growth tends to follow a recognizable, ordinary pattern rather than requiring dramatic experiences: regular intake of Scripture, consistent prayer, active involvement in a local church, the practice of confessing specific sin honestly and quickly (1 John 1:9) rather than allowing it to accumulate unaddressed, and the exercise of whatever spiritual gift God has given (see Q47, “What Are Spiritual Gifts, and Do They Still Operate Today?”) in genuine service to others. Growth is also, importantly, uneven and nonlinear in every believer’s actual experience — Scripture never promises steady, unbroken upward progress, and seasons of apparent stagnation or even regression do not indicate a loss of the salvation already secured (see Q20, “Can a Genuine Christian Lose Their Salvation?”), only a need to return again to these same ordinary means of grace.

The ultimate aim of all spiritual growth, Scripture is clear, is not the believer’s own private improvement as an end in itself, but Christlikeness for the sake of God’s own glory and the good of others: “whom He foreknew, He also predestined to be conformed to the image of His Son” (Romans 8:29). This is the fitting note on which this paper’s whole survey of hard questions closes — not a program to complete or a performance to maintain, but a gracious, secure relationship with a God who has already given everything necessary for both salvation and growth, and who is “faithful, who also will do it” (1 Thessalonians 5:24).
