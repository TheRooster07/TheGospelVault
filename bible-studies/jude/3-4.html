Jude 3–4 · Small Group Bible Study

The Battle Cry: Contend Earnestly for the Faith

Source: The Gospel Vault

Download PDF Leader / Learner Guide

① The Biblical Text

NASB · Jude 3–4

3 Beloved, while I was making every effort to write you about our common salvation, I felt the necessity to write to you appealing that you contend earnestly for the faith which was once for all handed down to the saints.

4 For certain persons have crept in unnoticed, those who were long beforehand marked out for this condemnation, ungodly persons who turn the grace of our God into licentiousness and deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ.

② Devotional

Personal preparation · All group members

"Beloved, while I was making every effort to write you about our common salvation, I felt the necessity to write to you appealing that you contend earnestly for the faith which was once for all handed down to the saints."

Jude had one letter in his heart and another on his desk. He wanted to write about the salvation every believer shares. Instead, something forced his hand: people were turning God’s grace into a license to do as they pleased, and the faith itself was being altered.

Notice that his call to fight comes out of love, not out of a love of argument. You contend for what you treasure. The faith was handed down to you — you did not invent it, and you are not free to improve it. Your task is to receive it, believe it, and hand it on whole.

That is a calming thought as much as a challenging one. You are not the author of the gospel. You are its steward, and the One who gave it is the One who keeps you.

Reflect

  1. What is the “common salvation” you most want to protect in your own church or family?
  2. Where have you been tempted to soften or adjust the gospel so that it feels easier to hand on?
  3. What would it look like for you to contend for the faith with love instead of with heat?

The faith was handed down once. Our job is to hand it on whole.

③ Commentary

Teacher preparation resource · Full text organized under outline headings

PropositionJude set aside the letter he wanted to write — about our common salvation — to urge believers to contend earnestly for the faith once for all handed down, because men had slipped in who turned God’s grace into license and denied the Lord.

Introduction — The Letter Jude Did Not Write

One of the most revealing things about a person is what he almost said. Jude tells us he was “making every effort” to write a different letter. The Greek word, spoudē, means eagerness, even longing. He had that letter in his heart and the pen in his hand. It was to be about “our common salvation” — the shared life of grace that ties every believer to every other believer.

He did not write it. “I felt the necessity,” he says, and the word anankē means compulsion from outside himself. Paul uses the same word when he says “woe is me if I do not preach the gospel” (1 Corinthians 9:16). Jude was not choosing between a pleasant subject and an unpleasant one. A pastoral emergency made the first letter impossible to write in good conscience. He is like a doctor who stopped by for a routine visit and found a patient in crisis.

Every pastor and teacher knows the pull toward the warm sermon instead of the hard one. Jude shows how to answer it. He does not abandon the warm letter. He writes the urgent one first, and the common salvation he wanted to celebrate is the reason the urgent one exists.

I. The Change of Plan – v. 3a

A. Our common salvation

“Common” here does not mean ordinary. It means shared — held in common by every believer, whatever his background, education, or standing. It is the one possession no circumstance can create or take away, and it is why this letter is addressed to a whole community and not to a few experts.

The warmth in this phrase matters. Jude’s battle cry is not a departure from his love for the common salvation. It is the expression of it. You cannot love something and be indifferent when it is attacked.

B. The necessity

What forced the change is named in verse 4. Something was happening inside the church that could not wait. Jude’s response is not to panic and not to ignore it. He turns his readers from the warm subject to the urgent one — and he does it as a pastor who is still writing to people he calls “beloved.”

II. The Battle Cry – v. 3b

A. Contend earnestly

“Contend earnestly” is one Greek word, epagōnizesthai, found only here in the New Testament. Its root is agōn, the word for an athletic contest — a race or a wrestling match that demands everything on the day of the event. The prefix adds intensity. To epagōnizomai for something is to agonize for it, to pour yourself out for it as if the outcome depended on you.

That is not the language of casual theological preference. Jude is not asking his readers to hold their views with mild conviction and mention them politely if asked. He is saying: fight for this. But look at what the fight is for. You do not fight the faith; you fight for it. The contest is not against people — their judgment belongs to God, as the next section will show — but for the preservation and handing on of the apostolic deposit. A church that fights against people is consumed by conflict. A church that fights for the gospel is defined by its love of the gospel.

Paul uses the same family of words elsewhere. He tells the Philippians to be “striving together for the faith of the gospel” (Philippians 1:27) and tells Timothy to “fight the good fight of the faith” (1 Timothy 6:12). In each case the faith is something worth fighting for, and the fight is shared: not a solitary crusade, but a community standing shoulder to shoulder. Jude’s “contend” is a plural command to a whole church.

B. The faith once for all handed down

The object of the contending is “the faith which was once for all handed down to the saints.” Hapax means once for all, once only, never to be repeated. Paradotheisē is an aorist passive of paradidōmi: to hand over, to deliver, to entrust. The aorist points to a completed historical act. Someone handed this faith to the saints at a definite time, and that handing-down is finished. The deposit is closed.

This matters enormously. “The faith” here is the apostolic message — what the apostles taught about Jesus Christ and the gospel. It is a fixed content. We are receivers, not originators. The church’s relationship to the faith is that of trustee, not artist. We may explain the gospel in new words, illustrate it with new pictures, and apply it to new situations; that is faithful handing-on. What “once for all” forbids is altering the content: adding conditions to the promise, subtracting its comfort, or redirecting its focus from Christ to ourselves.

Paul says the same to Timothy: “Retain the standard of sound words which you have heard from me… Guard, through the Holy Spirit who dwells in us, the treasure which has been entrusted to you” (2 Timothy 1:13–14). And he tells Timothy to entrust what he has heard to faithful men who will teach others also (2 Timothy 2:2). The pattern is receive, guard, hand on. Galatians 1:8–9 shows how serious it is: if anyone, even an angel, preaches another gospel, “let him be accursed.” The faith is not up for revision.

A will that has been probated is a closed document. You cannot add heirs after the testator has died, and you cannot change the terms. The lawyer’s job is not to improve it but to preserve it and administer it faithfully. The church’s job with the apostolic faith is the same.

III. The Infiltrators – v. 4

A. They crept in unnoticed

Jude now names the crisis: “certain persons have crept in unnoticed.” Pareisedysan is a compound verb picturing someone slipping in alongside, through a side door, without detection. This is not an army on the horizon. It is infiltration. The enemy is already inside, at the table, using the right words.

That is one of Jude’s most uncomfortable insights. The most dangerous threats to the gospel rarely announce themselves. By the time the danger is visible, some of the damage has already been done. The community’s greatest strengths — its hospitality and openness — become the door. The answer is not to give up welcome but to pair it with wisdom: the table is for everyone, and discernment sits at the table too.

The New Testament remembers the same thing happening before. Paul speaks of “false brethren secretly brought in, who had sneaked in to spy out our liberty which we have in Christ Jesus, in order to bring us into bondage” (Galatians 2:4). The verb is from the same family as Jude’s pareisedysan. The aim was not simply to teach something different, but to take away liberty. Infiltration works because it looks like fellowship. It is the oldest strategy in the book, and the church has met it in every age.

B. Marked out long beforehand

The phrase “long beforehand marked out for this condemnation” translates progegrammenoi, “written about beforehand.” Some Reformed interpreters take this as eternal reprobation — these men were decreed from eternity to this end. The context points elsewhere. Jude immediately proves his point from the Old Testament (vv. 5–11): the judgment on this pattern of behavior was written about long ago, in the stories of the wilderness generation, the fallen angels, Sodom, Cain, Balaam, and Korah. The “long beforehand” is the record of Scripture, not a secret decree. Jude does not tell us whether these men were ever believers, and nothing in his argument depends on deciding. He tells us what they do and where it leads.

C. Two charges: license and denial

Jude gives two descriptions. First, they “turn the grace of our God into licentiousness.” Aselgeia is unrestrained, shameless self-indulgence. These men took the most freeing truth in the universe and used it as permission for the very thing grace was meant to free people from. Grace rightly understood is not the removal of the fence at the cliff edge; it is the gift of eyes to see the cliff and the strength to walk away from it (Titus 2:11–12).

Paul meets this error head on. “Are we to continue in sin so that grace may increase? May it never be!” (Romans 6:1–2). “Shall we sin because we are not under law but under grace? May it never be!” (Romans 6:15). Paul does not answer the objection by shrinking grace. He answers it by showing what grace does: it breaks the old master’s hold, and it gives us a new Master. Jude is making the same argument from the other side. Those who turn grace into license have not understood what grace is. And notice that even Paul’s critics accused him of teaching this (Romans 3:8). If your gospel can’t be mistaken for license, it is not the gospel Paul preached.

Second, they “deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ.” Whether the denial is spoken or lived, it is the logical partner of the first. If grace is license, there is no Lord to whom the believer answers. Together the two errors empty the faith of its Master.

This is the point at which Free Grace is most often misunderstood — and Jude helps. Critics such as John MacArthur argue that a gospel which makes eternal life a free gift without regard to obedience is the very license Jude condemns.1 Free Grace replies that Jude condemns using grace as an excuse for sin, not the free offer of eternal life by faith alone, and that the answer to license is not to add works to the condition of salvation but to teach what grace itself teaches.2 Jude’s own remedy confirms this: he will not call believers to qualify for the keeping of verse 1 but to build up, pray, and keep themselves in God’s love (vv. 20–21).

One pastoral caution. Jude’s description should not produce a church that sees an infiltrator behind every hard question. The marks he gives are specific: turning grace into license and denying the Lord. Those are not the marks of sincere doubt or honest struggle. Discernment, not suspicion, is the response he asks for.

Conclusion

The battle cry of verse 3 is not the cry of a man who loves a fight. It is the cry of a man who loves the common salvation and could not write about it in peace because it was being taken from the people he loved. The urgency to contend comes from love, not from love of argument.

The faith was handed down once. It is not ours to modify, soften, or improve. Our task is to receive it, believe it, and — when necessary, and it is often necessary — to fight for it. Not with bitterness or contempt, but with the full effort the word epagōnizesthai demands. What is at stake is not our theological reputation. It is the common salvation, the one thing every person in every room needs.

Lord, give us love for the faith, and wisdom to guard it.

⑤ Application

Closes the study · Personal & corporate

Application 1 — Love What You Defend

Jude 3a

Drawn from Commentary Introduction and I. Key terms: spoudēn (eagerness), koinēs (common).

Jude fights because he loves. The common salvation was what he wanted to write about, and its endangerment is what made him pick up the pen. If we contend for the faith without loving it, the contending turns into mere argument.

Reflection: When you stand up for the gospel, do the people with you sense that you love it, or that you love being right?

Practical Step: Before your next doctrinal conversation, tell the person one thing about the gospel you are glad of.

Application 2 — Receive It and Hand It On Whole

Jude 3b

Drawn from Commentary II.B. Key terms: hapax (once for all), paradotheisē (handed down).

We are trustees. The apostolic faith does not need our improvements, only our faithful handing-on. New words, new pictures, and new applications are welcome; changing the content is not.

Reflection: Where have you been tempted to soften or adjust the gospel to make it easier to hand on?

Practical Step: Write the gospel in three sentences, using only what the apostles taught. Compare it with how you actually explain it to others.

Application 3 — Recognize Quiet Infiltration

Jude 4

Drawn from Commentary III.A–B. Key term: pareisedysan (crept in unnoticed).

Dangerous teaching seldom arrives announced. It arrives at the table, using the right language. The remedy is not suspicion, but steady familiarity with the apostolic faith — so that a departure from it is noticed early.

Reflection: Which voices — books, podcasts, teachers — are shaping you most? Have you tested them against the apostolic message?

Practical Step: Read the one-page summary of the gospel in 1 Corinthians 15:1–4 each day this week and compare it with what you are hearing.

Application 4 — Answer License with Grace, Not with Works

Jude 4; Titus 2:11–12

Drawn from Commentary III.C. FGR: Hodges, Absolutely Free!; RTR: MacArthur, The Gospel According to Jesus.

Grace can be abused, and Jude says so plainly. But the cure for license is not to turn grace into a wage. Grace itself teaches us to deny ungodliness. A church that keeps both — grace as free gift, and grace as teacher — honors Jude’s whole argument.

Reflection: Where are you tempted to use grace as an excuse? Where are you tempted to add rules to protect it?

Practical Step: Read Titus 2:11–14 slowly. Write down what grace is said to do in our lives, and pray it back as a request.
