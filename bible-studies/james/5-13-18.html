Prayer for Every Season

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

Source: The Gospel Vault · Developed from teaching outlines by Dr. C. Norman Sellers, ThD, used by permission

① The Biblical Text

James 5:13–18 · New American Standard Bible (NASB95)

13 Is anyone among you suffering? Then he must pray. Is anyone cheerful? He is to sing praises.

14 Is anyone among you sick? Then he must call for the elders of the church and they are to pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord;

② Devotional

“The effective prayer of a righteous man can accomplish much.” — James 5:16

James ends his letter where he began, with prayer. Are you suffering? Pray. Are you cheerful? Sing. Are you sick? Call the elders, and let them pray. Is there sin between you and a brother? Confess it, and pray for one another. Every season of life has its own kind of prayer.

Prayer is not a last resort for the desperate. It is the first resource of every believer. God is able to do far more than we ask or think, and He invites us to come to Him boldly for grace in time of need.

And James reminds us that the great prayer warriors of the Bible were ordinary people. Elijah was a man with a nature like ours. He was afraid at times, and discouraged. But he prayed, and heaven answered. The same God hears the prayers of His children today.

Reflection Questions

  1. What season am I in right now, and how should I be praying in it?
  2. Is there someone I need to confess a wrong to, so that we can pray together?
  3. Do I think of prayer as my first resource or my last resort?

Lord, teach me to pray in every season, and to believe that You hear.

Introduction: Three Kinds of Prayer

James has called suffering believers to patience (5:7–11) and to truthful speech (5:12). Now he tells them what to do while they wait: pray. The whole section, 5:13–18, is about prayer, and it moves in three circles. First, personal prayer, for the individual (5:13). Second, pastoral prayer, by the elders for the sick (5:14–15). Third, congregational prayer, by believers for one another (5:16–18).

This passage has been badly misused. Some broadcast ministries promise healing in exchange for a gift and treat James 5 as a formula. Others ignore it altogether. James gives neither a formula nor a footnote. He gives a pattern of prayer for a local church that loves its members.

I. Personal Prayer (5:13)

“Is anyone among you suffering? Then he must pray. Is anyone cheerful? He is to sing praises.”

James 5:13 (NASB95)

A. If You Are Suffering, Pray

The word for suffering (kakopatheō) belongs to the same family as the “suffering” of the prophets in 5:10. It describes hardship, often hardship caused by others. James is still speaking to the same people about the same subject: believers mistreated by the rich (5:1–6). His answer is simple. Before you complain (5:9), before you swear (5:12), pray.

Why pray? Because God is able: He “is able to do far more abundantly beyond all that we ask or think” (Ephesians 3:20). And because God has told us to come. Jesus told of a man who knocked on his friend’s door at midnight and kept knocking until he got the bread he needed. Then He said, “ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you” (Luke 11:9). He told another parable “to show that at all times they ought to pray and not to lose heart” (Luke 18:1).

And prayer brings a gift even before the answer comes. “Be anxious for nothing, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all comprehension, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus” (Philippians 4:6–7). The circumstances may not change at once, but the heart is guarded.

B. What to Pray For

First, pray for deliverance. Paul was once burdened so far beyond his strength that he “despaired even of life.” God delivered him, and Paul wrote that He “will yet deliver us, you also joining in helping us through your prayers” (2 Corinthians 1:8, 10–11).

Second, pray for wisdom to understand what God is doing (1:5). Third, pray for grace to endure. Sometimes God removes the trial; sometimes He gives grace to bear it. Paul asked three times for his thorn in the flesh to be removed, and the Lord answered, “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is perfected in weakness” (2 Corinthians 12:9). We have a High Priest who sympathizes with our weaknesses, so we can “draw near with confidence to the throne of grace, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need” (Hebrews 4:16).

Fourth, pray to become like Christ. Why did God save you? Not only to take you to heaven, but so that you would “become conformed to the image of His Son” (Romans 8:29). That happens as we look at Him in His Word: “we all, with unveiled face, beholding as in a mirror the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from glory to glory” (2 Corinthians 3:18). One day the process will be complete: “when He appears, we will be like Him, because we will see Him just as He is” (1 John 3:2). Suffering is often the tool God uses to shape us. How long should we pray? As long as the need remains.

C. If You Are Cheerful, Sing

“Is anyone cheerful? He is to sing praises.” The word psallō originally meant to pluck a stringed instrument, and came to mean to sing psalms of praise. The suffering man prays and the cheerful man sings for the same reason: both turn to the God who gave or allowed their condition. Paul tells believers to speak to one another “in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing and making melody with your heart to the Lord” (Ephesians 5:19; cf. Colossians 3:16). And sometimes the two belong together. At midnight in a Philippian jail, with their backs bleeding, “Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns of praise to God, and the prisoners were listening to them” (Acts 16:25).

II. Pastoral Prayer for the Sick (5:14–15)

“Is anyone among you sick? Then he must call for the elders of the church and they are to pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord; and the prayer offered in faith will restore the one who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up, and if he has committed sins, they will be forgiven him.”

James 5:14–15 (NASB95)

Before we look at James’s instructions, it helps to see what the rest of the New Testament says about healing, so that we neither overstate nor understate this passage.

A. Healing in the Ministry of Jesus

Jesus did not heal everyone who was sick. At the pool of Bethesda “a multitude of those who were sick, blind, lame, and withered” lay waiting, yet He healed one man (John 5:3–9). His healings were signs that He was the Messiah. When John the Baptist asked whether He was the Coming One, Jesus said, “Go and report to John what you hear and see: the blind receive sight and the lame walk” (Matthew 11:4–5). And when the disciples asked whose sin caused a man’s blindness, Jesus said, “It was neither that this man sinned, nor his parents; but it was so that the works of God might be displayed in him” (John 9:3).

B. Healing in the Early Church

The Spirit gave “gifts of healing” in the early church (1 Corinthians 12:9). Peter healed a lame man at the temple gate (Acts 3:6). Paul healed the father of Publius on Malta (Acts 28:8). But even the apostles could not heal at will. Epaphroditus “was sick to the point of death” before God had mercy on him (Philippians 2:27). Paul told Timothy to “use a little wine for the sake of your stomach and your frequent ailments” (1 Timothy 5:23). And he wrote, “Trophimus I left sick at Miletus” (2 Timothy 4:20). The apostles never held healing campaigns. Their message was the gospel, not healing.

C. God Can Heal, but Does Not Always

God can and does heal in answer to prayer. But it is not His will to heal every sickness. He said to Moses, “Who makes him mute or deaf, or seeing or blind? Is it not I, the Lord?” (Exodus 4:11). Satan was permitted to strike Job (Job 2:7). Sickness can be the Lord’s discipline (Hebrews 12:6; 1 Corinthians 11:30). Our bodies wear out, and the whole creation groans under the effects of Adam’s sin as we wait for “the redemption of our body” (Romans 8:23). That God does not heal does not mean He cannot.

God also uses means. Luke was “the beloved physician” (Colossians 4:14), and Paul recommended a remedy to Timothy. Can God give you a crop of tomatoes if you never plant? He could, but ordinarily He works through planting and watering. Seeking medical care is not a lack of faith.

What about the claim that physical healing is guaranteed in the atonement? Isaiah wrote, “by His scourging we are healed” (Isaiah 53:5). But Peter applies that verse to sin: “He Himself bore our sins in His body on the cross, so that we might die to sin and live to righteousness; for by His wounds you were healed” (1 Peter 2:24). The cross does secure the final healing of our bodies, but that comes at the resurrection, not on demand today (Romans 8:23).

D. The Pattern James Gives

First, the sick person calls the elders, not the other way around. The word for sick (astheneō) means weak, without strength; he is probably at home, too ill to come to the assembly. And he calls the elders of his own church, men who know him and his life.

Second, the elders, plural, pray over him. Note that no offering is taken.

Third, they anoint him with oil “in the name of the Lord.” The word aleiphō was used of rubbing oil on the body, and oil was a common remedy (Luke 10:34). The disciples also anointed the sick when Jesus sent them out (Mark 6:13). The oil may have been medicinal, or symbolic of setting the person apart to God and of the Spirit’s work. Either way, the healing comes from the Lord, not the oil.

“In the name of the Lord” means by His authority and in dependence on Him. The elders do not claim power of their own. They bring the sick man to the Lord, and leave the outcome with Him.

Roman Catholic teaching draws on this verse for a sacrament once called “extreme unction,” given to the dying. But James is not preparing anyone for death. He expects the sick person to be raised up from his sickbed. The evangelical reading takes the passage at face value: the elders pray for recovery, and the Lord, not a ritual, does the healing.

Fourth, “the prayer offered in faith will restore the one who is sick.” The faith here is the faith of the elders who pray. If healing does not come, James does not blame the sick man’s faith. The word “restore” is sōzō, the word usually translated “save.” Here it plainly means physical restoration, as it does in Matthew 9:22, “your faith has made you well.” And “the Lord will raise him up,” from his sickbed.

E. Sickness and Sin

“And if he has committed sins, they will be forgiven him.” Not all sickness comes from sin (John 9:3), but some does. At Corinth, some believers were sick and some had died because of their conduct at the Lord’s Table (1 Corinthians 11:30). This is one reason the sick man calls his own elders. They know him, and if there is unconfessed sin behind his illness, it can be confessed and forgiven. This is the forgiveness of fellowship for a believer, not the gift of eternal life, which he already has.

III. Congregational Prayer (5:16)

“Therefore, confess your sins to one another, and pray for one another so that you may be healed. The effective prayer of a righteous man can accomplish much.”

James 5:16 (NASB95)

A. Confess to One Another

“Therefore” ties this to the sick man and the elders, but James widens the circle to the whole church. The word exomologeō means to confess openly, to agree about sin. We confess our sins to God (1 John 1:9). James adds that we confess to one another, not to a priest. There is “one mediator also between God and men, the man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5).

This does not mean total public unburdening. Confession should go as far as the sin has gone. If I have wronged a brother, I confess it to him. If I have sinned against the whole church, I confess it to the church. Confession is not to the general public, and not to just anyone.

B. Pray for One Another

Confession opens the door to forgiveness, and forgiveness opens the door to intercession. When I know that you have confessed and I have forgiven, I can pray for you with a whole heart. This needs to go on all the time to keep the unity of the body. It is how a church carries out Paul’s word: “restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness … Bear one another’s burdens” (Galatians 6:1–2). Sometimes that means a word of loving reproof to keep a brother from straying, and sometimes a word of correction to help him back. In both, we use the Word.

The early church practiced this. When Herod put Peter in prison, “prayer for him was being made fervently by the church to God” (Acts 12:5), and God sent an angel to bring him out. The church that prays for one another is the church that sees God work.

C. That You May Be Healed

The sick man is still in view, so physical healing is included. But the healing of relationships and of the soul comes first, and often the healing of the body follows. James does not promise instant healing, and he does not say how it will come. The elders pray, the church prays, and God heals in His way and His time.

D. The Prayer of a Righteous Man

“The effective prayer of a righteous man can accomplish much.” The word “effective” translates energeō, from which we get “energy.” It is prayer energized by God: earnest, not glib or perfunctory. Have you ever finished saying grace and wondered whether you had said it at all? That is not the prayer James means.

And it is the prayer of a “righteous” man. Every believer has the righteousness of Christ as a gift. But James is speaking of practical righteousness, a life walking in fellowship with God. “If I regard wickedness in my heart, the Lord will not hear” (Psalm 66:18). That is why confession comes first. We cannot come before a holy God with unconfessed sin and expect our prayers to be heard.

IV. Elijah, a Man Like Us (5:17–18)

“Elijah was a man with a nature like ours, and he prayed earnestly that it would not rain, and it did not rain on the earth for three years and six months. Then he prayed again, and the sky poured rain and the earth produced its fruit.”

James 5:17–18 (NASB95)

A. Why Elijah?

James could have chosen Moses, who prayed and the fire of judgment died down (Numbers 11:1–2), or Samuel, who prayed for thunder and rain (1 Samuel 12:17–18). God Himself named those two as great intercessors (Jeremiah 15:1). Instead James chose Elijah, the fourth Old Testament figure in his letter after Abraham, Rahab and Job. Elijah was revered by the Jews, second only to Moses in their eyes, and he appeared with Moses on the Mount of Transfiguration (Matthew 17:3).

B. A Nature Like Ours

But James stresses that Elijah “was a man with a nature like ours.” The word homoiopathēs means of like passions, the same word Paul used when the crowd at Lystra tried to worship him: “We are also men of the same nature as you” (Acts 14:15). Elijah was no superman. After his great victory on Mount Carmel, he “was afraid and arose and ran for his life,” sat under a juniper tree and asked God to take his life (1 Kings 19:3–4). He knew fear and discouragement. He carried an unpopular message to a wicked king, who called him the “troubler of Israel” (1 Kings 18:17). His setting was a nation sunk in Baal worship, led by Ahab and Jezebel.

C. His Prayers and Their Answer

First, Elijah prayed that it would not rain, and he announced to Ahab, “surely there shall be neither dew nor rain these years, except by my word” (1 Kings 17:1). James says “he prayed earnestly,” literally “he prayed with prayer,” a Hebrew way of saying he prayed intensely. And it did not rain for three years and six months (cf. Luke 4:25). During that time God hid Elijah by the brook Cherith, where ravens fed him (1 Kings 17:3–4), and then sent him to a widow in Zarephath, whose flour and oil did not run out (17:9–16).

At the end of the drought came the contest on Mount Carmel. Elijah challenged the people, “How long will you hesitate between two opinions? If the Lord is God, follow Him; but if Baal, follow him” (1 Kings 18:21). The prophets of Baal cried out all day with no answer. Then Elijah prayed a simple prayer: “Answer me, O Lord, answer me, that this people may know that You, O Lord, are God” (1 Kings 18:37). “Then the fire of the Lord fell and consumed the burnt offering” (18:38).

Then, after the fire fell on Carmel, Elijah prayed again. He went up to the top of the mountain, “crouched down on the earth and put his face between his knees” (1 Kings 18:42). Seven times he sent his servant to look toward the sea, until a small cloud appeared. “In a little while the sky grew black with clouds and wind, and there was a heavy shower” (1 Kings 18:45). “And the earth produced its fruit.” The point: the effective prayer of a righteous man, not a superhuman one, accomplishes much.

Conclusion: Let Us Pray

So what difference does this make? It means that every season of life calls for prayer: suffering, joy, sickness and sin. It means that a church should be a praying community, where the sick call the elders, where believers confess to one another and pray for one another, and where no one suffers alone. Keep a prayer list. Gather to pray. Pray for one another by name.

Elijah’s prayers changed the weather over a nation. Ours may change the course of a sickness, a marriage, a church or a wandering brother’s life. We do not know how God will answer, but we know that He hears, and that He has chosen to work through the prayers of His people.

Every believer has eternal life and access to the throne of grace. We do not pray to earn God’s favor; we pray because we already have it. And the God who answered Elijah hears His children today.

Lord, thank You.

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. Why does James tell the suffering to pray before he tells them anything else?

Show Leader Answer

Prayer brings our troubles to the God who is able and who invites us. It guards us from grumbling (5:9) and rash oaths (5:12).

2. How do the examples of Epaphroditus, Timothy and Trophimus keep us from overstating James 5:14–15?

Show Leader Answer

Even the apostles could not heal at will. God can heal, but He does not always, and lack of healing does not mean lack of faith.

3. Why is it important that the sick person calls the elders of his own church?

Show Leader Answer

They know him and his life, and they can pray with understanding, including about any sin behind the sickness.

4. What does healthy confession “to one another” look like?

Show Leader Answer

Confessing to the person we have wronged, as far as the sin has gone, leading to forgiveness and prayer, not public unburdening or confession to a priest.

5. How does Elijah’s fear and discouragement (1 Kings 19) encourage us to pray?

Show Leader Answer

He was a man with a nature like ours, yet God answered his prayers. We do not have to be spiritual giants to pray effectively.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Pray First

James 5:13; Philippians 4:6–7

Source: Commentary ③ — I

Make prayer your first response to suffering and praise your first response to joy.

Reflection: What am I worrying about that I have not prayed about?

Practical Step: Each morning this week, name one burden and one blessing to God before you start your day.

Call the Elders

James 5:14–15

Source: Commentary ③ — II

God has given the church elders to pray for the sick. Do not suffer alone or be too proud to ask.

Reflection: Is there a sickness or burden I have kept from my church?

Practical Step: If you are ill or know someone who is, ask your church leaders to pray, and seek good medical care too.

Keep Short Accounts

James 5:16; 1 John 1:9

Source: Commentary ③ — III

Confession to God and to those we have wronged clears the way for prayer and healing.

Reflection: Is there a wrong I need to confess to someone?

Practical Step: Go to that person this week, confess simply, and ask if you may pray together.

Pray Like Elijah

James 5:17–18; 1 Kings 18:42–45

Source: Commentary ③ — IV

God answers the earnest prayers of ordinary believers who walk with Him.

Reflection: Do I believe my prayers matter?

Practical Step: Start a prayer list with three people or needs, and pray for them by name every day for a month.
