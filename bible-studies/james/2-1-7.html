Favorites in the Pew

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

Source: The Gospel Vault · Developed from teaching outlines by Dr. C. Norman Sellers, ThD, used by permission

Leader / Learner Guide

① The Biblical Text

James 2:1–7 · New American Standard Bible (NASB95)

1 My brethren, do not hold your faith in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ with an attitude of personal favoritism.

2 For if a man comes into your assembly with a gold ring and dressed in fine clothes, and there also comes in a poor man in dirty clothes,

3 and you pay special attention to the one who is wearing the fine clothes, and say, “You sit here in a good place,” and you say to the poor man, “You stand over there, or sit down by my footstool,”

4 have you not made distinctions among yourselves, and become judges with evil motives?

5 Listen, my beloved brethren: did not God choose the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom which He promised to those who love Him?

6 But you have dishonored the poor man. Is it not the rich who oppress you and personally drag you into court?

7 Do they not blaspheme the fair name by which you have been called?

Everyone reads before anything else.

② Devotional

“My brethren, do not hold your faith in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ with an attitude of personal favoritism.” — James 2:1

Two visitors walk into church. One wears a gold ring and expensive clothes; the other smells of the street. James asks a simple, uncomfortable question: who gets the good seat? We would like to think we would treat them the same, but our eyes notice the difference before our hearts do.

Favoritism judges by the outside, by money, looks, age, background or how much someone is like us. God does not. He chose people the world overlooks to be rich in faith and to rule with Christ in His coming kingdom. Our Lord Himself became poor for our sake.

When we stand before the glorious Lord Jesus Christ, every earthly distinction fades. A church that remembers His glory will open its best seats to anyone He brings through the door.

Reflection Questions

  1. Which kind of person am I most likely to overlook or favor without thinking?
  2. How does remembering the glory of Christ change the way I see people?
  3. Who could I welcome this week whom others might ignore?

Lord of glory, help me see people as You see them.

Introduction: The Near-Sighted Usher

Chapter 1 ended with pure religion: caring for orphans and widows, the helpless people of society (1:27). Chapter 2 opens with the opposite: believers who fawn over the rich and push aside the poor. James has been telling us to be “doers of the word” (1:22). Now he names one specific way his readers were failing to do it. They were showing favoritism, and they were doing it in their worship gatherings, of all places.

This passage is still part of the section on being “quick to hear” (1:21–2:26). The believer who truly hears the Word will treat people the way the Word says to treat them. And it leads directly to the royal law of love (2:8–13) and the question of useful faith (2:14–26), where the illustration is, again, a poor brother in need (2:15–16).

I. Favoritism Identified

A. What Favoritism Is

Favoritism has two sides. On the positive side, it is giving someone special treatment because of outward factors: appearance, money, position or connections. On the negative side, it is discrimination: mistreating someone because of those same outward factors. James addresses both. The rich man gets the good seat; the poor man gets the floor.

God has always forbidden it among His people. The Law said, “you shall not be partial to the poor nor defer to the great, but you are to judge your neighbor fairly” (Leviticus 19:15), and, “you shall hear the small and the great alike” (Deuteronomy 1:17). Notice that the Law forbids partiality both ways. Favoring the poor because they are poor is no better than favoring the rich because they are rich.

B. The Many Faces of Favoritism

Favoritism comes in many shapes and sizes. Here are some of the most common:

KindHow it shows up
AppearanceAttractive or unattractive; well dressed or shabby
AgeThe old dismiss the young as immature; the young dismiss the old as out of touch
AchievementEnvying those who have succeeded, or looking down on those who have not; quick learners and slow learners
AffinityPreferring people “just like me” in culture, background, education or style
AffluenceThe poor resent the rich, the rich look down on the poor, and the middle class looks both ways
AncestryJudging by family, nationality, language or ethnic background
AbilityIgnoring those with disabilities instead of seeing what they can do and how we can help
CircumstancesMarried or divorced, employed or out of work, “respectable” past or troubled past

Common ground is a good thing. It is natural to enjoy people who share our interests. But it is never a reason to shut others out or to treat them as less important. As one old saying goes, “I know what I like, and I like what I know.” Sometimes the cure is simply to get to know someone.

II. Favoritism Forbidden (2:1)

“My brethren, do not hold your faith in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ with an attitude of personal favoritism.”

James 2:1 (NASB95)

A. “Your Faith”

James addresses “my brethren” and speaks of “your faith in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ.” These are believers. The phrase can be read as the faith by which they believed in Christ, or as “the faith,” the body of truth about Christ that every believer holds (cf. Jude 3). Either way, the point is the same: what you believe about Jesus Christ cannot be held together with favoritism. The two are incompatible.

B. “Our Glorious Lord Jesus Christ”

James’s description of Jesus is remarkable. Literally it reads, “our Lord Jesus Christ, of the glory.” It may mean that He is the glorious Lord, or that He is the Lord who is the glory. Every Jewish reader would think of the Shekinah, the visible glory of God that filled the tabernacle and the temple. John says that in Jesus, “we saw His glory, glory as of the only begotten from the Father” (John 1:14). Paul calls Him “the Lord of glory” (1 Corinthians 2:8).

And this is the man who once thought his Brother had lost His senses (Mark 3:21). Now he calls Him the Lord of glory. The point here is clear: in the presence of that glory, all human glory fades. Gold rings and fine clothes look very small next to the Shekinah. To be impressed by a man’s wealth while worshiping the Lord of glory is to have one’s eyes in the wrong place.

C. “Personal Favoritism”

The word prosōpolēmpsia is vivid. It means literally “receiving the face,” judging by the face, by outward appearance. The idea goes back to the Hebrew expression for lifting someone’s face, showing favor. God does not do it: “God sees not as man sees, for man looks at the outward appearance, but the Lord looks at the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7). Peter learned this lesson at the house of Cornelius:

“Opening his mouth, Peter said: ‘I most certainly understand now that God is not one to show partiality,”

Acts 10:34 (NASB95)

If God does not judge by the face, neither should we. At the foot of the cross, every person stands on level ground: “there is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus” (Galatians 3:28).

Favoritism toward the rich is especially out of place when we remember our Lord:

  • He became poor. “Though He was rich, yet for your sake He became poor, so that you through His poverty might become rich” (2 Corinthians 8:9).
  • He lived poor. “The Son of Man has nowhere to lay His head” (Matthew 8:20).
  • He preached to the poor. “The poor have the gospel preached to them” (Matthew 11:5; cf. Luke 4:18).

A believer who would have given the good seat to the rich man and the floor to the poor man might well have given the floor to Jesus.

III. Favoritism Illustrated (2:2–4)

“For if a man comes into your assembly with a gold ring and dressed in fine clothes, and there also comes in a poor man in dirty clothes,”

James 2:2 (NASB95)

“and you pay special attention to the one who is wearing the fine clothes, and say, ‘You sit here in a good place,’ and you say to the poor man, ‘You stand over there, or sit down by my footstool,’”

James 2:3 (NASB95)

A. The Setting: Your Assembly (2:2)

James describes a scene in “your assembly.” The Greek word is synagōgē, synagogue. James does not use the usual word for church (ekklēsia) here, though he uses it in 5:14. This is one of the signs that the letter comes from very early in church history, when Jewish believers still met in what they called synagogues.

“If a man comes…” is grammatically a hypothetical. But James paints the scene so vividly that we can almost see him wink. He has watched it happen.

Two visitors arrive. The first is a man “with a gold ring,” literally “gold-fingered” (chrysodaktylios). In the Roman world, rings were a mark of rank and wealth; some wore several on each hand. He is “dressed in fine clothes,” lampros, bright, shining, splendid. The second is “a poor man in dirty clothes,” rhyparos, the same root as the moral “filthiness” of 1:21. He has probably worn the same garment for a long time; it is the only one he owns.

B. The Reception (2:3)

What does the usher do? He does what he always does. To the rich man: “You sit here in a good place,” the comfortable seat, up front, where everyone can see him. The phrase “pay special attention” (epiblepō) means to look upon with favor. To the poor man: “You stand over there, or sit down by my footstool.” Stand against the wall, out of the way, or sit on the floor.

This was exactly what Jesus condemned in the religious leaders of His day, who “love the place of honor at banquets and the chief seats in the synagogues” (Matthew 23:6). And it is exactly the opposite of what He taught His disciples to do:

“But when you give a reception, invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind,”

Luke 14:13 (NASB95)

“and you will be blessed, since they do not have the means to repay you; for you will be repaid at the resurrection of the righteous.’”

Luke 14:14 (NASB95)

C. The Indictment (2:4)

“have you not made distinctions among yourselves, and become judges with evil motives?”

James 2:4 (NASB95)

James asks two questions, and both expect the answer “yes.”

  • “Have you not made distinctions among yourselves?” The verb is diakrinō, the same word translated “doubting” in 1:6. There it described a divided mind in prayer; here it describes a divided judgment toward people. The double-minded believer of chapter 1 shows up again in chapter 2, wavering between loyalty to the Lord of glory and admiration for a man with gold rings.
  • “Have you not become judges with evil motives?” They have set themselves up as judges (kritai), deciding who is worthy of honor, and their reasoning (dialogismoi) is corrupt. They are judging by appearance, which Jesus forbade: “Do not judge according to appearance, but judge with righteous judgment” (John 7:24).

What were their evil motives? Perhaps they hoped the rich man would give generously. Perhaps they wanted the prestige of having important people in their congregation. Perhaps they simply found the poor man unpleasant. Whatever the reason, they were treating people according to what they could get from them, not according to how God sees them.

IV. Favoritism Is Inconsistent: Rejecting Those God Has Approved (2:5–6a)

“Listen, my beloved brethren: did not God choose the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom which He promised to those who love Him?”

James 2:5 (NASB95)

“Listen, my beloved brethren.” James calls for attention, the way Jesus did in the letters to the seven churches: “He who has an ear, let him hear” (Revelation 2–3). He adds “beloved.” He is correcting people he loves.

A. Chosen by God

“Did not God choose the poor of this world?” The question expects a “yes.” James’s Jewish readers understood choosing. God had chosen Israel, not because they were great: “The Lord did not set His love on you nor choose you because you were more in number than any of the peoples, for you were the fewest of all peoples” (Deuteronomy 7:7). Paul saw the same pattern in the church:

“For consider your calling, brethren, that there were not many wise according to the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble;”

1 Corinthians 1:26 (NASB95)

“but God has chosen the foolish things of the world to shame the wise, and God has chosen the weak things of the world to shame the things which are strong,”

1 Corinthians 1:27 (NASB95)

“The poor of this world” means poor “as far as the world is concerned,” poor by the world’s measure. Notice what James is not saying:

  • He is not saying that poverty has merit in itself.
  • He is not saying that God chose them because they were poor.
  • He is not saying that all poor people will be saved and all rich people lost.

And notice what they were chosen for. The point here is not their choosing for eternal life. Eternal life comes to anyone who believes in Christ (John 3:16). James says they were chosen “to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom.”

B. Rich in Faith

To be “rich in faith” does not mean that faith is their wealth, as if they had a lot of it in a bank. It means they are rich in the realm of faith. In the realm of the world they are poor; in the realm of faith they are wealthy. In the world, gold is the coin of the realm; with God, faith is the coin of the realm. “Without faith it is impossible to please Him” (Hebrews 11:6), and Peter calls tested faith “more precious than gold” (1 Peter 1:7).

This is not the simple faith that receives eternal life; that is a single act of believing God’s promise. James means faith exercised in daily life: trusting God for bread, for strength in trials, for wisdom (1:5). The poor often know this kind of faith better than the rich, because they have fewer earthly things to lean on. It echoes 1:9: “let the brother of humble circumstances glory in his high position.”

C. Heirs of the Kingdom

They are also chosen to be “heirs of the kingdom which He promised to those who love Him.” This phrase deserves careful attention. In Scripture, to inherit the kingdom is not the same as to enter it. Every believer will enter Christ’s kingdom, because entrance is by faith alone (John 3:3–5). But to inherit the kingdom means to possess it, to share in Christ’s rule within it. The Father promised the Son: “Ask of Me, and I will surely give the nations as Your inheritance” (Psalm 2:8). Faithful believers will share that inheritance:

“If we endure, we will also reign with Him; If we deny Him, He also will deny us;”

2 Timothy 2:12 (NASB95)

“He who overcomes, and he who keeps My deeds until the end, to him I will give authority over the nations;”

Revelation 2:26 (NASB95)

“and he shall rule them with a rod of iron, as the vessels of the potter are broken to pieces, as I also have received authority from My Father;”

Revelation 2:27 (NASB95)

Paul makes the same distinction. All believers are “heirs of God,” but some will also be “fellow heirs with Christ, if indeed we suffer with Him so that we may also be glorified with Him” (Romans 8:17). Notice the condition in James: the kingdom is promised “to those who love Him.” This is the same phrase used of the crown of life in 1:12. Eternal life is free to all who believe. Reigning with Christ is promised to believers who love Him and show it by faithful obedience (cf. Joseph C. Dillow, The Reign of the Servant Kings, 2nd ed. [Schoettle Publishing, 2011]; Zane C. Hodges, The Epistle of James: Proven Character Through Testing [Irving, TX: Grace Evangelical Society, 1994]).

Jesus tied love and obedience together:

“He who has My commandments and keeps them is the one who loves Me; and he who loves Me will be loved by My Father, and I will love him and will disclose Myself to him.’”

John 14:21 (NASB95)

And Paul shows where such obedience comes from: “the life which I now live in the flesh I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave Himself up for me” (Galatians 2:20). Living faith produces loving obedience, and loving obedience will be rewarded.

D. “But You Have Dishonored the Poor Man”

Now James draws the contrast sharply: “But you have dishonored the poor man.” The verb atimazō means to treat with contempt, to strip of honor. God chose the poor to be rich in faith and to reign with Christ, and the church sent him to sit on the floor. They rejected those whom God had approved.

V. Favoritism Is Inconsistent: Approving Those Who Oppose God (2:6b–7)

“But you have dishonored the poor man. Is it not the rich who oppress you and personally drag you into court?”

James 2:6 (NASB95)

“Do they not blaspheme the fair name by which you have been called?”

James 2:7 (NASB95)

The second inconsistency is the reverse of the first. They were honoring the very people who were hurting them.

A. They Oppress You

“Is it not the rich who oppress you?” The word katadynasteuō means to exercise harsh power over someone, to exploit. James does not say every rich person did this, but as a class the wealthy were the oppressors of his readers. Some were probably unbelievers trying to make a profit at others’ expense. Some may have hated these believers because they no longer took part in the temple system. And sadly, some may even have been believers whose faith had not yet reached their business dealings. James will speak to such rich men directly in 5:1–6.

B. They Drag You into Court

“And personally drag you into court.” The verb helkō means to drag, to haul by force. In the early chapters of Acts, the wealthy and powerful priestly party, the Sadducees, were the first persecutors of the church. They were “greatly disturbed because they were teaching the people and proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection from the dead,” and they “laid hands on them and put them in jail” (Acts 4:2–3). Later, the owners of a slave girl “seized Paul and Silas and dragged them into the market place before the authorities” (Acts 16:19), because their profits were gone. Jesus had warned His disciples it would happen:

“But beware of men, for they will hand you over to the courts and scourge you in their synagogues;”

Matthew 10:17 (NASB95)

C. They Blaspheme the Fair Name

“Do they not blaspheme the fair name by which you have been called?” The “fair name” is the name of Jesus. Literally, it is “the good name which was called upon you,” the name pronounced over them, perhaps publicly at their baptism, marking them as belonging to Him. In Antioch, believers came to be called “Christians” (Acts 11:26), and Peter writes, “if anyone suffers as a Christian, he is not to be ashamed, but is to glorify God in this name” (1 Peter 4:16).

The rich oppressors mocked that name, perhaps scorning the believers for worshiping a crucified criminal. And the believers were giving such men the best seats in the house. It was inconsistent to reject those God has approved, and to approve those who reject God.

VI. What James Is Not Saying

It is important to read James carefully here.

  • He is not calling for reverse discrimination. The answer to favoring the rich is not mistreating them to even things out. The Law forbade partiality in both directions (Leviticus 19:15).
  • He is not condemning wealth itself. “The rich and the poor have a common bond, the Lord is the maker of them all” (Proverbs 22:2). James is aiming at the misuse of money, power and position, and at believers who are dazzled by them.
  • He is not saying that the poor are automatically godly. He is saying that God often chooses those the world overlooks, and so must we.

Conclusion: Seats for Everyone

James has asked the near-sighted usher to get his eyes checked. In the presence of the glorious Lord Jesus Christ, gold rings and fine clothes do not matter. What matters is that God has chosen people the world overlooks to be rich in faith and heirs of His kingdom.

Every church can ask itself some honest questions. Who is most welcome here? Who would feel out of place? Do we treat the generous giver differently from the struggling single mother? Paul’s counsel applies: “do not be haughty in mind, but associate with the lowly” (Romans 12:16). The Lord of glory made room for us when we had nothing. We can make room for anyone He brings through the door.

Lord, thank You.

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. Which of the “faces” of favoritism (appearance, age, achievement, affinity, affluence, ancestry, ability) do you find most common in churches today?

Show Leader Answer

Answers will vary. Help the group see that favoritism is often unconscious and can run in either direction, including against the rich.

2. How does focusing on “our glorious Lord Jesus Christ” cure favoritism?

Show Leader Answer

His glory makes human status look small, and His humility (2 Corinthians 8:9) shows us how to treat the lowly.

3. James 2:5 says God chose the poor “to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom.” Why is it important not to read this as “God saves the poor because they are poor”?

Show Leader Answer

Salvation is by faith alone, open to rich and poor alike. James is speaking of a life of faith and of inheriting (ruling in) the kingdom, promised to those who love Him.

4. What is the difference between entering the kingdom and inheriting it? Why does that matter here?

Show Leader Answer

Every believer enters by faith; inheriting means sharing Christ’s rule, a reward for faithful, loving believers. It shows that the poor believer may be far richer in eternity than the rich man in the good seat.

5. What practical steps could a church take to guard against favoritism?

Show Leader Answer

Train greeters to welcome everyone equally; avoid special treatment for large givers; involve people from different backgrounds in ministry; make seating, food and fellowship open to all.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Check Your Eyes at the Door

James 2:1–4; 1 Samuel 16:7

Source: Commentary ③ — III

Favoritism begins with what we notice first. Training our eyes to see people as God sees them takes deliberate effort.

Reflection: When a newcomer walks in, what do I notice first?

Practical Step: This Sunday, introduce yourself to the person who looks least like you or most out of place.

Remember Who Became Poor

James 2:1; 2 Corinthians 8:9

Source: Commentary ③ — II.C

The Lord of glory became poor for us. Remembering His humility guards us against admiring wealth.

Reflection: Do I treat people differently because of what they can do for me?

Practical Step: Read Philippians 2:5–8 and ask God to show you one relationship where you need His humility.

Invest in Future Rulers

James 2:5; 2 Timothy 2:12

Source: Commentary ③ — IV.B–C; cf. Dillow, The Reign of the Servant Kings

The poor believer who loves God may be a future co-ruler with Christ. Honoring such believers now is honoring what God honors.

Reflection: Whom have I overlooked who may be rich in faith?

Practical Step: Ask an older or poorer believer in your church to share how God has provided for them, and listen.

Welcome Without Repayment

Luke 14:13–14; James 2:3

Source: Commentary ③ — III.B

Jesus told us to welcome those who cannot repay us, and promised reward at the resurrection. That is the opposite of favoritism.

Reflection: Whom do I invite into my home or life, and why?

Practical Step: Invite someone to a meal this month who cannot return the favor.
