What Does It Mean That Scripture Is Inspired and Inerrant?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Inspiration means God superintended the human authors so that they wrote what He intended; inerrancy means the original manuscripts are true in all they affirm. Jesus Himself held this view of Scripture.

Key verses2 Timothy 3:16 · John 10:35 · Matthew 5:18

“Inspired” and “inerrant” are precise theological terms describing specific, related claims about the Bible’s origin and reliability, and confusion often arises from imprecise popular use of these words. Inspiration refers to the process by which God superintended the human authors of Scripture so that what they wrote, using their own vocabulary, personality, and historical circumstances, was exactly what God intended communicated: “All Scripture is given by inspiration of God” (2 Timothy 3:16) — literally, in the Greek, “God-breathed.” This is not dictation, in which human authors served as passive stenographers, nor is it merely human insight about God; it is a unique, dual-authorship process in which fully human writing became, in its result, fully God’s own Word.

Inerrancy is the specific claim that follows from this doctrine of inspiration applied consistently: because Scripture is God’s own Word, and because God cannot lie or err (Titus 1:2; Numbers 23:19), Scripture in its original manuscripts (the autographs) is entirely true and without error in everything it affirms — not only in matters of theology and morality, but in whatever historical and factual claims it makes as well. This claim is carefully and deliberately restricted to the original manuscripts rather than every existing copy or translation, since inerrancy is a claim about what God actually inspired, not about the accuracy of every subsequent scribe’s copying or every modern translator’s rendering — which is precisely why the discipline of textual criticism, comparing thousands of manuscripts to reconstruct the original wording, matters so much, and why the manuscript evidence (see Q6, “How Do We Know the Bible Is Trustworthy?”) is so significant.

Jesus’ own view of Scripture models the doctrine believers are called to hold: He treated Old Testament narratives, including ones modern readers sometimes find difficult, as straightforward historical fact — Jonah and the great fish (Matthew 12:40), Noah’s flood (Matthew 24:37–39), and Adam and Eve’s creation (Matthew 19:4–5) — and declared that “the Scripture cannot be broken” (John 10:35) and that not even “one jot or one tittle” of the law would pass away without fulfillment (Matthew 5:18). A believer’s view of Scripture’s reliability is, at bottom, a question of trusting Jesus’ own explicitly stated view of the Bible He treated as fully authoritative.

Why does this doctrine matter beyond technical precision? Because every other doctrine in this paper — the security of salvation, the reality of the resurrection, the promises of assurance, the hope of eternal life — depends entirely on Scripture’s statements being reliably, truly what God actually said. If Scripture can be mistaken in what it plainly affirms about history or the natural world, there is no principled reason to trust its claims about salvation, resurrection, or eternity any more confidently; the doctrine of inerrancy is what allows every other doctrine in this paper to be received as God’s own trustworthy word rather than merely one ancient religious opinion among many.

On this foundational doctrine of Scripture’s inspiration and inerrancy, Bible-believing Christians of many traditions stand together, as the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy sets out.1
