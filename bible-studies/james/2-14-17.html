What Good Is Faith That Does Nothing?

② Devotional

“What use is it, my brethren, if someone says he has faith but he has no works?” — James 2:14

A brother in your church has no coat and nothing in the pantry. You see him after the service, smile warmly, and say, “I’ll be praying for you. Stay warm, and eat well!” Then you drive home to a full refrigerator. James asks a simple question: what good did that do him?

James is not asking whether you are going to heaven. Eternal life is God’s free gift to everyone who believes in His Son, and it can never be lost. He is asking whether your faith is doing anyone any good. Faith that never moves into action is like a dead battery: it is real, but it does not start anything.

God saved us by grace so that we could walk in good works He prepared for us. Faith put to work feeds the hungry brother, rescues our own lives from waste, and will be rewarded when Christ returns.

Reflection Questions

  1. Is there a need around me that I have answered with words but not with help?
  2. Where is my faith “idle” right now, believed but not put to work?
  3. What is one good work God may have prepared for me this week?

Lord, You saved me freely. Now put my faith to work for others.

Introduction: The Most Misunderstood Passage in James

“What use is it, my brethren, if someone says he has faith but he has no works? Can that faith save him?”

James 2:14 (NASB95)

James 2:14–26 is one of the most important, most debated and most misunderstood passages in the New Testament. Its key words, faith and works, appear together again and again in thirteen verses. For centuries it has been used to argue that no one can be sure of heaven without good works. Martin Luther, troubled by how it was used, questioned whether the letter belonged with the apostolic writings and called it “an epistle of straw.”

But James is not fighting the gospel. He is the brother of Jesus, a pillar of the church, and he shook hands with Paul over the very gospel Paul preached (Galatians 2:9). Read in its own context, James 2:14–26 does not threaten the believer’s assurance at all. It answers a different question than the one people usually bring to it.

We will take this section in two studies. This one covers the thesis and its illustration (2:14–17). The next takes up the objection and the proof from Abraham and Rahab (2:18–26). The theme of both is the same: “faith without works is dead” (2:17, 20, 26).

I. Three Ways to Read James 2

Broadly speaking, readers have taken this passage in three ways.

A. Works Required to Receive Eternal Life

Some hold that faith and works together are required to be saved from hell. Roman Catholic teaching takes this view, and so do a number of other groups. On this reading, a person must believe and also do good works, keep commandments, be baptized, or remain faithful to the end, to gain eternal life. This view “front-loads” works: they are conditions for receiving salvation.

B. Works Required to Prove Eternal Life

Many Protestants, especially in the Reformed and “lordship salvation” traditions, say that eternal life is received by faith alone, but that genuine faith will inevitably produce good works. If works do not follow, the person never had real, saving faith. This view “back-loads” works: they are not conditions for salvation, but they are the necessary proof of it. The practical result is that no one can be sure he is saved until he examines his works, and since his works are never perfect, assurance always remains uncertain.

C. Works Required for a Useful Life

The view taken in this study, the Free Grace reading, is that works are neither a condition for receiving eternal life nor the necessary proof of it. Eternal life is a free gift, received the moment a person believes in Jesus Christ for it, and it can never be lost. James is writing to “my brethren” about something else: whether their faith is doing them, or anyone else, any good. Faith without works does not cost a believer his eternal life; it costs him a useful, rewarded life.

ViewRole of worksResult for assurance
Works to receive lifeA condition of salvationAssurance depends on continued performance
Works to prove lifeThe necessary evidence of “genuine” faithAssurance depends on examining one’s works
Works for a useful lifeThe fruit God desires, rewarded at the Judgment SeatAssurance rests on Christ’s promise alone

Scripture is clear on how eternal life is received: “to the one who does not work, but believes in Him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is credited as righteousness” (Romans 4:5). “He who believes has eternal life” (John 6:47). “He saved us, not on the basis of deeds which we have done in righteousness, but according to His mercy” (Titus 3:5). If James taught otherwise, Scripture would contradict itself. It does not.

II. The Key Words (2:14)

A. “Use” or “Profit”

James opens with ti to ophelos, “What use is it?” or “What does it profit?” He repeats the question in 2:16. This is the question of the whole section. James is not asking, “How does a person get to heaven?” He is asking, “What good does it do?” Profit is about benefit, usefulness and reward, not about the free gift.

B. “Faith”

“Faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen” (Hebrews 11:1). Faith is confidence in God’s promise. We need faith to enter the Christian life: “He who believes in the Son has eternal life” (John 3:36). And we need faith to live the Christian life: “without faith it is impossible to please Him” (Hebrews 11:6). The man in 2:14 “says he has faith,” and James never says he is lying. He addresses him among “my brethren.”

C. “Works”

Scripture uses “works” in two different settings. Works done to earn salvation are always rejected: “a man is not justified by the works of the Law but through faith in Christ Jesus” (Galatians 2:16; Ephesians 2:8–9). Works done by those who already believe are always encouraged: “Let your light shine before men in such a way that they may see your good works” (Matthew 5:16). Paul puts both in one passage:

“For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand so that we would walk in them.”

Ephesians 2:10 (NASB95)

We are saved “not as a result of works” (2:9), but we are saved “for good works” (2:10). Paul also tells Titus that “those who have believed God” should “be careful to engage in good deeds. These things are good and profitable for men” (Titus 3:8). Notice the word profitable. Which kind of works does James mean? Clearly the second. He calls his readers “brethren” fifteen times. His examples are believers: a needy brother or sister (2:15), Abraham, who “believed God” (2:23), and Rahab, who welcomed the spies by faith (2:25; Hebrews 11:31).

D. “Save”

The word sōzō appears five times in James, and none of them clearly means deliverance from hell:

VerseTextDeliverance from
1:21“the word implanted, which is able to save your souls”Sin’s deadly effects on a believer’s life
2:14“Can that faith save him?”The question of this study
4:12“the One who is able to save and to destroy”God’s power over life and death
5:15“the prayer offered in faith will restore [save] the one who is sick”Sickness
5:20“will save his soul from death”Physical death

When we read 2:14 in light of the rest of the letter, a pattern appears. James has already warned that sin “brings forth death” (1:15) and that the Word “is able to save your souls,” your lives (1:21). He will end the letter the same way (5:20). His concern is the rescue of a believer’s life from the destructive consequences of sin.

III. The Question: “Can That Faith Save Him?” (2:14b)

A. The Usual Answer: A Different Kind of Faith

In Greek, the question includes the article, literally “Can the faith save him?” Many translations render it “that faith” (NASB) or “such faith” (NIV), and many commentaries build an argument on it: there are two kinds of faith, a mere intellectual faith that does not save, and a genuine heart faith that produces works and does save from hell. On that view, James is warning that people who claim faith without works were never really saved.

But there are good reasons to question that argument. In Greek the article is often used with abstract nouns like “faith,” “love” and “hope,” where English leaves it out. James uses the article with “faith” several more times in this section (2:17, 18, 20, 22, 26), and in those verses most translations simply say “faith.” The article points back to the faith just mentioned; it does not create a second, inferior kind of faith. And nowhere does James say the man’s faith is fake. He says it is useless.

B. A Better Answer: Saved from What?

The real question is not what kind of faith, but saved from what? In context, the answer is clear. Can faith that is never put to work rescue a believer’s life from the deadly consequences of sin and from a wasted, profitless life? No. Can it feed a hungry brother? No. Faith has to be put to work to accomplish that kind of deliverance (cf. Zane C. Hodges, The Epistle of James: Proven Character Through Testing [Irving, TX: Grace Evangelical Society, 1994]; Robert N. Wilkin, ed., The Grace New Testament Commentary [Denton, TX: Grace Evangelical Society, 2010]).

So the answers to James’s questions look like this:

  • Can faith without works save him (from a profitless life and sin’s ruin)? No.
  • Can faith with works save him from that? Yes.
  • Can a person earn eternal life by works? No. It can only be received, by believing in Jesus.

IV. What Faith at Work Gains

If faith without works profits nothing, what does faith at work profit? James has already told us, and he will tell us more. Here is a summary from the letter itself:

What faith put to work gainsReference in James
Endurance and maturity1:3–4
God’s blessing in what we do1:25
Approval and the crown of life1:12
The righteousness God desires1:20
A life rescued from sin’s ruin1:21; 5:20
Heirship in the kingdom2:5
“You are doing well”2:8
Mercy at the Judgment Seat2:13
Being called “the friend of God”2:23
Justification before men2:24

Paul agrees: “godliness is profitable for all things, since it holds promise for the present life and also for the life to come” (1 Timothy 4:8). Peter says that growing in Christian qualities will “render you neither useless nor unfruitful” (2 Peter 1:8). Faith at work is profitable faith.

V. The Thesis Illustrated (2:15–16)

“If a brother or sister is without clothing and in need of daily food,”

James 2:15 (NASB95)

“and one of you says to them, ‘Go in peace, be warmed and be filled,’ and yet you do not give them what is necessary for their body, what use is that?”

James 2:16 (NASB95)

A. A Fitting Illustration

James chooses an example his readers knew all too well. Agabus had predicted “a great famine all over the world,” which “took place in the reign of Claudius,” and the believers in Antioch sent relief to “the brethren living in Judea” (Acts 11:28–29). Josephus also describes a severe famine in Judea in those years, around AD 46–48 (cf. Josephus, Antiquities 20.2.5). Hungry, poorly clothed believers were not a hypothetical problem.

The illustration also continues a theme that runs through James: orphans and widows in distress (1:27), the poor man in dirty clothes (2:2), and now a brother or sister without food or clothing. It is one example among many possible good works, but it is a telling one.

B. A Brother or Sister in Need

The needy person is “a brother or sister,” a fellow believer. And the need is serious. “Without clothing” (gymnos) can mean poorly clothed, lacking what is needed for warmth. “In need of daily food” (ephēmeros) means lacking even today’s meal. These are life-threatening needs.

C. Words Without Help

“One of you says to them, ‘Go in peace, be warmed and be filled.’” Notice that the speaker is also “one of you,” a believer. And he says all the right things. “Go in peace” was a common Jewish farewell. “Be warmed and be filled” sounds kind. It may even be a kind of prayer. But “you do not give them what is necessary for their body.” He has the means to help, and he sends them away empty. John asks the same question:

“But whoever has the world’s goods, and sees his brother in need and closes his heart against him, how does the love of God abide in him?”

1 John 3:17 (NASB95)

“Little children, let us not love with word or with tongue, but in deed and truth.”

1 John 3:18 (NASB95)

And Proverbs had long warned: “Do not say to your neighbor, ‘Go, and come back, and tomorrow I will give it,’ when you have it with you” (Proverbs 3:28).

D. “What Use Is That?”

James repeats his question: “What use is that?” The answer is obvious: none. The brother is still cold and hungry. The kind words did nothing for him. And the speaker gained nothing either; he missed an opportunity to serve the Lord. Jesus said that whatever is done for the least of His brothers is done for Him (Matthew 25:40). Doing nothing gets nothing.

VI. The Thesis Stated (2:17)

“Even so faith, if it has no works, is dead, being by itself.”

James 2:17 (NASB95)

Now James applies the illustration. Just as words without help are useless to the hungry brother, so faith without works is “dead, being by itself.”

A. What James Does Not Say

  • He does not say that faith does not exist in the man who has no works.
  • He does not say that this man never had faith.
  • He does not say that the man does not now have faith.
  • He does not say that the man himself is dead, spiritually or otherwise. It is his faith that is dead.

B. What James Does Say

He says that the man has faith, and that because his faith is not doing anything, it is dead. Literally, it is dead “by itself” (kath’ heautēn), on its own, cut off from the activity that would make it useful.

C. What “Dead” Means

“Dead” does not have to mean “never alive.” We use the word figuratively all the time: a dead battery, a dead letter, dead silence, dead capital (money that earns nothing), a dead law still on the books but never enforced. In each case, something real has stopped functioning or producing. One writer put it this way: dead not as to its existence, but as to its effect.

The Bible uses “dead” in the same way:

ReferenceWhat is “dead”
Romans 4:19Abraham’s body “as good as dead,” and the deadness of Sarah’s womb: alive, but unable to produce children
Luke 15:24The prodigal son “was dead and has come to life again”: alive, but cut off from his father
Hebrews 9:14“Dead works”: real actions that produce nothing for God
Revelation 3:1The church at Sardis: “you have a name that you are alive, but you are dead”

James will finish the section with his own picture: “just as the body without the spirit is dead, so also faith without works is dead” (2:26). A body without the spirit is a corpse. It is still a real body, but it does nothing. That is faith without works: real, but inactive, unproductive, useless to the needy brother and to the believer himself. It is the same point James made with the forgetful hearer (1:23–24) and the worthless religion (1:26).

Once we stop assuming that “death” in James must mean hell, the passage makes sense. The believer whose faith has no works is not lost. But his faith is doing nothing, for anyone.

VII. Two Kinds of Salvation

It helps to set the two kinds of “salvation” side by side. The New Testament speaks of both, and confusion between them causes much of the misunderstanding of James.

Deliverance from sin’s penalty (justification)Deliverance from sin’s power and ruin (James 2)
Justification before GodVindication before people (2:24)
Faith seen only by GodWorks that show faith to others
Saved from eternal judgmentSaved from sin’s consequences in daily life
Sin judged at the crossLife’s work evaluated at the Judgment Seat (2:12)
Eternal lifeThe crown of life (1:12)
One condition: believe in JesusMany conditions: hearing, doing, enduring
A free giftA reward
Always acceptedMay or may not be approved (1:12)
A child of God and heir of GodA faithful servant, co-heir with Christ (2:5)
Received instantly; can never be lostGained progressively; rewards can be lost

James is writing about the right-hand column. He never says that a believer can lose the left-hand column. That is why Paul and James do not contradict each other. They are answering different questions (cf. Joseph C. Dillow, The Reign of the Servant Kings, 2nd ed. [Schoettle Publishing, 2011]).

VIII. How Paul and James Fit Together

Some readers think Paul and James contradict each other. Paul says, “we maintain that a man is justified by faith apart from works of the Law” (Romans 3:28). James will say, “a man is justified by works and not by faith alone” (2:24). But when we notice the questions each man is answering, the conflict disappears.

Paul (Romans 4; Galatians 2)James (James 2)
The questionHow does a sinner receive eternal life and right standing before God?What use is a believer’s faith if it is not put to work?
The audienceAnyone who would be justified before God“My brethren,” people already born again (1:18)
“Works” meansWorks done to earn salvationWorks of love done by believers
“Justified” meansDeclared righteous before GodShown to be righteous before people (2:24)
“Save” meansDeliverance from eternal judgmentDeliverance of a life from sin’s ruin (1:21; 5:20)
Key exampleAbraham believed God (Genesis 15:6)Abraham offered Isaac, years later (Genesis 22)

Paul and James even use the same Old Testament verse, Genesis 15:6, “Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness” (Romans 4:3; James 2:23). Paul uses it to show how Abraham was declared righteous before God, by faith, apart from works. James uses it to show how that same faith, put to work years later on Mount Moriah, was “perfected” and displayed before others (2:21–23).

When James and Paul met in Jerusalem, they did not argue. James, Peter and John gave Paul and Barnabas “the right hand of fellowship” (Galatians 2:9). They preached the same gospel. Paul himself urged believers to good works as strongly as James did: “those who have believed God will be careful to engage in good deeds” (Titus 3:8), and “our people must also learn to engage in good deeds to meet pressing needs, so that they will not be unfruitful” (Titus 3:14). Notice Paul’s word unfruitful. That is exactly James’s concern.

Conclusion: Put Your Faith to Work

James asks, “What use is it?” Faith that never moves into action is like a warm wish spoken to a hungry brother: kind-sounding, but useless. It does not cost the believer his eternal life, which is secure in Christ. But it does cost him the blessing, usefulness and reward that God intends.

Paul’s encouragement fits perfectly: “Let us not lose heart in doing good, for in due time we will reap if we do not grow weary. So then, while we have opportunity, let us do good to all people, and especially to those who are of the household of the faith” (Galatians 6:9–10). And God does not forget: “God is not unjust so as to forget your work and the love which you have shown toward His name, in having ministered and in still ministering to the saints” (Hebrews 6:10).

Lord, thank You.

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. Why is it important to settle how eternal life is received (Romans 4:5; John 6:47) before reading James 2?

Show Leader Answer

If eternal life is a free gift by faith alone, James cannot be teaching that works are required to receive or keep it. That forces us to ask what James really means by “save.”

2. How does the “works to prove eternal life” view affect a believer’s assurance? How does the Free Grace view differ?

Show Leader Answer

It makes assurance depend on examining one’s works, which are never perfect, so doubt remains. The Free Grace view rests assurance on Christ’s promise alone, while still calling believers to good works for reward and usefulness.

3. In the illustration (2:15–16), who is helped and who is harmed by the believer’s empty words?

Show Leader Answer

No one is helped. The needy brother stays cold and hungry, and the believer loses the opportunity and reward of serving Christ (Matthew 25:40).

4. What everyday examples help explain what “dead” means in 2:17? How does Scripture use the word figuratively?

Show Leader Answer

A dead battery, dead letter, dead capital. Scripture: Abraham’s body “as good as dead,” the prodigal “dead,” “dead works,” Sardis “dead.” In each case something real is not functioning.

5. What practical “good works” might God have prepared for you (Ephesians 2:10)? What keeps you from doing them?

Show Leader Answer

Answers will vary. Common obstacles: busyness, fear, selfishness, not noticing needs. Encourage one specific step.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Rest in the Gift

John 6:47; Romans 4:5; James 2:14

Source: Commentary ③ — I

Before we can serve freely, we must be sure of what we have. Eternal life is a free gift, received by faith alone, and it cannot be lost.

Reflection: Have I ever doubted my salvation because of James 2?

Practical Step: Write out John 6:47 and Romans 4:5 and keep them where you will see them this week.

Answer Needs with Help

James 2:15–16; 1 John 3:17–18

Source: Commentary ③ — V

Kind words are good, but not when we have the means to help. Love acts.

Reflection: What need have I recently answered with words only?

Practical Step: Go back to that person this week with practical help.

Start the Dead Battery

James 2:17; Ephesians 2:10

Source: Commentary ③ — VI

Faith that is real but idle does no one any good. God has prepared good works for each of us.

Reflection: In what area is my faith believed but not lived?

Practical Step: Ask God each morning this week to show you one good work He has prepared, and do it.

Do Not Grow Weary

Galatians 6:9–10; Hebrews 6:10

Source: Commentary ③ — Conclusion; VII

Works done in faith are never wasted. God remembers and will reward them.

Reflection: Have I grown tired of doing good because no one seems to notice?

Practical Step: Thank God that He sees, and commit to one act of service this week that no one else will know about.
