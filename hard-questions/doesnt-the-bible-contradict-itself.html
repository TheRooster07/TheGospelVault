Doesn’t the Bible Contradict Itself?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Most apparent contradictions dissolve with attention to context, genre, and the way honest eyewitnesses report the same event. None touches a core doctrine of the faith.

Key verses1 Corinthians 15:6 · 1 Thessalonians 5:21 · Matthew 1:17

Skeptics frequently cite apparent contradictions as evidence against Scripture’s reliability, and a fair response requires distinguishing genuine logical contradictions (which Scripture, properly understood, does not contain) from apparent discrepancies that dissolve under closer examination of context, genre, and the ordinary way multiple witnesses describe the same event.

Many alleged contradictions arise from failing to distinguish complementary accounts from contradictory ones. The Gospels, for instance, describe the same events from different vantage points, with different emphases and different selections of detail for different original audiences — much as two honest newspaper reporters covering the same event might emphasize different details without either being wrong. Matthew and Mark each mention one angel at the empty tomb (Matthew 28:2–5; Mark 16:5), while Luke and John mention two (Luke 24:4; John 20:12); this is the same kind of variation any careful historian expects from independent, honest witnesses to the same event, not a contradiction, since a writer who mentions one angel does not claim there was only one.

Other apparent difficulties resolve through attention to genre and literary convention. Numbers that seem inflated or precise chronologies that seem to conflict often reflect ancient Near Eastern conventions for counting, rounding, and genealogical compression that differ from modern expectations but were well understood by the original audience — Matthew’s genealogy of Jesus, for example, deliberately compresses generations into three sets of fourteen for a clear, memorable structure (Matthew 1:17), a common and accepted ancient practice, not a factual error.

Difficult passages remain — questions about precise reconciliation of certain chronological details across parallel accounts, for instance — and honest engagement with Scripture does not require pretending every apparent difficulty has already been perfectly and exhaustively resolved by scholarship. What it does show, on careful examination across nearly two thousand years of intense scrutiny by both believing and skeptical scholars, is that the vast majority of alleged contradictions dissolve upon closer study, and that no remaining difficulty touches a core doctrine of the faith — the reality of the Fall, the deity and resurrection of Christ, salvation by grace through faith, or the hope of eternal life.

Paul’s own confidence, writing decades after the events he describes and inviting readers to verify his claims against still-living eyewitnesses (1 Corinthians 15:6), reflects the same posture believers can hold today: Scripture invites scrutiny rather than fearing it, confident that careful examination strengthens rather than undermines its trustworthiness. “Test all things; hold fast what is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21) applies to Scripture’s own claims as readily as to any other; two thousand years of testing has left its central message entirely intact.1
