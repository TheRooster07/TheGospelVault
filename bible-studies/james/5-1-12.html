Rusted Gold and the Patient Farmer

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

② Devotional

“You too be patient; strengthen your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is near.” — James 5:8

James pulls back the curtain on wealth. The rich who hoard and oppress think they are secure, but their food rots, their clothes are eaten by moths, and their gold corrodes. What they trusted will testify against them. Money was never meant to be a refuge.

Then James turns to the believers who were being cheated and mistreated. He does not tell them to fight back. He tells them to wait, like a farmer who has planted his seed and watches the sky for rain. The Lord is coming. The Judge is at the door. He will set every wrong right.

Waiting is hard. It tempts us to grumble at one another and to make rash promises. But the prophets waited, and Job endured, and in the end they saw that the Lord is full of compassion and mercy.

Reflection Questions

  1. Where am I trusting money more than God?
  2. What am I waiting on God for right now, and how am I waiting?
  3. Have I been grumbling against the people closest to me?

Lord, make me rich toward You and patient with others, until You come.

Introduction: Money Is Not the Cure-All

Years ago a famous athlete told a magazine that he had everything: a good family, health and plenty of money. Yet he sat alone in his big house and felt lonely and bored. Money, he said, is not the cure-all. He was right, and James would agree.

Chapter 5 has three themes that we might summarize as possessions, patience and prayer. James begins with a blistering word to the rich (5:1–6), turns to the suffering brethren with a call to patience (5:7–12), and ends with prayer and restoration (5:13–20). In this study we take the first two.

I. Weep and Howl: The Misery of the Rich (5:1)

“Come now, you rich, weep and howl for your miseries which are coming upon you.”

James 5:1 (NASB95)

A. Who the Rich Are

“Come now” is the same attention-getter James used for the merchants in 4:13: Listen here! But who are these rich? James has mentioned rich believers before (1:10) and rich men who visit the assembly (2:2). Yet the rich of chapter 5 hoard, cheat their workers and condemn the righteous. And unlike 4:13–17, there is no call to repent, no “brethren,” no word of hope. Only judgment.

The Old Testament prophets often spoke oracles against Babylon, Moab, Philistia and other nations: “Wail, for the day of the Lord is near!” (Isaiah 13:6). Those nations never read the scroll. The prophecies were written for Israel, to warn and comfort God’s people. James does the same. He addresses the unbelieving rich oppressors as if they were present, but he writes for the believers who suffer under them. The message to the brethren is threefold: you will be vindicated; do not envy or imitate the rich; and God will judge all who do these things. It is not so much about the rich themselves as about their wealth and how it is gained and used.

B. Weep and Howl

“Weep” (klaiō) means to burst into tears. “Howl” (ololyzō) is a word that sounds like what it means: to wail aloud, the shriek of people in terror. The prophets used it in the Greek Old Testament: “Awake, drunkards, and weep; and wail” (Joel 1:5). This is not the godly sorrow of 4:9 that leads to restoration. It is the anguish of those who see judgment coming.

C. Miseries Coming

The “miseries” (talaipōria) are already on their way, like a thunderstorm rolling across the hills. The same family of words described the godly grief James called for in 4:9. The rich refuse to be miserable over their sin now, so they will be miserable under God’s judgment later.

II. The Corrosion of Wealth (5:2–3)

“Your riches have rotted and your garments have become moth-eaten. Your gold and your silver have rusted; and their rust will be a witness against you and will consume your flesh like fire. It is in the last days that you have stored up your treasure!”

James 5:2–3 (NASB95)

In James’s world, wealth was stored in four forms: grain and food, fine clothing, precious metals, and flocks and herds. James names the first three and shows that every one of them decays.

A. Rotted Riches

“Your riches have rotted.” This probably refers to food stored in barns. The verb (sēpō) is in the perfect tense: an action already completed with results that remain. James speaks as if the decay has already happened, because in God’s eyes it is certain. Jesus told of the rich fool who tore down his barns to build bigger ones, and God said, “This very night your soul is required of you” (Luke 12:20). “Beware,” Jesus said, “not even when one has an abundance does his life consist of his possessions” (Luke 12:15).

B. Moth-Eaten Garments

Fine clothing was a sign of wealth and a common gift. Naaman brought “ten changes of clothes” along with his silver and gold to Israel (2 Kings 5:5). Paul could say, “I have coveted no one’s silver or gold or clothes” (Acts 20:33). The poor had rough garments; the rich had closets full. And the moths were eating them.

C. Rusted Gold and Silver

Gold and silver do not rust in the ordinary sense. The word (katioō) means to corrode or tarnish. Whatever the process, the point is clear: their treasure is becoming worthless. Worse, the corrosion “will be a witness against you and will consume your flesh like fire.” Unused, hoarded wealth testifies against its owner. Jesus said, “Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy … But store up for yourselves treasures in heaven” (Matthew 6:19–20).

D. Treasure in the Last Days

“It is in the last days that you have stored up your treasure!” The irony is sharp. The last days had already begun with the coming of Christ, and judgment was near. These men were stockpiling wealth at the very moment when it was about to become worthless. For unbelievers, such treasure leads only to judgment. For a believer who imitates them, it means loss at the Judgment Seat, treasure that does not last (1 Corinthians 3:15).

III. The Condemnation of the Rich (5:4–6)

A. Wages Withheld

“Behold, the pay of the laborers who mowed your fields, and which has been withheld by you, cries out against you; and the outcry of those who did the harvesting has reached the ears of the Lord of Sabaoth.”

James 5:4 (NASB95)

The landowners hired day-laborers to mow and harvest, and then held back their pay. The Law of Moses was clear:

“‘You shall not oppress a hired servant who is poor and needy, whether he is one of your countrymen or one of your aliens who is in your land in your towns. You shall give him his wages on his day before the sun sets, for he is poor and sets his heart on it; so that he will not cry against you to the Lord and it become sin in you.”

Deuteronomy 24:14–15 (NASB95)

Two voices cry out to God here: the withheld wages themselves, and the workers who were cheated. And their cry has reached “the ears of the Lord of Sabaoth,” the Lord of hosts, the commander of heaven’s armies. The poor look defenseless, but they are not. The Lord of armies hears them and fights for them.

B. Fattened for Slaughter

“You have lived luxuriously on the earth and led a life of wanton pleasure; you have fattened your hearts in a day of slaughter.”

James 5:5 (NASB95)

At harvest time a landowner might throw a feast, as Nabal did at his sheep-shearing (1 Samuel 25:2–11), while his workers went hungry. It recalls the rich man who “habitually dressed in purple and fine linen, joyously living in splendor every day,” while Lazarus lay at his gate (Luke 16:19–20). James’s picture is grim: they are like cattle eating their fill on the very day they are led to slaughter.

C. The Righteous Condemned

“You have condemned and put to death the righteous man; he does not resist you.”

James 5:6 (NASB95)

The rich used the courts and their power against the righteous poor (cf. 2:6). Some starved; some were ruined by debt; some died. “He does not resist you” means he could not, or would not, fight back. That is what makes the crime so heinous. God is the defender of the defenseless.

D. Wealth Rightly Used

Is James saying that all believers should take a vow of poverty? Not at all. Paul tells the rich “not to be conceited or to fix their hope on the uncertainty of riches, but on God, who richly supplies us with all things to enjoy,” and to be “rich in good works, to be generous and ready to share” (1 Timothy 6:17–18). James calls us to examine three things: our motives for gaining wealth, our methods of gaining it, and our measures for using and sharing it, with the work of God and with the people of God (Galatians 6:10).

IV. Be Patient Until the Lord Comes (5:7–9)

“Therefore be patient, brethren, until the coming of the Lord. The farmer waits for the precious produce of the soil, being patient about it, until it gets the early and late rains.”

James 5:7 (NASB95)

A. What Patience Is

Now James turns to the “brethren.” The word for patience is makrothymeō, from makros (long) and thymos (temper). It is the opposite of being short-tempered. It means bearing trouble without losing self-control, refusing to be provoked, holding back from outbursts. It is not the absence of all anger (Ephesians 4:26), but anger kept on a long leash. Paul uses the same word: “Love is patient” (1 Corinthians 13:4).

We need this patience with difficult people, enemies and friends alike. Some are mean; some simply get on our nerves; some cannot help it. We need it with difficult problems too, whether they come from people or from circumstances beyond anyone’s control. And how long? “Until the coming of the Lord.” That should help. He is coming. The wrongs will be judged. The righteous will be vindicated. You may say, “I want it now!” James says, wait.

The Lord Jesus is the great example. “While being reviled, He did not revile in return; while suffering, He uttered no threats, but kept entrusting Himself to Him who judges righteously” (1 Peter 2:23). Patience is not weakness. It is trust that God will settle the account. “Never take your own revenge, beloved, but leave room for the wrath of God, for it is written, ‘Vengeance is Mine, I will repay,’ says the Lord” (Romans 12:19).

B. The Farmer’s Example

The farmer in Israel planted in the fall, after the early rains softened the ground. He waited through the winter. He needed the late rains in spring to fill out the grain (Deuteronomy 11:14). Then came the harvest. He could not rush it. He did his work and waited on God for the rest. How long should we wait? As long as the Lord does: “The Lord is not slow about His promise, as some count slowness, but is patient toward you” (2 Peter 3:9).

C. Strengthen Your Hearts

“You too be patient; strengthen your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is near.”

James 5:8 (NASB95)

James repeats the call and adds something: “strengthen your hearts.” The word stērizō means to make firm, to fix in place. The heart is the inner person: mind, emotions and will. And the reason is that “the coming of the Lord is near.” The verb (engizō, perfect tense) means it has drawn near and stands close. James lived in expectation that the Lord could come at any time. Believers today live with the same blessed hope: the Lord Himself will descend from heaven, and we who are alive will be caught up to meet Him (1 Thessalonians 4:16–17; Titus 2:13). Nothing must happen first.

Someone may ask how James could say the Lord’s coming was near nearly two thousand years ago. Peter answers: “with the Lord one day is like a thousand years, and a thousand years like one day” (2 Peter 3:8). “Near” does not mean that a date has been set; it means that the coming is the next great event on God’s calendar and could happen at any moment. Every generation of believers is meant to live ready. And there is a reward for doing so: Paul speaks of “the crown of righteousness” laid up “to all who have loved His appearing” (2 Timothy 4:8).

D. Do Not Complain Against One Another

“Do not complain, brethren, against one another, so that you yourselves may not be judged; behold, the Judge is standing right at the door.”

James 5:9 (NASB95)

Here is the limit on patience: it does not grumble. The word stenazō means to groan, whether in a sigh inside or a complaint outside. When believers are under pressure, they tend to turn on each other. James warns that the Judge is “standing right at the door.” All judgment has been given to the Son (John 5:22). For unbelieving oppressors, that is a threat. For grumbling believers, it is a reminder that we too will give an account at His Judgment Seat (2 Corinthians 5:10).

V. Examples of Endurance (5:10–11)

“As an example, brethren, of suffering and patience, take the prophets who spoke in the name of the Lord.”

James 5:10 (NASB95)

A. The Prophets

The prophets spoke for God and suffered for it. Jesus said, “in the same way they persecuted the prophets who were before you” (Matthew 5:12). Stephen asked, “Which one of the prophets did your fathers not persecute?” (Acts 7:52). Jeremiah was beaten and put in stocks (Jeremiah 20:2), shut up in the guard’s court (32:2), and lowered into a muddy cistern (38:6). Yet he kept proclaiming God’s message. If the men who spoke in the Lord’s name suffered, we should not be surprised when we do.

Hebrews 11 remembers others who “experienced mockings and scourgings, yes, also chains and imprisonment,” men “of whom the world was not worthy” (Hebrews 11:36, 38). They did not see every promise fulfilled in their lifetime, but they kept speaking and kept trusting. Their patience was not passive. They went on doing what God had called them to do while they waited for Him to act.

B. Job

“We count those blessed who endured. You have heard of the endurance of Job and have seen the outcome of the Lord’s dealings, that the Lord is full of compassion and is merciful.”

James 5:11 (NASB95)

Notice that James changes words. The prophets are examples of “patience” (makrothymia). Job is an example of “endurance” (hypomonē), remaining under a load without giving up. Perhaps the change is deliberate, for Job was not always calm. He asked why he was born, why he did not die at birth, why he could not die now (Job 3). But he did not abandon his faith in God.

Job was the greatest man of the East, with seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels and a large household (Job 1:3). Satan claimed he served God only for what he got. In one day Job lost everything, and he fell to the ground and worshiped: “The Lord gave and the Lord has taken away. Blessed be the name of the Lord” (Job 1:21). When his own body was struck and his wife said, “Curse God and die!” he answered, “Shall we indeed accept good from God and not accept adversity?” (Job 2:9–10).

And James says, you “have seen the outcome of the Lord’s dealings.” In the end “the Lord increased all that Job had twofold” (Job 42:10). The outcome revealed God’s heart: “the Lord is full of compassion (polysplanchnos) and is merciful.” Those who endure will see it. “We count those blessed who endured.” That echoes the promise of 1:12: the one who perseveres under trial will receive the crown of life.

VI. Above All, Do Not Swear (5:12)

“But above all, my brethren, do not swear, either by heaven or by earth or with any other oath; but your yes is to be yes, and your no, no, so that you may not fall under judgment.”

James 5:12 (NASB95)

At first this verse seems out of place. But it fits the theme. Under pressure and suffering, people are tempted to swear: to call on God’s name to back up a promise, an excuse or a denial. Swearing here does not mean foul language. It means taking an oath. When Peter was afraid, “he denied it with an oath, ‘I do not know the man’” (Matthew 26:72).

In Jesus’ day many used oaths as a way of lying. They swore by heaven or by earth or by Jerusalem, and then argued that some oaths were binding and others were not. Jesus cut through it all: “make no oath at all … But let your statement be, ‘Yes, yes’ or ‘No, no’” (Matthew 5:34, 37). James echoes his brother almost word for word. The issue is not formal oaths in a courtroom, which Scripture elsewhere permits (Hebrews 6:16). The issue is everyday speech. A believer should be so consistently truthful that his word needs no oath to support it. Careless use of God’s name to prop up our words also comes close to taking His name in vain (Exodus 20:7). Let your yes be yes, and your no, no.

Why does James say “above all”? Perhaps because the tongue has been his concern throughout the letter (1:19, 26; 3:1–12; 4:11), and this is its final test. Under pressure we are tempted to grumble (5:9) and to make rash vows to get out of trouble. A believer who waits patiently for the Lord has no need to bargain with oaths. His simple word, kept faithfully, is a witness to the God of truth.

Conclusion: Rich Toward God, Patient Toward Others

James has shown us two ways to live. The rich oppressor stores up treasure that rots, rusts and testifies against him. The patient believer plants, waits, strengthens his heart and keeps his word, looking for the Lord who is near. Every believer already has eternal life as a free gift (John 6:47). The question James presses is how we will use our possessions and how we will wait.

For the believer who is being treated unfairly, this passage is a comfort. God sees the withheld wages. He hears the cry. He is coming, and He will judge righteously. For the believer who has been blessed with plenty, it is a warning and an invitation: do not hoard, do not cheat, do not live as though this world is all there is. Use what God has given to store up treasure in heaven.

The Judge is at the door. The farmer’s harvest is coming. Be rich toward God, generous with others, patient in trouble, and truthful in every word, until He comes.

Lord, thank You.

1. Why would James write to the unbelieving rich in a letter to believers?

Show Leader Answer

Like the prophets’ oracles against the nations, it comforts the oppressed, warns believers not to envy or imitate the rich, and declares that God will judge injustice.

2. How do motives, methods and measures help us evaluate our own use of money?

Show Leader Answer

They ask why we want wealth, how we get it, and how we use and share it. A believer can be wealthy and godly if all three honor God.

3. What does the farmer’s patience look like in a hard situation you face?

Show Leader Answer

Doing what God has given us to do, then waiting on His timing without forcing the outcome or giving up.

4. Why do believers under pressure tend to grumble against each other?

Show Leader Answer

Stress looks for a target, and those closest are easiest to blame. James reminds us that the Judge is near and we will give an account.

5. How does Job’s outcome encourage those who are suffering now?

Show Leader Answer

It shows that God’s purposes end in compassion and mercy. Those who endure are counted blessed and will see His goodness.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Check Your Treasure

James 5:1–3; Matthew 6:19–20

Source: Commentary ③ — I–II

Earthly wealth rots, rusts and decays. Only what is invested in God’s purposes lasts.

Reflection: Where am I storing up treasure that will not last?

Practical Step: Choose one possession or amount of money to give to God’s work or to someone in need this month.

Pay and Treat People Fairly

James 5:4–6; Deuteronomy 24:14–15

Source: Commentary ③ — III

God hears the cry of those who are cheated. Believers should be known for fairness.

Reflection: Is anyone waiting on me for something I owe them?

Practical Step: Settle one debt, bill or promise this week, promptly and in full.

Wait Like a Farmer

James 5:7–9; 2 Peter 3:9

Source: Commentary ③ — IV

The Lord is coming. Do your part, strengthen your heart, and do not grumble at those around you.

Reflection: Whom have I been complaining about while I wait?

Practical Step: Replace one complaint each day this week with a prayer for that person.

Let Your Yes Be Yes

James 5:12; Matthew 5:37

Source: Commentary ③ — VI

A believer’s word should be so reliable that it needs no oath.

Reflection: Do I exaggerate or make promises I do not keep?

Practical Step: Keep one promise you have been putting off, and speak plainly without overstatement this week.
