Why Are There So Many Bible Translations, and Which Is Best?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Translations differ mainly in method — word-for-word or thought-for-thought — not in doctrine. A formal translation serves close study best, and comparing several is wise.

The multiplicity of English Bible translations reflects a genuine translation challenge rather than confusion or corruption of the text: Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek do not map onto English word-for-word, and translators must choose, sentence by sentence, how closely to preserve the original’s exact wording and structure versus how clearly to convey its meaning in natural, readable English. Different translations sit at different points along this spectrum, and understanding that spectrum resolves most of the apparent confusion about why they differ.

At one end sit “formal equivalence” translations, which prioritize word-for-word correspondence to the original languages as closely as English grammar allows — the New American Standard Bible (NASB) and the New King James Version (NKJV) are prominent examples, valued especially for detailed word studies and close textual analysis, since they preserve more of the original sentence structure and word choice even at some cost to natural English flow. At the other end sit “dynamic” or “functional equivalence” translations, which prioritize conveying the meaning of each thought or phrase in the clearest possible natural English, sometimes restructuring sentences significantly — the New Living Translation (NLT) is a clear example. Many widely used translations, including the New International Version (NIV) and the English Standard Version (ESV), occupy a middle position, balancing accuracy to the original wording with readability.

A specialized category worth noting is the New English Translation (NET Bible), produced by scholars associated with the Dallas Theological Seminary tradition represented throughout this paper’s sources, which includes over 60,000 translators’ notes explaining specific textual and interpretive decisions — making it unusually valuable for serious personal study, since it shows its work rather than simply presenting a finished translation without explanation.1

None of this variety indicates that the underlying text is uncertain or that translators disagree about what the Bible actually says at any doctrinally significant point; the manuscript evidence (see Q6, “How Do We Know the Bible Is Trustworthy?”) is remarkably consistent, and no textual variant among the thousands of manuscripts affects any major Christian doctrine. The differences among translations are almost entirely questions of English style and translation philosophy, not disputes about the underlying Hebrew or Greek text.

Which translation is “best” depends on purpose rather than having one universally correct answer: a formal equivalence translation like the NASB or NKJV serves careful word study and detailed exegesis well, a dynamic translation like the NLT serves devotional reading and readability for newer believers well, and a well-annotated study translation like the NET Bible serves those wanting to understand the reasoning behind translation choices. Most serious students of Scripture benefit from consulting more than one translation rather than relying on a single one exclusively, since comparing translations often illuminates nuances that no single English rendering can fully capture on its own.2
