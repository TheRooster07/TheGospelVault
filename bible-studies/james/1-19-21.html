The Word That Saves a Life

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

① The Biblical Text

20 for the anger of man does not achieve the righteousness of God.

Everyone reads before anything else.

② Devotional

“In humility receive the word implanted, which is able to save your souls.” — James 1:21

God gave us new life through His Word (1:18). Now James tells us how to keep growing by it: be quick to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger. Most of us are the other way around. We are quick to talk, slow to listen, and easily stirred up, and an angry, noisy heart cannot hear God.

So James calls us to clear away what clutters the soil: moral filth and the leftover habits of the old life. Then we receive the Word with humility, as a gift from God and a message meant for us. The Word was planted in us when we believed. It has the power to grow and to rescue our lives from the damage sin does.

Being saved from hell is not the issue here; believers already have eternal life. The issue is whether our lives will be preserved, healthy and fruitful, or wasted. The difference is how we receive the Word.

Reflection Questions

  1. When I read or hear the Word, am I more eager to listen or to respond?
  2. What anger or clutter in my heart makes it hard to hear God right now?
  3. What would it look like to receive this week’s Scripture “in humility”?

Lord, plant Your Word deep in me, and let it rescue my life from sin’s ruin.

Introduction: Born by the Word, Growing by the Word

Few things are more needed in our day than a real knowledge of God’s Word. Biblical illiteracy is widespread, not only in the culture but in the church. Many believers own several Bibles and know very little of what is in them.

James talks about the Word again and again in this part of his letter. He calls it “the word of truth” (1:18), “the word implanted” (1:21), simply “the word” (1:22–23), and “the perfect law, the law of liberty” (1:25). His logic is simple:

  • We were born by the Word. “He brought us forth by the word of truth” (1:18).
  • We must keep hearing the Word. Jesus said, “Take care how you listen” (Luke 8:18).
  • We must know the Word. Knowing does not guarantee growing, but we cannot grow without knowing. “If you continue in My word, then you are truly disciples of Mine; and you will know the truth, and the truth will make you free” (John 8:31–32).
  • We must grow by the Word. The same Word that gave us life is the food that makes us grow (1 Peter 2:2).

In this study James tells us how to receive that Word, what the Word is, and why it matters so much.

I. The Key Verse of the Letter (1:19–20)

“This you know, my beloved brethren. But everyone must be quick to hear, slow to speak and slow to anger;”

James 1:19 (NASB95)

“for the anger of man does not achieve the righteousness of God.”

James 1:20 (NASB95)

In Study 01 we saw that 1:19 is the hinge of the whole letter. The body of James takes up its three phrases one at a time: quick to hear (1:21–2:26), slow to speak (3:1–18), and slow to anger (4:1–5:6). But before it is an outline, it is a command, and its first application is to the way we receive God’s Word.

“This you know” (iste) can be read as a statement or as a command (“Know this”). Either way, James is pointing back to what he has just said: God gave you new birth by His Word. Since that is true, here is how to treat that Word. He is not giving lessons in social manners. He is writing to “my beloved brethren” about how to hear God.

Notice the order. Our natural tendency is exactly the reverse: quick to speak, slow to hear, and quick to anger. God’s Word turns that around.

II. Receive the Word with a Listening Heart (1:19a)

“Everyone must be quick to hear.” The word tachys means swift, ready. We are to be eager listeners, quick to give the Word our attention.

A. Listening Requires Preparation

In the parable of the sower, Jesus described four kinds of soil, and the seed is the same in every case: “the seed is the word of God” (Luke 8:11). The difference is the ground. The hard path does not receive the seed; the rocky soil does not nourish it; the thorny ground does not keep it. Only the good soil receives it, keeps it and bears fruit:

“But the seed in the good soil, these are the ones who have heard the word in an honest and good heart, and hold it fast, and bear fruit with perseverance.”

Luke 8:15 (NASB95)

Jesus’ concern in the parable is fruitfulness, and He ends it with a warning about how we listen:

“So take care how you listen; for whoever has, to him more shall be given; and whoever does not have, even what he thinks he has shall be taken away from him.’”

Luke 8:18 (NASB95)

So preparation matters. We can prepare physically, by being rested enough to pay attention. We can prepare emotionally, by setting aside our worries long enough to hear what God has to say about them. And we can prepare spiritually, by reminding ourselves whose Word it is. Solomon gave the same counsel long ago: “draw near to listen rather than to offer the sacrifice of fools” (Ecclesiastes 5:1).

B. Listening Requires Concentration

Good listening is active. Follow the passage in your own Bible. Use the outline, if there is one, to track where the teaching is going. Take notes. Ask, “What is this passage actually saying?” The Bereans were commended because “they received the word with great eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so” (Acts 17:11).

C. Listening Requires Application

The goal is not information but transformation. The key question is, “How does this passage apply to my life?” James will press this hard in the next study: “prove yourselves doers of the word, and not merely hearers” (1:22).

D. Listening Requires Repetition

Go back over what you heard as soon as you can. Talk about it. Share it with someone else. The Word is a strange kind of treasure: when you give it away, you still have it, and the more you give, the more you seem to have.

III. Receive the Word with a Humble Heart (1:19b)

“Slow to speak.” The word bradys means slow, unhurried. Proverbs says it well:

“Even a fool, when he keeps silent, is considered wise; When he closes his lips, he is considered prudent.”

Proverbs 17:28 (NASB95)

“He who gives an answer before he hears, It is folly and shame to him.”

Proverbs 18:13 (NASB95)

A. The Setting in the Early Assemblies

The early Jewish-Christian gatherings were more like a synagogue Bible study than a modern sermon. People could ask questions and make comments. That had real benefits, but also real dangers. Some spoke up to show how much they knew. Some wanted to embarrass the teacher. Some wanted to push a favorite doctrine. Some were simply thinking about what they were going to say instead of listening to what was being said. Paul had to bring order to similar gatherings in Corinth (1 Corinthians 14:26–33).

B. You Cannot Learn While You Talk

James’s remedy is simple: be slow to speak. Nobody learns anything while his own mouth is moving. Humility listens first and speaks later. It gives the teacher, and above all the Lord, a chance to teach. This also prepares for chapter 3, where James will warn that not many should become teachers, because the tongue is so hard to control (3:1–2).

IV. Receive the Word with a Peaceful Heart (1:19c–20)

A. What Anger Is

“Slow to anger.” The word orgē describes a settled, smoldering anger, a strong feeling of hostility. Jesus treated it seriously, warning that anger toward a brother is the first step on the road to murder (Matthew 5:21–22).

Is all anger wrong? No. Scripture speaks of righteous anger: “Be angry, and yet do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger” (Ephesians 4:26). But even then it comes with a time limit and a warning. James does not say “never be angry.” He says “slow to anger.”

B. Why Anger Blocks the Word

James gives the reason: “for the anger of man does not achieve the righteousness of God.” Human anger does not produce the kind of right living God desires, in our own lives or in the church.

These early believers had a real problem here. James will later ask, “What is the source of quarrels and conflicts among you?” and he answers, “your pleasures that wage war in your members” (4:1–2). No one can receive the Word well in that atmosphere. An angry heart is too loud to hear God. It argues with the sermon instead of receiving it.

C. Removing Anger

When anger comes from a conflict with another believer, Jesus gave a clear path for resolving it:

“‘If your brother sins, go and show him his fault in private; if he listens to you, you have won your brother.”

Matthew 18:15 (NASB95)

“But if he does not listen to you, take one or two more with you, so that by the mouth of two or three witnesses every fact may be confirmed.”

Matthew 18:16 (NASB95)

“If he refuses to listen to them, tell it to the church; and if he refuses to listen even to the church, let him be to you as a Gentile and a tax collector.”

Matthew 18:17 (NASB95)

Go privately first. If that fails, take one or two others. If that fails, bring it before the church. And if the person will not listen even to the church, the relationship of fellowship changes. The goal throughout is restoration: “you have won your brother.” Paul adds that this should be done “in a spirit of gentleness” (Galatians 6:1).

If we harbor anger, we will not hear the Word clearly, we will not grow, and we will not produce the righteousness of God in our own lives or in the life of the church.

V. Receive the Word with a Clean Heart (1:21a)

“Therefore, putting aside all filthiness and all that remains of wickedness, in humility receive the word implanted, which is able to save your souls.”

James 1:21 (NASB95)

A. Look Back: “Therefore”

“Therefore” ties verse 21 to what came before. Because human anger does not achieve God’s righteousness, and because God gave us new birth by His Word, here is what we must do. James now broadens the category from anger to every kind of moral filth.

B. Look Inward: Filth and Leftover Wickedness

“Putting aside all filthiness and all that remains of wickedness.” The word rhyparia means dirt, used here for moral filth. The second phrase uses perisseia, an overflow or surplus, and kakia, wickedness or malice. The NASB renders it “all that remains of wickedness”; older versions say “superfluity of naughtiness.” Either way, the idea is the leftover habits of the old life that still cling to the believer. They do not belong in the new life, and they choke the Word.

C. Look Upward: A Holy God

Why does this matter so much? Because the Word comes from a holy God. When God gave the Law at Sinai, the sight was so awesome that “Moses said, ‘I am full of fear and trembling’” (Hebrews 12:21). When Isaiah saw the Lord, he cried, “Woe is me, for I am ruined! Because I am a man of unclean lips” (Isaiah 6:5). We do not receive the Word of a holy God casually.

D. Lay It Aside

“Putting aside” (apotithēmi) is the language of taking off dirty clothes. Paul uses the same picture:

“But now you also, put them all aside: anger, wrath, malice, slander, and abusive speech from your mouth.”

Colossians 3:8 (NASB95)

“Do not lie to one another, since you laid aside the old self with its evil practices,”

Colossians 3:9 (NASB95)

“and have put on the new self who is being renewed to a true knowledge according to the image of the One who created him—”

Colossians 3:10 (NASB95)

Notice Paul’s logic. In Christ, believers have already “laid aside the old self.” So now they should take off its dirty habits. James says the same: put them aside, so that the Word can do its work.

VI. Receive the Word with a Welcoming Heart (1:21b)

“In humility receive.” The verb dechomai means to welcome, to receive a guest. We welcome the Word into our lives the way we would welcome an honored visitor.

A. As from God

When the Word is taught, James wrote it, and a teacher explains it, but God is the One speaking. Paul thanked God that the Thessalonians “accepted it not as the word of men, but for what it really is, the word of God, which also performs its work in you who believe” (1 Thessalonians 2:13).

B. As for Me

Not every verse in the Bible is addressed to me. Some commands were given to Israel under the Mosaic Law, and some promises belong to a particular person or time. But all of Scripture is for me, profitable for my instruction (2 Timothy 3:16; Romans 15:4). The welcoming listener does not let the Word bounce off; he asks what God is saying to him through it.

C. With Humility

“In humility” is prautēs, often translated “meekness” or “gentleness.” It is not weakness. It is strength under control, a teachable spirit that does not argue with God. Jesus used the related word of Himself: “I am gentle and humble in heart” (Matthew 11:29). A humble heart realizes who it is (a creature and a sinner saved by grace) and whose it is (it belongs to God). It is the opposite of the angry, argumentative heart of verse 20.

VII. What We Receive: The Implanted Word (1:21c)

James calls it “the word implanted.” The word emphytos means planted in, rooted in, as a seed is planted in the soil. It looks back to 1:18: God gave us new birth by the word of truth. At that moment the Word was planted in us, in seed form, through the gospel.

A seed is small, but it carries great potential. It must be watered and given room to grow. The new believer receives the Word like a newborn receives milk (1 Peter 2:2), and then grows, through childhood and youth, toward maturity (1 Corinthians 3:1–2; Hebrews 5:14). The Word does not need to be planted again; it needs to be received, nourished and allowed to grow.

“For the word of God is living and active and sharper than any two-edged sword, and piercing as far as the division of soul and spirit, of both joints and marrow, and able to judge the thoughts and intentions of the heart.”

Hebrews 4:12 (NASB95)

VIII. Why We Receive It: To Save Our Lives (1:21d)

James ends the verse with a striking phrase: the Word “is able to save your souls.” Many readers immediately hear that as eternal salvation, deliverance from hell. But that cannot be what James means here. His readers are “beloved brethren” who have already been born again by the Word (1:18). How, then, can the Word save their souls?

A. The Meaning of “Save”

The verb sōzō is used in the New Testament in many ways. It means to rescue or deliver, and the context tells us from what:

Deliverance fromExampleReference
The penalty of sin“By grace you have been saved through faith”Ephesians 2:8
The power of sin in daily life“Work out your salvation with fear and trembling”Philippians 2:12–13
The presence of sin (future)“Salvation is nearer to us than when we believed”Romans 13:11
Illness“So that she will get well and live”Mark 5:23
Physical death“Lord, save me!” (Peter sinking)Matthew 14:30
A wasted life“Whoever loses his life for My sake … will save it”Mark 8:35

B. The Meaning of “Soul”

The word psychē is often translated “soul,” but its most common meaning is simply “life.” It can refer to a person (“seventy souls” went down to Egypt), to the inner person, or very often to one’s physical life. When the angels told Lot, “Escape for your life!” (Genesis 19:17), the word in the Greek Old Testament is psychē. Jesus asked, “Is it lawful … to save a life or to kill?” (Mark 3:4), using psychē. And at the end of this very letter James writes that whoever turns a wandering brother back “will save his soul from death” (5:20), that is, will save his life. Zane Hodges observed that the phrase “save the soul” is never used in the Bible to mean deliverance from hell (cf. Hodges, The Epistle of James: Proven Character Through Testing [Irving, TX: Grace Evangelical Society, 1994]).

C. What the Clause Means

Put the pieces together:

  • The readers already have eternal life. They were born again by the Word (1:18).
  • James has just warned that sin leads to death (1:15). For a believer, that is not the loss of eternal life, but the ruin of his life, and it can end in physical death as God’s discipline (1 Corinthians 11:30; 1 John 5:16).
  • The Word, properly received, is the remedy. It is able to rescue the believer’s life from sin’s deadly consequences. It preserves his physical life from sin’s destruction, and it preserves the spiritual health and fruitfulness of his life with God.

That is why James says to receive it. The question is not whether a believer will go to heaven. That is settled by faith in Christ. The question is whether his life on earth will be preserved, healthy and useful, or damaged and wasted by sin.

IX. Three Pictures of Good Hearers

Scripture gives us living examples of the kind of hearing James describes.

A. Samuel: The Ready Listener

As a boy serving in the tabernacle, Samuel did not yet know the Lord’s voice. When he finally understood that God was calling him, Eli taught him how to answer:

“Then the Lord came and stood and called as at other times, ‘Samuel! Samuel!’ And Samuel said, ‘Speak, for Your servant is listening.’”

1 Samuel 3:10 (NASB95)

That is the posture of being “quick to hear.” Samuel did not say, “Listen, Lord, for Your servant is speaking.” Many of our prayers and Bible readings sound more like that. A good hearer begins with, “Speak, Lord.”

B. Mary of Bethany: The Undistracted Listener

“She had a sister called Mary, who was seated at the Lord’s feet, listening to His word.”

Luke 10:39 (NASB95)

Her sister Martha was busy serving, and there was nothing wrong with serving. But Martha was “worried and bothered about so many things,” and Jesus gently told her, “only one thing is necessary, for Mary has chosen the good part, which shall not be taken away from her” (Luke 10:41–42). Mary had laid aside the distractions, the “thorns” of Jesus’ parable, long enough to listen. Many believers today are Marthas: busy for the Lord but rarely still before Him.

C. The People in Nehemiah’s Day: The Attentive Congregation

After the exile, Ezra read the Law to the people gathered in Jerusalem:

“He read from it before the square which was in front of the Water Gate from early morning until midday, in the presence of men and women, those who could understand; and all the people were attentive to the book of the law.”

Nehemiah 8:3 (NASB95)

“They read from the book, from the law of God, translating to give the sense so that they understood the reading.”

Nehemiah 8:8 (NASB95)

Notice three things. The people stood for hours and were “attentive.” The teachers explained the text, “to give the sense so that they understood.” And the result was deep conviction, then great joy (Nehemiah 8:9–12). That is the pattern for every gathering of God’s people around His Word: careful reading, clear explanation, attentive hearing, and a response of the heart.

The psalmist sums up why it matters: “Your word I have treasured in my heart, that I may not sin against You” (Psalm 119:11). The Word received is the Word that protects.

Conclusion: How Are You Hearing?

James has given us a portrait of a good hearer of the Word:

  • A listening heart: quick to hear, prepared and attentive (1:19a).
  • A humble heart: slow to speak, ready to learn (1:19b).
  • A peaceful heart: slow to anger, free of quarrels (1:19c–20).
  • A clean heart: putting aside moral filth (1:21a).
  • A welcoming heart: receiving the Word with humility as God’s word to us (1:21b).

The implanted Word is able to save our lives. But hearing is only the beginning. In the next study James will press the point: the Word must not only be heard; it must be done.

Lord, thank You.

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. Why do you think we naturally reverse James’s order, becoming quick to speak, slow to hear and quick to anger?

Show Leader Answer

Pride and self-concern. We want to be heard and to defend ourselves. God’s Word humbles us and reverses the order.

2. What practical steps can you take to prepare to hear the Word on Sunday or in your own reading?

Show Leader Answer

Rest; set worries aside; pray; remind yourself it is God’s Word; follow along, take notes, and review it afterward.

3. How does unresolved anger in a church affect its ability to hear God? What does Matthew 18:15–17 teach about resolving it?

Show Leader Answer

It drowns out the Word and stops growth. Jesus gives a path: privately first, then with witnesses, then the church, always aiming to win the brother.

4. Why is it important to see that “save your souls” in 1:21 is not about going to heaven?

Show Leader Answer

The readers are already born again (1:18). Reading it as eternal salvation would make eternal life depend on our receptiveness. James means the Word rescues a believer’s life from sin’s ruin (1:15; 5:20).

5. What does it mean to receive the Word “in humility”? How is that different from simply agreeing with it?

Show Leader Answer

A teachable, submissive spirit that welcomes the Word as God speaking to me and lets it change me, not just nodding along.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Prepare to Hear

James 1:19; Luke 8:18

Source: Commentary ③ — II.A

How we prepare often decides how much we hear. Coming rested, prayerful and expectant makes room for the Word.

Reflection: How do I usually come to church or to my Bible reading: hurried, distracted, or ready?

Practical Step: Before your next time in the Word, pause for two minutes and ask God to make your heart good soil.

Listen More Than You Talk

James 1:19; Proverbs 18:13

Source: Commentary ③ — III.B

Humility listens first. In Bible study and in conversation, slowing our speech gives God room to teach us.

Reflection: In group settings, am I more focused on what I will say or on what is being said?

Practical Step: In your next conversation or study, make it your goal to ask one good question before offering an opinion.

Settle It Before Sunset

James 1:20; Ephesians 4:26; Matthew 18:15

Source: Commentary ③ — IV.C

Unresolved anger blocks the Word. Jesus gave a clear, gentle path for making things right with a brother.

Reflection: Is there someone I am angry with whom I have not gone to privately?

Practical Step: Pray about it, then take the first step of Matthew 18:15 this week.

Let the Word Rescue Your Life

James 1:21; 5:20; 1:15

Source: Commentary ③ — VIII; cf. Hodges, The Epistle of James

Our eternal life is secure. But the Word, humbly received, can rescue our lives from the damage sin does, now.

Reflection: What area of my life is sin damaging right now?

Practical Step: Find one passage that speaks to that area and read it daily this week, asking God to apply it.
