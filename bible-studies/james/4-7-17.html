Submit, Draw Near, and Say “If the Lord Wills”

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

Source: The Gospel Vault · Developed from teaching outlines by Dr. C. Norman Sellers, ThD, used by permission

Leader / Learner Guide

① The Biblical Text

James 4:7–17 · New American Standard Bible (NASB95)

8 Draw near to God and He will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners; and purify your hearts, you double-minded.

② Devotional

“Draw near to God and He will draw near to you.” — James 4:8

After the diagnosis comes the cure. James does not tell worldly believers to try harder. He tells them to come closer. Submit to God. Resist the devil. Draw near, and God will draw near to you. Wash your hands and purify your hearts. Take your sin seriously enough to grieve over it. Then humble yourself, and let God do the lifting.

The cure reaches our relationships too. A humble believer stops tearing down his brother with his tongue. There is only one Lawgiver and Judge, and it is not us.

And the cure reaches our calendars. We make plans as if tomorrow belonged to us, but life is a vapor, here this morning and gone by noon. The humble believer still plans, but he holds his plans with an open hand and says, “If the Lord wills.”

Reflection Questions

  1. Where do I need to submit to God instead of arguing with Him?
  2. Whom have I been judging with my words?
  3. Have I left God out of my plans for tomorrow?

Lord, I draw near to You. Humble me, and let every plan I make begin with “if You will.”

Introduction: The Cure After the Diagnosis

In Study 12 James diagnosed the disease: worldliness, desires at war within, friendship with the world. He ended with the promise that God “gives a greater grace” to the humble (4:6). Now he writes the prescription. Verses 7–10 contain ten commands in a row, all in the aorist tense: decisive actions to be taken now.

Some read these verses as an invitation to unbelievers to be saved. But nothing in the context signals that James has changed audiences. He has just spoken of the Spirit whom God “has made to dwell in us” (4:5), and in the next breath he calls his readers “brethren” (4:11). There is also no call to believe in Christ for eternal life, which is the one condition of the gospel (John 6:47). This is a call to believers to return to fellowship with God (cf. Andy Woods, James series, Spirit and Truth, 2021).

That matters for how we read words like “repent” and “mourn.” Repentance, in the sense of sorrow for sin and turning from it, is not a condition for receiving eternal life. But it is something believers are called to again and again. A child of God who has wandered into worldliness does not need to be born again. He needs to come home.

I. Get Right with God (4:7–10)

“Submit therefore to God. Resist the devil and he will flee from you. Draw near to God and He will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners; and purify your hearts, you double-minded.”

James 4:7–8 (NASB95)

A. Submit to God

“Submit therefore to God.” The word “therefore” ties this to verse 6: since God opposes the proud and gives grace to the humble, take the humble position. The verb hypotassō means to arrange oneself under, a military word for falling into rank under a commander.

Submission is not quite the same as obedience. We are called to obey civil authorities, parents and church leaders, and a person can obey outwardly with a proud heart. Submission is the inner yielding of the will to one who is greater. Here, in context, it means turning from the world’s desires to God’s will.

B. Resist the Devil

“Resist the devil and he will flee from you.” Notice the order: submit to God first, then resist the devil. A believer who tries to fight Satan without first submitting to God is fighting in his own strength, and he will lose.

The word anthistēmi means to take a stand against. It is a defensive word. We are not told to go hunting the devil, but to hold our ground when he comes. Paul describes the armor of God, and every piece is for standing firm:

“Therefore, take up the full armor of God, so that you will be able to resist in the evil day, and having done everything, to stand firm.”

Ephesians 6:13 (NASB95)

Every piece of that armor is about truth and trust: the belt of truth, the breastplate of righteousness, the shoes of the gospel of peace, the shield of faith, the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God (Ephesians 6:14–17). Satan works by lies. He tells the worldly believer that the world will satisfy, that God is holding out on him, that his sin does not matter or that it is too great to be forgiven. We resist him the way Jesus did in the wilderness, with “It is written.”

Peter says the same: “Your adversary, the devil, prowls around like a roaring lion … But resist him, firm in your faith” (1 Peter 5:8–9). And the promise is that he will flee. That does not make us stronger than Satan; pride goes before a fall. He flees because the believer is standing under God.

C. Draw Near to God

“Draw near to God and He will draw near to you.” Here is a promise of fellowship. The believer’s standing before God never changes, but his closeness to God in daily experience does. When we move toward Him, He moves toward us.

The first step is confession: “If we confess our sins, He is faithful and righteous to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness” (1 John 1:9). The word homologeō means to say the same thing. To confess is simply to agree with God, to call sin what He calls it.

The Old Testament priests are a picture of this. They came with a sacrifice, but before they entered to serve they stopped at the bronze laver to wash. Jesus showed the same truth when He washed His disciples’ feet: “He who has bathed needs only to wash his feet, but is completely clean” (John 13:10). The believer has been bathed once for all. What he needs day by day is to have his feet washed.

Drawing near is not a burden; it is an invitation. “Therefore let us draw near with confidence to the throne of grace, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need” (Hebrews 4:16). The greater grace of 4:6 is found close to God, not at a distance.

D. Cleanse Your Hands, Purify Your Hearts

“Cleanse your hands, you sinners; and purify your hearts, you double-minded.” Hands stand for outward actions; hearts for inner thoughts and motives. James calls them “sinners,” not because they are unbelievers, but because they are acting like sinners. And he calls them “double-minded” (dipsychos), the same word he used for the wavering man in 1:8, a person with two souls, one turned toward God and one toward the world. Both the outside and the inside need cleaning.

E. Grieve over Sin

“Be miserable and mourn and weep; let your laughter be turned into mourning and your joy to gloom.”

James 4:9 (NASB95)

This is not a call to permanent gloom. It is a call to take sin seriously. The word “be miserable” (talaipōreō) means to feel wretched. “Mourn” is the grief of one who has lost something precious. “Weep” is the outward sign of that grief. Laughter is not wrong in itself, but there is a time when it is out of place. Solomon spoke of “the laughter of the fool,” like “the crackling of thorn bushes under a pot” (Ecclesiastes 7:6). These believers were laughing their way through spiritual adultery. James calls for genuine sorrow, not a casual apology or mild regret. Paul called it the sorrow “according to the will of God” (2 Corinthians 7:10).

F. Humble Yourselves

“Humble yourselves in the presence of the Lord, and He will exalt you.”

James 4:10 (NASB95)

This verse sums up the rest. To humble yourself is to resist the devil, draw near to God, forsake sinful deeds and thoughts, and grieve honestly over sin. And the promise is sure: “He will exalt you.” The way up is down. The world says, exalt yourself. Jesus said, “everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted” (Luke 14:11). Peter adds, “humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God, that He may exalt you at the proper time” (1 Peter 5:6). Let Him do the lifting.

II. Get Right with Your Brother (4:11–12)

“Do not speak against one another, brethren. He who speaks against a brother or judges his brother, speaks against the law and judges the law; but if you judge the law, you are not a doer of the law but a judge of it.”

James 4:11 (NASB95)

A. Do Not Speak Against a Brother

James returns to the tongue. The word katalaleō means to speak down on someone: slander, run-down talk, criticism behind a person’s back. There will be no end to the quarrels of 4:1 until there is an end to critical and condemning speech. Notice the word “brethren” three times in this one verse. James is talking about how believers speak about each other.

B. Do Not Sit in Judgment on the Law

Why does speaking against a brother mean speaking against the law? Because the law James has in mind is “the royal law,” “You shall love your neighbor as yourself” (2:8). When I tear down my brother, I am breaking that law. And when I break it, I am acting as though it does not matter, as though that part of the law is not worth keeping. I have made myself a judge over the law instead of a doer of it.

There is also a second kind of judging here. When I condemn my brother, I am usually judging his motives, which I cannot see. Then I decide for him which parts of the law he is failing to keep, and for myself which parts I will keep. Either way, I have stepped out of my place.

C. There Is Only One Lawgiver and Judge

“There is only one Lawgiver and Judge, the One who is able to save and to destroy; but who are you who judge your neighbor?”

James 4:12 (NASB95)

Picture a classroom when the teacher steps out. One student goes to the front and starts playing teacher, scolding everyone else. Then the teacher walks back in. But with God, the Judge never steps out. He is always watching. “Who are you to judge the servant of another? To his own master he stands or falls” (Romans 14:4).

God is “able to save and to destroy.” In James, “save” most often refers to deliverance of life (1:21; 5:15, 20), and the contrast here is between preserving a life and taking it. God disciplines His own children, even to the point of physical death in serious cases (1 Corinthians 11:30–32). He does not need our help. This does not forbid all discernment. Jesus told us to remove the log from our own eye first, “and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother’s eye” (Matthew 7:5). What it forbids is the condemning, critical spirit that puts us in God’s seat.

D. Discernment Is Not Condemnation

Does this mean believers may never evaluate anything or anyone? No. James himself has just called his readers adulteresses and sinners. Paul tells the spiritual to restore a brother “caught in any trespass” (Galatians 6:1). The difference is the spirit and the purpose. Discernment looks at conduct in the light of Scripture, with humility, in order to help. Condemnation looks down on a brother, guesses at his motives, and talks about him rather than to him. Jesus warned, “in the way you judge, you will be judged” (Matthew 7:2). The question to ask is simple: am I trying to restore my brother, or to rank myself above him?

III. The Conceit of the Worldly Planner (4:13–17)

Before a famous heavyweight fight in 1971, a famous boxer told the world that no man could beat him, that he was the greatest, the king. He lost that night. It was a public reminder of an old truth: “Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before stumbling” (Proverbs 16:18).

Some think these verses belong with chapter 5 and the rich. But they fit the theme of worldliness that runs through chapter 4. In 4:1–12 James addressed the “have nots,” who wanted much and fought to get it. Now he turns to the “haves”: traveling businessmen. The early church had them. Lydia was a seller of purple from Thyatira (Acts 16:14). Priscilla and Aquila moved from Rome to Corinth to Ephesus and back to Rome, making tents as they went (Acts 18:2, 18–19; Romans 16:3–5).

“Come now, you who say, ‘Today or tomorrow we will go to such and such a city, and spend a year there and engage in business and make a profit.’”

James 4:13 (NASB95)

A. Their Plan

Their plan is detailed. The location: “such and such a city.” You can almost see them putting a pin in a map. The duration: “a year.” The activity: “engage in business,” buy low, sell high. The outcome: “make a profit.” They had it all figured out.

Planning is not the problem. Jesus asked, “which one of you, when he wants to build a tower, does not first sit down and calculate the cost?” (Luke 14:28). The saying is true: when you fail to plan, you plan to fail. The problem is the way they planned.

B. Their Problem: They Left God Out

The only One who knows the future was never consulted. Solomon warned, “Do not boast about tomorrow, for you do not know what a day may bring forth” (Proverbs 27:1). Jesus told of a rich farmer who planned bigger barns and said to his soul, “take your ease, eat, drink and be merry.” But God said, “You fool! This very night your soul is required of you” (Luke 12:19–20).

C. What They Forgot: Life Is a Vapor

“Yet you do not know what your life will be like tomorrow. You are just a vapor that appears for a little while and then vanishes away.”

James 4:14 (NASB95)

Two things they ignored. First, earthly life is uncertain. Weather changes. Markets fall. A traveler can get sick or robbed on the road. You drive to work on an ordinary morning, and in a moment your life is turned upside down. Don’t count your chickens before they hatch.

Second, earthly life is fleeting. “You are just a vapor (atmis) that appears for a little while and then vanishes away.” A puff of smoke, the steam from a tea kettle, morning mist that is gone by noon. The psalmist said, “every man at his best is a mere breath” (Psalm 39:5), and prayed, “teach us to number our days, that we may present to You a heart of wisdom” (Psalm 90:12).

Our health, our wealth, our opportunities could all be gone tomorrow. But some things can never be taken away: our eternal life in Christ, our Savior and our security in Him (John 10:28). Paul learned to look “not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen; for the things which are seen are temporal, but the things which are not seen are eternal” (2 Corinthians 4:18).

Hebrews speaks of a coming day when God will shake heaven and earth, “so that those things which cannot be shaken may remain” (Hebrews 12:27). The merchant’s profit can be shaken. The believer’s life in Christ cannot. That is the perspective that frees us to plan without presumption. We are only sinners saved by grace; everything we have has been given to us, and we are stewards of it for a little while.

D. What They Should Say: “If the Lord Wills”

“Instead, you ought to say, ‘If the Lord wills, we will live and also do this or that.’”

James 4:15 (NASB95)

The planners should have laid their plans before the Lord, acknowledging that they depend on Him and will do nothing without His approval. Paul spoke this way often. Leaving Ephesus, he said, “I will return to you again if God wills” (Acts 18:21). To the Corinthians: “I will come to you soon, if the Lord wills” (1 Corinthians 4:19), and “I hope to remain with you for some time, if the Lord permits” (1 Corinthians 16:7). The writer of Hebrews said, “this we will do, if God permits” (Hebrews 6:3).

This is not a magic formula to tack onto every sentence. Paul sometimes stated his plans without it (Acts 19:21; Romans 15:24–28). But the attitude should always be in our hearts. Jesus Himself prayed, “not My will, but Yours be done” (Luke 22:42).

E. Their Boasting Is Evil

“But as it is, you boast in your arrogance; all such boasting is evil” (4:16). The word for arrogance (alazoneia) is plural: repeated displays of self-confidence, the swagger of a man who thinks he controls his future. James calls it what God calls it: evil. It is not only foolish or annoying. It is sin. The only proper boast for a believer is in the Lord: “may it never be that I would boast, except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ” (Galatians 6:14).

F. The Sin of Leaving Good Undone

“Therefore, to one who knows the right thing to do and does not do it, to him it is sin.”

James 4:17 (NASB95)

James closes the section with a principle that reaches far beyond business plans. Sin is not only doing what is wrong; it is also failing to do what we know is right. These readers now knew they should humble themselves, stop judging their brothers and submit their plans to God. To know that and not do it is sin. It is the same truth James has pressed from the start: be doers of the word, and not merely hearers (1:22).

Think of the ways this verse reaches into ordinary life: the word of encouragement we meant to speak, the visit to the widow we kept postponing (1:27), the generous gift we talked ourselves out of, the apology we knew we owed. We often measure our lives by the wrong things we avoided. James asks us also to count the good things we left undone. The goal is not guilt but action. If God has shown you something good to do, do it while it is still today.

Conclusion: Not the Captain of My Soul

In 1888 the poet William Ernest Henley published the famous lines, “I am the master of my fate: I am the captain of my soul.” Many people still live by them. James says the opposite. We are not the masters of our fate; we are a vapor. We are not the captains of our souls; we are servants of the One who holds tomorrow.

Every believer is secure in Christ, with eternal life that no change of fortune can touch. James calls us to live like it: humble before God, gracious toward our brothers, and holding every plan with an open hand. Submit. Draw near. And when you make your plans, say, “If the Lord wills.”

Lord, thank You.

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. How does reading 4:7–10 as addressed to believers change the way we apply it?

Show Leader Answer

It becomes a path back to fellowship, not a test of salvation. Believers who have drifted into worldliness are called to submit, draw near, confess and humble themselves.

2. What is the difference between obeying and submitting?

Show Leader Answer

Obedience can be outward with a proud heart. Submission yields the will to God from the inside.

3. Why is critical talk about a brother so serious?

Show Leader Answer

It breaks the royal law of love, puts us in God’s seat as judge, and fuels the quarrels of 4:1.

4. How can a believer plan wisely without leaving God out?

Show Leader Answer

Plan carefully (Luke 14:28), but lay the plans before God, hold them loosely, and say, “If the Lord wills.”

5. Where might the “sin of omission” in 4:17 apply to you this week?

Show Leader Answer

Answers will vary: a call not made, an apology not given, a gift not shared. Encourage one specific step.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Come Back Close

James 4:7–10; 1 John 1:9

Source: Commentary ③ — I

A believer never loses his standing, but he can drift from fellowship. The way back is submission, confession and humility.

Reflection: Is there sin I have been excusing instead of confessing?

Practical Step: Name it to God today, agree with Him about it, and thank Him for His cleansing.

Stand Firm, Don’t Go Hunting

James 4:7; Ephesians 6:13

Source: Commentary ③ — I.B

We resist the devil by standing under God, not by our own strength.

Reflection: Where is the enemy pressing me hardest right now?

Practical Step: Memorize Ephesians 6:13 this week and pray it when temptation comes.

Stop the Run-Down Talk

James 4:11–12; Romans 14:4

Source: Commentary ③ — II

Critical talk about a brother breaks the royal law and puts us in God’s seat.

Reflection: Whom have I been judging in conversation?

Practical Step: For one week, say nothing about another believer that you would not say in their presence.

Plan with an Open Hand

James 4:13–17; Proverbs 27:1

Source: Commentary ③ — III

Plan carefully, but submit every plan to God, remembering that life is a vapor.

Reflection: Have I left God out of my plans?

Practical Step: Write down your main plans for this month and pray over each one, “If the Lord wills.”
