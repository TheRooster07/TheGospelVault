② Devotional

Drawn from the commentary · for personal reading

As Sure as the Sunrise

“If this fixed order departs from before Me,” declares the Lord, “then the offspring of Israel also will cease from being a nation before Me forever.”
— Jeremiah 31:36 (NASB95)

Tomorrow morning the sun will rise. You did not arrange it, and you cannot stop it. Through Jeremiah, God tied His promise to Israel to that same fixed order. As long as the sun, moon and stars keep their courses, Israel will not cease to be a nation before Him.

History has tested that promise as hard as any promise can be tested. Moses warned that Israel would be scattered among all peoples, and it was, again and again. Empires that conquered Israel disappeared; Israel did not. The prophets said God would gather His people back to their land, and in our own era the world has watched a scattered people return.

If God has kept faith with Israel through all of that, He will keep faith with you. His gifts and His calling are irrevocable. The promise of eternal life He makes to everyone who believes in His Son is as sure as tomorrow’s sunrise.

Reflect

  1. What “fixed order” in your daily life reminds you of God’s faithfulness?
  2. Why do you think God tied His promise to Israel to the sun, moon and stars?
  3. How does God’s faithfulness to Israel strengthen your assurance that He will keep His promise to you?

The God who keeps the stars in their courses keeps His word.

I. Scattered, Just as Moses Said

A. The Warnings of the Covenant

Before Israel ever entered the promised land, Moses laid out the blessings of obedience and the consequences of rebellion. Among the consequences was something no nation would want to hear about itself: exile. “You, however, I will scatter among the nations” (Leviticus 26:33). “The Lord will scatter you among all peoples, from one end of the earth to the other end of the earth” (Deuteronomy 28:64).

64 Moreover, the Lord will scatter you among all peoples, from one end of the earth to the other end of the earth; and there you shall serve other gods, wood and stone, which you or your fathers have not known.

— Deuteronomy 28:64 (NASB95)

The word “scatter” (puts) pictures something flung outward and dispersed. It is not a description of one deportation to one place. It is a picture of a people spread across the whole earth. When Moses spoke, Israel was a nation of former slaves in the wilderness, with no land, no capital and no kingdom. Yet he spoke as if the land, the nation, and its dispersion across the world were all certain.

Moses also described what life in that dispersion would feel like: “Among those nations you shall find no rest, and there will be no resting place for the sole of your foot; but there the Lord will give you a trembling heart, failing of eyes, and despair of soul” (Deuteronomy 28:65). These are hard words. But Moses did not end there. In the same address he promised that when the people turned back, “the Lord your God will restore you from captivity, and have compassion on you, and will gather you again from all the peoples where the Lord your God has scattered you” (Deuteronomy 30:3). Scattering and regathering were announced together, at the very beginning of Israel’s national life.

B. The Dispersion in History

History fulfilled those words more than once. Assyria carried the northern kingdom into exile in 722 BC. Babylon destroyed Jerusalem and took Judah captive in 586 BC. After a partial return under Cyrus, Rome destroyed Jerusalem and the temple in AD 70, as we saw in Study 2, and crushed a second Jewish revolt in AD 135. After that the Romans renamed the city and banned Jews from it. For nearly nineteen centuries the Jewish people lived scattered across Europe, the Middle East, North Africa and eventually the whole world, “from one end of the earth to the other.”

Even in the lands where they settled, the Jewish people often found “no resting place.” England expelled its Jews in 1290. Spain expelled its Jews in 1492, the same year Columbus sailed. Similar expulsions and massacres took place across Europe and beyond, century after century. The Holocaust of the twentieth century, in which about six million Jews were murdered, was the most terrible of all. We read Deuteronomy 28 with sorrow, not with any sense of triumph. Yet we cannot miss that the history unfolded as Moses described it more than three thousand years ago.

C. “Many Days Without King or Sacrifice”

Hosea, writing in the eighth century BC, described that long condition with remarkable precision:

4 For the sons of Israel will remain for many days without king or prince, without sacrifice or sacred pillar and without ephod or household idols.

5 Afterward the sons of Israel will return and seek the Lord their God and David their king; and they will come trembling to the Lord and to His goodness in the last days.

— Hosea 3:4–5 (NASB95)

For “many days” Israel would have no king and no sacrifice, yet also no household idols. That is an exact description of Jewish life since AD 70: no temple, no sacrifices, no Davidic king, and yet a people who did not return to the idolatry that had brought judgment in Hosea’s day. Then comes the promise: “Afterward the sons of Israel will return and seek the Lord their God and David their king.” Bloom notes that predictions like Hosea’s, of a nation restored after long absence, are exactly the kind a later editor would not have dared to invent.1

II. Preserved, Just as God Promised

A. “I Will Not Destroy Them Completely”

The same chapter that warned of scattering also promised preservation. In the middle of the curses, God said:

44 Yet in spite of this, when they are in the land of their enemies, I will not reject them, nor will I so abhor them as to destroy them, breaking My covenant with them; for I am the Lord their God.

— Leviticus 26:44 (NASB95)

Jeremiah repeated the promise as Babylon closed in: “I will destroy completely all the nations where I have scattered you, only I will not destroy you completely” (Jeremiah 30:11). That is a staggering statement. God promised that the nations who scattered Israel would disappear, but Israel would remain.

B. Tied to the Sun, Moon and Stars

In our key passage God binds His promise to creation itself:

35 Thus says the Lord,

Who gives the sun for light by day

And the fixed order of the moon and the stars for light by night,

Who stirs up the sea so that its waves roar;

The Lord of hosts is His name:

36 “If this fixed order departs

From before Me,” declares the Lord,

“Then the offspring of Israel also will cease

From being a nation before Me forever.”

37 Thus says the Lord,

“If the heavens above can be measured

And the foundations of the earth searched out below,

Then I will also cast off all the offspring of Israel

For all that they have done,” declares the Lord.

— Jeremiah 31:35–37 (NASB95)

The phrase “fixed order” (chuqqim, decrees or statutes) refers to the laws God set for the sun, moon and stars. As long as those laws hold, Israel will not cease to be a nation before Him. And as long as the heavens cannot be measured and the foundations of the earth cannot be searched out, God will not cast off Israel “for all that they have done.” God is not saying Israel deserved preservation. He says the opposite. He preserves Israel in spite of what they have done, because of His own covenant faithfulness.2

Behind this promise stands God’s covenant with Abraham. In Genesis 15 God alone passed between the pieces of the covenant sacrifice, as a smoking oven and a flaming torch, while Abraham slept (Genesis 15:17–18). In the ancient world the parties to a covenant walked between the pieces, as if to say, “May this happen to me if I break my word.” Here only God walked through. The covenant depended on Him alone. That is why Israel’s unfaithfulness could bring discipline, even exile, but could never cancel the promise. Dispensational theologians call the Abrahamic covenant unconditional for exactly this reason.

C. The Test of History

Consider what happened to the nations that conquered Israel. Assyria is gone. Babylon is gone. The Philistines, the Edomites, the Moabites and the Ammonites, Israel’s neighbors and enemies, have long since disappeared as distinct peoples. Most nations that lose their land, their capital and their central place of worship are absorbed into others within a few generations.

The Jewish people were scattered for nearly two thousand years, often persecuted, expelled from one country after another, and in the twentieth century targeted for complete destruction in the Holocaust. Yet they remain a distinct people, with their Scriptures, their language and their identity. That survival is not a sentimental point. It is a fulfillment of what God said through Moses and Jeremiah. Paul saw it in his own day: “God has not rejected His people, has He? May it never be! … God has not rejected His people whom He foreknew” (Romans 11:1–2).3

There is another remarkable detail. The people scattered across the world carried with them the very Scriptures that foretold their scattering. Paul says the Jews “were entrusted with the oracles of God” (Romans 3:2). Generation after generation of Jewish scribes copied the Law and the Prophets with extraordinary care, including the chapters that announced their own judgment and dispersion and the passages Christians read as pointing to Jesus. Those who did not believe in Jesus preserved the prophecies that point to Him. No one could accuse the church of having written them after the fact.

III. Regathered, Just as the Prophets Said

A. The Promise of Return

The prophets did not stop at preservation. They promised a return to the land, and not only the return from Babylon. Isaiah speaks of the Lord recovering His people “the second time” and gathering “the dispersed of Judah from the four corners of the earth” (Isaiah 11:11–12). The return from Babylon came from one empire in the east. Isaiah’s second gathering is worldwide. Ezekiel saw the same:

21 Say to them, ‘Thus says the Lord God, “Behold, I will take the sons of Israel from among the nations where they have gone, and I will gather them from every side and bring them into their own land;

22 and I will make them one nation in the land, on the mountains of Israel; and one king will be king for all of them; and they will no longer be two nations and no longer be divided into two kingdoms.

— Ezekiel 37:21–22 (NASB95)

Amos adds a final promise: “I will also plant them on their land, and they will not again be rooted out from their land which I have given them” (Amos 9:15). The gathering (qavats, to collect, assemble) is God’s own work. He says “I will take … I will gather … I will make them one nation.”

Ezekiel is careful to give the reason for the return: “It is not for your sake, O house of Israel, that I am about to act, but for My holy name, which you have profaned among the nations where you went” (Ezekiel 36:22). The regathering is not a reward for Israel’s faithfulness. It is the vindication of God’s name. “Then the nations will know that I am the Lord” (36:23). The return of Israel to the land is meant to be evidence, not first of all about Israel, but about God.

B. A Return Before Our Eyes

In the late nineteenth century Jews began returning to the land in significant numbers. On May 14, 1948, the modern State of Israel was declared, the first independent Jewish state in the land since ancient times. In 1967 Jerusalem came under Jewish control. Millions have returned from scores of nations, from every direction Isaiah named. Hebrew, a language used for centuries mainly in prayer and study, became the everyday language of a nation again. For a people scattered for nearly two thousand years, that is without parallel in history.4

C. A Stage, Not the Final Fulfillment

We need to be careful here. Dispensational interpreters such as Arnold Fruchtenbaum do not say that the modern state is the complete fulfillment of the regathering prophecies. Ezekiel’s vision of the dry bones happens in stages. First the bones come together, and sinews and flesh cover them, “but there was no breath in them” (Ezekiel 37:8). Only afterward does the breath enter and they live (37:9–10). Many dispensational scholars see the present regathering as the first stage: a return to the land largely in unbelief, preparing for a future day when Israel will look on the One whom they pierced and mourn for Him (Zechariah 12:10), and “all Israel will be saved” (Romans 11:26).5

That caution keeps us honest. We do not need to claim more than Scripture says, and we should not treat any modern government as beyond criticism. But we can say plainly that a people scattered as Moses foretold, preserved as Jeremiah promised, is being gathered back to its land as Isaiah and Ezekiel said. Many Reformed (covenant) theologians read these promises as fulfilled spiritually in the church rather than in the nation of Israel.6 This study holds, with Paul in Romans 11, that Israel and the church are distinct and that “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29). God’s promises to Israel mean what they say.

For a believer, the right response to all of this is not political enthusiasm but worship and prayer. Paul, who grieved over Israel’s unbelief, said, “Brethren, my heart’s desire and my prayer to God for them is for their salvation” (Romans 10:1). The same apostle who taught that God has not rejected His people longed for individual Jews to believe in their Messiah now. Fulfilled prophecy about Israel should move us in the same direction: confidence in God’s word, love for the Jewish people, and prayer for the day Zechariah describes.

IV. The Messiah at the Center

A. “All Things Which Are Written About Me”

Israel’s Scriptures do not only foretell the nation’s history. They point to a Person. On the evening of His resurrection Jesus told His disciples:

44 Now He said to them, “These are My words which I spoke to you while I was still with you, that all things which are written about Me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled.”

— Luke 24:44 (NASB95)

Jesus named all three divisions of the Hebrew Bible: the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms (the first book of the Writings). Earlier that day He had walked with two disciples to Emmaus and, “beginning with Moses and with all the prophets, He explained to them the things concerning Himself in all the Scriptures” (Luke 24:27). The word “must” (dei) expresses divine necessity. What was written had to be fulfilled.

Think about how remarkable it is that Jesus chose this method on the day of His resurrection. He could simply have shown His hands and feet. He did that too (Luke 24:39–40). But He also sat with His disciples and opened the Scriptures. The risen Lord grounded their faith in what had been written beforehand. Evidence from fulfilled prophecy was not a second-rate support for faith in His eyes. It was the way He chose to settle His disciples’ hearts.

C. Israel and the Messiah Together

Israel’s preservation and Israel’s Messiah belong together. God preserved the nation so that He could keep His promises to it, and the greatest of those promises is the Messiah. Jesus came first as the suffering Servant, rejected by the nation’s leaders but received by a believing remnant and by Gentiles from every nation (Isaiah 49:6). He will come again as the reigning King, and Israel will look on Him whom they pierced (Zechariah 12:10). The same Scriptures that foretold the scattering and the regathering foretold the Savior.

V. Why This Matters

A. If God Keeps Faith With Israel, He Keeps Faith With You

Paul makes this connection in Romans 9–11. If God’s promises to Israel could fail, how could any believer be sure of His promises to the church? Paul’s answer is that they cannot fail: “the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable” (Romans 11:29). Israel’s survival through scattering, persecution and regathering is a living demonstration that God does not take back what He has promised.

B. A Promise as Sure as the Sunrise

God tied His promise to Israel to the sun and stars. He tied His promise of eternal life to His own word: “he who believes has eternal life” (John 6:47). That promise is not conditioned on our faithfulness any more than His promise to Israel was conditioned on theirs. It rests on His. When you see a sunrise, let it remind you of Jeremiah 31. And when you think of Israel, remember that the God who would not let His people disappear will not let go of you.

Imagine a family heirloom handed down through generations: a letter from a great-grandfather promising each descendant a share in an inheritance. Over the years the family is scattered, some members wander far and some forget the letter entirely. Yet when the time comes, the inheritance is paid, exactly as written, because it never depended on how well the family remembered it. It depended on the one who wrote the letter. That is Israel’s story. And for everyone who believes in Jesus, it is our story too. The inheritance of eternal life depends on the faithfulness of the One who promised it.

Lord, thank You.

What does Isaiah 11:11 mean by gathering “the second time”?A worldwide regathering beyond the return from Babylon.
Why do dispensational interpreters call the present regathering a stage?Ezekiel 37 shows bones gathered before breath enters; the return is largely in unbelief, awaiting Israel’s future turning (Zechariah 12:10; Romans 11:26).
Which three parts of Scripture did Jesus say were fulfilled in Him (Luke 24:44)?The Law of Moses, the Prophets and the Psalms.

Part 2 · Discussion Questions

1. Why is Israel’s survival as a distinct people such a striking fulfillment of prophecy?

Show Leader Note

Most peoples who lose land, capital and temple are absorbed within generations. Israel was scattered for nearly two thousand years and persecuted, yet remains a distinct people — exactly as Leviticus 26:44 and Jeremiah 30:11; 31:35–37 promised.

2. Hosea said Israel would be without sacrifice but also without idols (Hosea 3:4). Why is that detail significant?

Show Leader Note

It fits Jewish life since AD 70 precisely: no temple or sacrifice, yet no return to the idolatry that brought judgment. A vague prophecy could not be checked this closely.

3. How can we speak about modern Israel without overclaiming?

Show Leader Note

Say what Scripture says: scattering, preservation and regathering have happened. Treat 1948 as a stage, not the final fulfillment (Ezekiel 37:8–10), and do not treat any government as beyond criticism.

4. How does the messianic portrait (Micah 5:2; Daniel 9:26; Isaiah 53:5; Psalm 16:10) strengthen the case from prophecy?

Show Leader Note

Independent lines written centuries apart meet in one Person. The cumulative portrait fits only Jesus.

5. How does Romans 11:29 connect God’s faithfulness to Israel with your assurance of eternal life?

Show Leader Note

If God could revoke His calling to Israel, no believer could be sure. Because His gifts and calling are irrevocable, His promise that whoever believes has eternal life (John 6:47) stands.

⑤ Application

Living and sharing what we have studied

Application 1 — Let the Sunrise Preach to You

Jeremiah 31:35–37

From ③ II.B; cf. Fruchtenbaum, Israelology.

God tied His promise to Israel to the fixed order of creation. Each sunrise is a reminder that His promises do not lapse. Let ordinary mornings become reminders of His faithfulness.

Reflection: When do you most doubt that God will keep His word to you?

Practical Step: For seven mornings, read Jeremiah 31:35–37 at sunrise or first thing, and thank God for one promise He has kept.

Application 2 — Pray for Israel

Romans 10:1; 11:25–26

From ③ III.C and IV.C.

Paul’s heart’s desire was for Israel’s salvation (Romans 10:1). The regathering is a stage; the greater fulfillment is a nation that will look on the One they pierced. Believers can pray for that day.

Reflection: Do you think of Israel mainly as a political question or as a people God loves?

Practical Step: Pray Romans 10:1 and Zechariah 12:10 for the Jewish people this week.

Application 3 — Know the Portrait

Luke 24:44

From ③ IV.B; cf. McDowell, More Than a Carpenter; Strobel, The Case for Christ.

Jesus pointed His disciples to the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms. Six anchor texts — Micah 5:2, Daniel 9:26, Isaiah 53:5, Psalm 16:10, Zechariah 12:10, Isaiah 49:6 — are enough to show a friend that the Messiah’s portrait was drawn ahead of time.

Reflection: Which of the six anchor texts could you explain right now?

Practical Step: Learn two of the anchor texts this week and where each is fulfilled.

Application 4 — Rest in an Irrevocable Promise

Romans 11:29; John 6:47

From ③ V.A–B.

God’s calling of Israel is irrevocable, and so is His gift of eternal life. It does not rest on your record but on His. Israel’s survival is a living reminder that God keeps what He promises.

Reflection: Do you treat eternal life as a gift God might take back?

Practical Step: Write Romans 11:29 and John 6:47 side by side and read them together each day this week.

End of Study · Jeremiah 31:35–37
