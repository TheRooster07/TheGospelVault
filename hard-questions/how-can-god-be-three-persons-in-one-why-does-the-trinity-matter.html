50 Hard Questions · Question 23 of 50

How Can God Be Three Persons in One — Why Does the Trinity Matter?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

One God eternally exists as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, each fully God. The gospel depends on it: only God could pay for sin and seal the believer forever.

Key versesDeuteronomy 6:4 · Matthew 3:16–17 · 2 Corinthians 13:14

The Trinity is not a philosophical add-on to Christian belief; it is the shape Scripture itself gives to who God is. One God eternally exists as three distinct Persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — each fully God, none a part of God, and none the same Person as the others. The classic formula distinguishes what is one (God’s being or essence) from what is three (the Persons who share that one being), which is why the doctrine is a mystery to be believed on revealed authority rather than a contradiction to be solved.

The evidence is woven throughout Scripture rather than confined to a single proof text. The Father is called God (1 Peter 1:2), the Son is called God (John 1:1; 20:28; Titus 2:13), and the Spirit is called God (Acts 5:3–4) — yet Scripture insists with equal force that “the Lord our God, the Lord is one” (Deuteronomy 6:4). All three Persons appear distinctly and simultaneously at Jesus’ baptism: the Son is baptized, the Spirit descends as a dove, and the Father speaks from heaven (Matthew 3:16–17). The apostolic benediction addresses all three as the source of grace, love, and communion in a single sentence (2 Corinthians 13:14).

Why does this matter beyond correct doctrine for its own sake? Because the gospel itself depends on it. Only a Person who is fully God could bear the infinite weight of sin’s penalty and rise as the guarantee of resurrection (2 Corinthians 5:21; Romans 1:4); only a Person who is fully God, indwelling every believer, could seal them permanently and guarantee their inheritance (Ephesians 1:13–14; 4:30). If Jesus were merely a great teacher and not God incarnate, His death would carry no more saving weight than any other martyr’s; if the Spirit were merely an impersonal force rather than God Himself dwelling within, the promise of eternal security would rest on something less than God’s own presence. The Trinity is not incidental to salvation — it is the reason salvation actually secures what it promises.

The doctrine also shapes how believers understand God’s own inner life before creation ever existed. Because God has eternally existed as a triune community of Persons in relationship, love is not something God merely does toward creation; it is something true of who God is in Himself, prior to and independent of having anyone to love (John 17:24, “You loved Me before the foundation of the world”). This is affirmed across every branch of historic, orthodox Christianity.1

The Trinity, then, is neither an arithmetic puzzle nor an optional technicality. It is the reason the cross accomplishes what it claims to accomplish, the reason the indwelling Spirit’s seal is unbreakable, and the reason the God who saves is, at the deepest level of His own being, a God who has always been love.
