Mirror, Mirror: Hearing That Becomes Doing

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

② Devotional

“But prove yourselves doers of the word, and not merely hearers who delude themselves.” — James 1:22

It is possible to hear a great deal of Scripture and be changed very little by it. James compares that kind of hearer to a man who glances in a mirror, walks away, and forgets what he looks like. He saw the truth about himself, but it made no difference.

God’s Word is a better mirror. It shows us who we really are in Christ, forgiven, made new and set free, and it shows us how to live accordingly. The believer who looks into it intently, keeps looking, and does what it says will be blessed in what he does.

And what does that doing look like? James keeps it close to home: a controlled tongue, practical care for the helpless, and a life kept clean from the world’s stains. That is religion God calls pure.

Reflection Questions

  1. What did God show me in His Word recently that I have already forgotten?
  2. Is there a gap between what I hear on Sunday and how I speak during the week?
  3. Who are the “orphans and widows” near me, people without support, whom I could help?

Lord, don’t let me walk away from the mirror unchanged.

Introduction: Hear and Heed

In the last study James told us how to receive the Word: with listening, humble, peaceful, clean and welcoming hearts (1:19–21). Now he tells us how to respond to it. The pairs are easy to remember: receive and respond, hear and heed, apprehend and apply.

Spiritual growth begins with hearing the Word, but it must not end there. The Word is able to save our lives (1:21), but only as we do what it says. That is the burden of this passage, and it leads straight into James’s most famous section, on faith and works, in chapter 2. In fact, this passage is the hinge. The “hearer” who does not “do” here is the same kind of person as the believer whose faith has no works in 2:14–26. The subject has not changed, only the illustrations.

I. The Self-Deceived Hearer (1:22)

“But prove yourselves doers of the word, and not merely hearers who delude themselves.”

James 1:22 (NASB95)

A. An Old Lesson

The call to hear and do is not new. Moses told Israel, “the things revealed belong to us and to our sons forever, that we may observe all the words of this law” (Deuteronomy 29:29). God told Ezekiel that the people loved to hear him preach, but treated him like an entertainer:

“Behold, you are to them like a sensual song by one who has a beautiful voice and plays well on an instrument; for they hear your words but they do not practice them.”

Ezekiel 33:32 (NASB95)

Jesus said the same thing many times. When a woman called out a blessing on His mother, He answered:

“But He said, ‘On the contrary, blessed are those who hear the word of God and observe it.’”

Luke 11:28 (NASB95)

And He asked, “Why do you call Me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ and do not do what I say?” (Luke 6:46). His parable of the two builders makes the point unforgettable: the wise man is the one who “hears these words of Mine and acts on them” (Matthew 7:24). And in the Upper Room: “If you know these things, you are blessed if you do them” (John 13:17).

B. Become a Doer

Notice James’s exact wording: “prove yourselves doers” (ginesthe, literally “keep on becoming”). He is not saying, “Do something.” He is saying, “Become something.” A single good deed would be “doing something.” Becoming a doer means a settled pattern, a lifestyle. Some of James’s readers may well have done a good deed here and there. But they had not become doers of the Word.

The word James uses for a hearer, akroatēs, was used of people who audited a class without taking part. Jesus is not looking for auditors. He is looking for learners, disciples who become like their teacher: “everyone, after he has been fully trained, will be like his teacher” (Luke 6:40). His last command was to make disciples, “teaching them to observe all that I commanded you” (Matthew 28:20), not merely to know it.

C. Who Is Fooled?

The hearer who does not do is “deluding himself.” The word paralogizomai means to reason falsely, to talk oneself into a wrong conclusion. Notice what James does not say.

  • He does not say this man deceives other people. Most people can spot a hypocrite eventually.
  • He does not say this man fools God. “Man looks at the outward appearance, but the Lord looks at the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7).
  • He says this man deceives himself.

His reasoning goes something like this:

StepThe self-deceived hearer’s logic
Major premiseAll I need to do to please God is go to church and hear the Word.
Minor premiseI go to church and hear the Word.
ConclusionTherefore I please God.

The logic is valid, and he is content with it. But the major premise is false. Hearing alone does not please God; hearing that leads to doing does. Revelation opens with a blessing that has three parts: “Blessed is he who reads and those who hear the words of the prophecy, and heed the things which are written in it” (Revelation 1:3).

This is not a question of whether he has eternal life. He is a “brother,” and eternal life is a gift received by faith. The question is whether his hearing will profit him. James will use exactly that word in 2:14 and 2:16: “What use is it?”

II. The Forgetful Hearer (1:23–24)

“For if anyone is a hearer of the word and not a doer, he is like a man who looks at his natural face in a mirror;”

James 1:23 (NASB95)

“for once he has looked at himself and gone away, he has immediately forgotten what kind of person he was.”

James 1:24 (NASB95)

A. He Looks in the Mirror

Ancient mirrors were made of polished bronze or copper. They gave a dim, sometimes distorted reflection, but enough for a man to see his face. James says he sees “his natural face,” literally “the face of his birth” (genesis). Some suggest a hint here: the believer who looks into God’s Word sees not only his natural face but the face of his new birth (1:18), what he is in Christ by God’s grace. That is the strongest motivation for doing the Word: we live out who we already are.

B. He Walks Away

“Once he has looked at himself and gone away.” The look was brief. The verb “looked” (katanoeō) can mean to look carefully, but the tense and context suggest a quick glance followed by departure. Many believers do exactly that with the Word. They hear it on Sunday, walk out the door, and do not think about it again until the next Sunday. They take nothing with them.

C. He Forgets

“He has immediately forgotten what kind of person he was.” Imagine checking a mirror, seeing a smudge on your face, and walking off without wiping it away. That is the forgetful hearer. The Word showed him something that needed attention, and nothing changed. Worse, he forgets who he is: a child of God, made new, and goes out to live as he always did.

III. The Faithful Hearer (1:25)

“But one who looks intently at the perfect law, the law of liberty, and abides by it, not having become a forgetful hearer but an effectual doer, this man will be blessed in what he does.”

James 1:25 (NASB95)

A. The Perfect Law of Liberty

“The perfect law, the law of liberty” sounds like a contradiction. How can law mean liberty? James is not speaking of the Mosaic Law as a system for the believer to live under. The Law of Moses was a “yoke which neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear” (Acts 15:10), James’s fellow apostle Peter said at the council James led. Paul calls the old covenant a ministry of “the letter” that “kills” (2 Corinthians 3:6), and adds that no law could ever impart life (Galatians 3:21).

The “law of liberty” is God’s Word as it comes to the believer under the New Covenant: the same “word implanted” of 1:21. God promised that in the New Covenant He would write His law on hearts (Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 8:10). It works from the inside out. Paul calls it “the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus” (Romans 8:2) and “the law of Christ” (Galatians 6:2). James will call it “the royal law” (2:8). It is “perfect” because it is complete and lacking nothing, and it is “of liberty” because it frees rather than enslaves. We obey it not to gain life but because we have it.

B. He Looks Intently

The faithful hearer “looks intently” (parakyptō). The word means to stoop down and peer in, to get a close look. It is used of Peter at the empty tomb:

“But Peter got up and ran to the tomb; stooping and looking in, he saw the linen wrappings only; and he went away to his home, marveling at what had happened.”

Luke 24:12 (NASB95)

And of angels who long to look into the gospel (1 Peter 1:12). That is the kind of look the Word deserves: bending down, examining it closely, wanting to see what is really there.

Careful looking involves three questions. What does it say? (observation). Much misunderstanding comes from careless reading. For example, some take Luke 13:3, “unless you repent, you will all likewise perish,” as a statement about hell. But the context (13:1–5) is about Galileans killed by Pilate and eighteen people crushed by a falling tower; Jesus was warning Israel of physical destruction. What does it mean? (interpretation). That takes study, just as any trade or profession does. Paul told Timothy to be “a workman who does not need to be ashamed, accurately handling the word of truth” (2 Timothy 2:15). What does it mean for me? (application). That is where doing begins.

C. He Keeps On

The faithful hearer “abides by it” (paramenō), remaining beside it, continuing in it. He does not glance and leave. He keeps coming back, keeps looking, keeps doing. He is “not … a forgetful hearer but an effectual doer,” literally “a doer of work.”

D. He Is Blessed in What He Does

“This man will be blessed in what he does.” The blessing (makarios again) is not for hearing; it is in the doing. The person who does the Word finds God’s blessing in the very act of obedience: joy, peace, growth, usefulness, and, at the Judgment Seat of Christ, reward. The way we practice now is the way we will serve in the coming kingdom. This life is the rehearsal.

IV. Worthless Religion (1:26)

“If anyone thinks himself to be religious, and yet does not bridle his tongue but deceives his own heart, this man’s religion is worthless.”

James 1:26 (NASB95)

A. What “Religion” Means

James now gives examples of what “doing the Word” looks like, under the heading of “religion.” The word thrēskeia refers to outward religious practice: worship, ceremony, observance. Some today say “Christianity is not a religion, it’s a relationship.” There is a truth in that, but James is happy to use the word. The question is not whether we practice religion, but whether our religion is the kind God accepts.

Every Jewish reader would have remembered the prophets on this point. Through Isaiah, God told Israel He was weary of their offerings, new moons and Sabbaths, things He Himself had commanded:

“‘Bring your worthless offerings no longer, Incense is an abomination to Me. New moon and sabbath, the calling of assemblies— I cannot endure iniquity and the solemn assembly.”

Isaiah 1:13 (NASB95)

What was wrong? They emphasized the outward and neglected the inward. God told them what He really wanted:

“‘Wash yourselves, make yourselves clean; Remove the evil of your deeds from My sight. Cease to do evil,”

Isaiah 1:16 (NASB95)

“Learn to do good; Seek justice, Reprove the ruthless, Defend the orphan, Plead for the widow.”

Isaiah 1:17 (NASB95)

“He has told you, O man, what is good; And what does the Lord require of you But to do justice, to love kindness, And to walk humbly with your God?”

Micah 6:8 (NASB95)

B. The Untamed Tongue

“If anyone thinks himself to be religious.” His thinking is wrong. He measures himself by external observance: attendance, public prayers, singing, perhaps fasting. But “he does not bridle his tongue.” The word chalinagōgeō means to guide with a bit and bridle, as a rider controls a horse. A horse that cannot be controlled is useless to its owner. James will develop this picture in chapter 3: “If anyone does not stumble in what he says, he is a perfect man, able to bridle the whole body as well” (3:2).

It is fitting that the tongue comes first. James has just said, “slow to speak” (1:19). A believer who sings hymns on Sunday and gossips, curses or tears others down on Monday has not become a doer of the Word.

C. The Self-Deceived Heart

He also “deceives his own heart.” This is the second time James has warned about self-deception (1:22). It is one thing not to be able to trust what others say; it is worse not to be able to trust yourself. The verdict is blunt: “this man’s religion is worthless.” The word mataios means empty, futile, useless. James does not say “somewhat lacking.” He says worthless. All that religious activity, as a measure of his life with God, profits nothing.

V. Pure Religion (1:27)

“Pure and undefiled religion in the sight of our God and Father is this: to visit orphans and widows in their distress, and to keep oneself unstained by the world.”

James 1:27 (NASB95)

A. Pure in Whose Sight?

“Pure and undefiled religion in the sight of our God and Father.” The words “pure” (katharos) and “undefiled” (amiantos) were used of ritual cleanness. James borrows them for something deeper: what God Himself counts as clean. And notice the standard: “in the sight of” (para, beside) God. Put yourself alongside the Father and ask how He treats the helpless. He is “a father of the fatherless and a judge for the widows” (Psalm 68:5). He “executes justice for the orphan and the widow” (Deuteronomy 10:18). Above all, He loved us when we were helpless: “God demonstrates His own love toward us, in that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us” (Romans 5:8).

B. Caring for the Helpless

The first mark of pure religion is “to visit orphans and widows in their distress.” In the ancient world, orphans and widows were the most vulnerable people in society, without a father or husband to provide or protect. Among James’s readers there may have been many, left alone by persecution or by poverty; James will later rebuke rich men who withheld wages and condemned the righteous (5:4–6).

“Visit” (episkeptomai) means far more than a social call. It means to look after, to care for, with a view to relieving their distress. When Jesus raised the widow’s son at Nain, the people said, “God has visited His people!” (Luke 7:16). That is the kind of visiting James means: practical help that changes someone’s situation.

The principle reaches beyond orphans and widows to anyone with a genuine need we can meet. Paul says, “through love serve one another” (Galatians 5:13), and “let us do good to all people, and especially to those who are of the household of the faith” (Galatians 6:10). The church is a family, and families take care of one another.

That does not mean we are responsible to solve every need in the world, or that we should support those who refuse to work. Paul was clear: “if anyone is not willing to work, then he is not to eat, either” (2 Thessalonians 3:10), and families should care for their own first (1 Timothy 5:4). But within those limits, caring for the truly helpless is both our responsibility and our privilege.

C. Unstained by the World

The second mark is “to keep oneself unstained by the world.” The word kosmos originally meant order or arrangement (we get “cosmetics” from it, the arranging of a face). In Scripture it often describes the world-system: the arrangement of human life that leaves God out. John describes its contents: “the lust of the flesh and the lust of the eyes and the boastful pride of life” (1 John 2:16).

“Unstained” (aspilos) means spotless. Peter uses it of Christ, “a lamb unblemished and spotless” (1 Peter 1:19). Every believer has already been set apart to God positionally, the moment he believed. But there is also a practical, progressive sanctification in which we have a part, and we carry it out not in our own strength but in the power God supplies. Two areas need special guarding.

  • Morals. Stay away from the sin, and from the places and people that lead you into it. Stand firm in the evil day (Ephesians 6:13). Stay in the Word: the Word will keep you from sin, or sin will keep you from the Word. When you fail, confess it at once (1 John 1:9).
  • Money. “You cannot serve God and wealth” (Matthew 6:24). “The love of money is a root of all sorts of evil” (1 Timothy 6:10). Some of James’s readers were being dragged into court and defrauded by the rich (2:6; 5:4); the world’s attitude toward money was a stain they needed to avoid.

Notice how the two marks of pure religion belong together. One reaches out (care for the helpless); the other holds back (unstained by the world). Love without holiness becomes worldly; holiness without love becomes cold. God calls us to both.

VI. Looking Ahead: Hearing, Doing, and Faith That Works

This passage prepares us for the most debated section of the letter. In 2:14–26 James will ask, “What use is it, my brethren, if someone says he has faith but he has no works?” The parallels with 1:22–27 are close:

James 1:22–27James 2:14–26
A hearer who is not a doerA believer whose faith has no works
“Deluding themselves” (1:22)“What use is it?” (2:14, 16)
Worthless religion (1:26)Faith that is “dead,” useless (2:17, 20, 26)
Visiting orphans and widows in distress (1:27)A brother or sister without clothing and food (2:15–16)
“Blessed in what he does” (1:25)Faith “perfected” by works (2:22)

Seeing the connection protects us from a serious misreading. In 1:22–27 James is not asking whether his readers have eternal life. He calls them “brethren,” people born again by the Word (1:18). He is asking whether their hearing is doing them, or anyone else, any good. Chapter 2 asks the same question about faith. The issue is not whether faith without works can receive eternal life; Scripture is clear that eternal life is received by faith alone, apart from works (Ephesians 2:8–9). The issue is whether faith that sits idle is of any practical use, to the hungry brother or to the believer himself.

That is why the doer of the Word matters so much. Hearing without doing is like faith without works: real, perhaps, but unprofitable. The Word was given not only to inform us but to transform us, and through us to bless others.

Conclusion: What the Mirror Shows

James has shown us three kinds of hearers and two kinds of religion:

Hearer / religionDescription
The self-deceived hearer (1:22)Thinks hearing alone pleases God
The forgetful hearer (1:23–24)Glances, walks away, and forgets
The faithful hearer (1:25)Looks intently, keeps on, does the Word, and is blessed
Worthless religion (1:26)Outward observance with an untamed tongue
Pure religion (1:27)Care for the helpless and a life unstained by the world

God is working from the inside out. The doer of the Word starts by looking intently at the Word, seeing who he really is in Christ, and then lives it out in his speech, his service and his separation from the world. That is the kind of hearing that profits.

Lord, thank You.

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. Why is the hearer-only described as deceiving himself rather than others or God?

Show Leader Answer

Others may eventually see through him and God sees the heart; he alone is persuaded by his false premise that hearing pleases God.

2. What does the mirror of the Word show a believer that should motivate him to act?

Show Leader Answer

His true condition and his new identity in Christ. Living the Word is living out who he already is.

3. Why does James call the Word “the law of liberty”? How is it different from the Mosaic Law as a way of life?

Show Leader Answer

It is the New Covenant Word written on the heart, working from the inside out; the Law was an unbearable yoke that could not impart life (Acts 15:10; Galatians 3:21).

4. Why might James mention the tongue first as a test of genuine religion?

Show Leader Answer

He has just said “slow to speak” (1:19); the tongue reveals whether the Word has reached the heart, and he will develop this in chapter 3.

5. How do the two parts of pure religion (care for the helpless; unstained by the world) balance each other?

Show Leader Answer

One reaches out in love, the other keeps holy. Love without holiness becomes worldly; holiness without love becomes cold.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Leave the Mirror Changed

James 1:23–25

Source: Commentary ③ — II–III

The Word shows us what needs attention. Doing something about it, right away, is what separates the forgetful hearer from the faithful one.

Reflection: What did the Word last show me that I have done nothing about?

Practical Step: After your next time in the Word, write down one specific thing to do, and do it within 24 hours.

Read Carefully, Then Act

James 1:25; 2 Timothy 2:15

Source: Commentary ③ — III.B

Looking intently means asking what the text says, what it means, and what it means for me. Careless reading leads to careless living.

Reflection: Do I usually skip from reading straight to “what this means to me”?

Practical Step: Take one paragraph of James this week and answer all three questions in writing.

Bridle the Tongue

James 1:26; 3:2

Source: Commentary ③ — IV.B

Our speech shows whether our religion is real. A bridled tongue is one of the clearest signs of a doer of the Word.

Reflection: In what setting is my tongue least controlled?

Practical Step: For one week, before speaking in that setting, ask: Is it true, is it kind, is it necessary?

Visit Someone in Distress

James 1:27; Galatians 6:10

Source: Commentary ③ — V.B

Pure religion looks like practical care for people without support. The church is a family, and families take care of one another.

Reflection: Who in my church or neighborhood is alone and struggling?

Practical Step: Make one concrete visit or act of help this week: a meal, a ride, a repair, or a phone call.
