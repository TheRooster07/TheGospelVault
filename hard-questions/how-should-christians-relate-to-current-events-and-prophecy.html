50 Hard Questions · Question 44 of 50

How Should Christians Relate to Current Events and Prophecy?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Watch with interest, but don’t set dates. Prophecy is given to shape holy, faithful living until Christ comes.

Key versesMark 13:32 · 2 Peter 3:11–12 · 1 John 3:3

Bible prophecy generates enormous curiosity, especially in seasons of geopolitical instability, and Scripture does call believers to watchfulness — but it consistently redirects that watchfulness away from confident date-setting and news-headline speculation and toward sober, faithful living. Jesus was explicit that even He, in His earthly ministry, did not know the timing of end-time events: “of that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father” (Mark 13:32; cf. Matthew 24:36). Any teacher claiming precise knowledge of specific dates contradicts Jesus’ own express statement about the limits of that knowledge.

This does not mean prophecy is unimportant or that current events are irrelevant to it. Scripture presents specific, identifiable trends — the regathering of Israel as a nation, increasing global instability, and a trajectory toward global governance structures described in passages like Daniel 7 and Revelation 13 — as consistent with the general shape of what Scripture anticipates in the run-up to the Tribulation and the Second Coming (see Q42, “What Is the Rapture, and When Will It Happen?”). Watching these trends with sober interest is different from treating any specific current event as a confirmed, datable fulfillment of a specific prophecy, a practice that has repeatedly proven embarrassing and damaging to the church’s credibility whenever confident predictions have failed to materialize on schedule.

The New Testament’s own emphasis, whenever prophecy is discussed, falls overwhelmingly on its practical effect on present godly living rather than on satisfying curiosity about the calendar. Peter, discussing the eventual dissolution of the present created order, asks rhetorically, “what manner of persons ought you to be in holy conduct and godliness, looking for and hastening the coming of the day of God” (2 Peter 3:11–12) — the doctrine’s purpose is transformation, not merely information. John draws the identical conclusion from the doctrine of Christ’s return: “everyone who has this hope in Him purifies himself, just as He is pure” (1 John 3:3).

This means the healthiest posture toward prophecy and current events combines informed interest with real humility about the details and real resistance to the anxiety and sensationalism that often surrounds the subject. Paul specifically instructed a church that had grown anxious and disordered over end-time speculation to instead “work in quietness and eat their own bread” (2 Thessalonians 3:12), addressing confusion about the timing of Christ’s return not with more speculative detail but with a call to ordinary faithfulness in daily responsibilities.

Whatever a believer’s confidence about how near the end may be, Scripture’s consistent counsel is the same regardless of the calendar: “Do business till I come” (Luke 19:13) — faithful stewardship of ordinary life and gospel witness in the present, held in the settled hope that Christ’s promises will be kept exactly as He gave them, on His own timetable rather than ours.
