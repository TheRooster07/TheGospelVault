50 Hard Questions · Question 19 of 50

How Can I Know I’m Truly Saved?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Assurance rests on Jesus’ promise, not on our performance or feelings. If you have believed His promise of eternal life, you have it now.

Key verses1 John 5:13 · John 5:24 · John 6:47

Uncertainty about one’s own salvation is a common and needless burden, almost always caused by looking for assurance in the wrong place. John states his purpose in writing an entire epistle plainly: “These things I have written to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, that you may know that you have eternal life” (1 John 5:13). Assurance is the ordinary birthright of everyone who believes, not an advanced achievement.

The ground of that assurance is a promise, not a performance: “he who believes in Me has everlasting life” (John 6:47); “he who hears My word and believes in Him who sent Me has everlasting life, and shall not come into judgment, but has passed from death into life” (John 5:24). Notice the tense: has, present and settled. If eternal life is a present possession the moment a person believes, assurance can rest on the trustworthiness of Christ’s promise rather than on the shifting evidence of one’s own behavior.

Robert Wilkin made this the center of his ministry, arguing that assurance is not a bonus added after sufficient spiritual growth but is built into the content of what a person believes when they believe the gospel rightly.1 This raises the crucial question: what is the content of saving faith? It is not merely believing generic facts about Jesus while remaining uncertain those facts secure anything personally — it is believing Jesus’ promise of life — that whoever believes in Him receives everlasting life as a guaranteed gift — with His death, burial, and resurrection standing as the ultimate picture of sacrifice for all mankind (John 3:16; 1 Corinthians 15:3–4). A person who has believed that promise already possesses, by definition, what it guarantees.

The Free Grace tradition resists two assurance-destroying alternatives: looking inward at the consistency of one’s own works (which produces either pride or despair, since no believer’s works are fully consistent, and relocates the ground of assurance from Christ’s finished work to ongoing performance), and looking for a subjective felt confirmation (which fluctuates for reasons unrelated to spiritual reality). Zane Hodges argued the same point at length: assurance is of the very essence of saving faith — built into faith’s own definition, not added afterward as the fruit of proven obedience.2

The right question is simply, “Do I believe what Jesus promised?” If yes, assurance follows because it is grounded in the reliability of the Promiser, not the one receiving the promise. Doubt about assurance and doubt about salvation are not the same thing — the remedy for the first is not better feelings or a stricter performance audit, but returning again to the plain promises of Scripture until confidence in the Promiser outgrows the fluctuations of the moment.
