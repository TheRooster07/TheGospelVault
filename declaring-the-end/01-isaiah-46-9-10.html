The God Who Goes on Record

“Declaring the end from the beginning, and from ancient times things which have not been done, saying, ‘My purpose will be established, and I will accomplish all My good pleasure.’”
— Isaiah 46:10 (NASB95)

Most of us hedge our predictions. We say “probably,” “if all goes well,” or “we’ll see.” The God of Israel does the opposite. In Isaiah He puts the idols of Babylon on trial and sets one simple test: tell us what is going to happen. Then He takes the test Himself, in writing, centuries ahead of time.

He named a king, Cyrus, before Cyrus was born, and said he would let Jerusalem be rebuilt. He named another king, Josiah, about three hundred years before Josiah tore down the altar at Bethel. A God who says, “My purpose will be established,” and then shows it in history is a God whose word can be leaned on.

That is the point for us. If He kept His word about kings and cities, He keeps His word about you. The same God who declared the end from the beginning has promised eternal life to everyone who believes in His Son. His track record is the reason you can rest in His promise today.

Reflect

  1. Where do you find yourself hedging when you talk about God’s promises?
  2. Which of God’s fulfilled words in the Old Testament has most strengthened your confidence in Scripture?
  3. What promise of God do you most need to rest in this week because of His track record?

The God who names the future keeps His word — every time.

I. God Puts the Idols on Trial

A. A Courtroom in Isaiah

Isaiah 40–48 reads like a courtroom. The nations have their gods, Babylon has its idols and its diviners, and Israel is about to be carried away. In the middle of that world the LORD calls the case: “Present your case,” the Lord says. “Bring forward your strong arguments” (Isaiah 41:21). The question on trial is simple. Who is God? Is it the gods of the nations, carved and carried in procession, or the God of Israel?

The test God sets is not size, wealth, or the number of worshipers. It is knowledge of the future. Let the idols “declare to us what is going to take place … Declare the things that are going to come afterward, that we may know that you are gods” (Isaiah 41:22–23). Only the One who rules history can tell history ahead of time. An idol cannot speak at all, and a human diviner can only guess.

22 Let them bring forth and declare to us what is going to take place;

As for the former events, declare what they were,

That we may consider them and know their outcome.

Or announce to us what is coming;

23 Declare the things that are going to come afterward,

That we may know that you are gods;

Indeed, do good or evil, that we may anxiously look about us and fear together.

— Isaiah 41:22–23 (NASB95)

It helps to picture the scene Isaiah describes a few verses later. When Babylon falls, its great gods Bel and Nebo will be loaded onto carts and hauled away like baggage: “Bel has bowed down, Nebo stoops over; their images are consigned to the beasts and the cattle” (Isaiah 46:1). The idols that were supposed to carry Babylon have to be carried. They “could not rescue the burden, but have themselves gone into captivity” (46:2).

Then the LORD turns to His own people with a beautiful contrast: “You who have been borne by Me from birth and have been carried from the womb; even to your old age I will be the same, and even to your graying years I will bear you!” (Isaiah 46:3–4). Babylon carries its gods. Israel’s God carries His people. That is the setting of our key verses. The God who can declare the future is the same God who carries His people through it. Prophecy in Isaiah is never cold information. It is the word of a God who is personally committed to the ones He speaks to.

3 “Listen to Me, O house of Jacob,

And all the remnant of the house of Israel,

You who have been borne by Me from birth

And have been carried from the womb;

4 Even to your old age I will be the same,

And even to your graying years I will bear you!

I have done it, and I will carry you;

And I will bear you and I will deliver you.

— Isaiah 46:3–4 (NASB95)

B. God Takes His Own Test

Then the LORD does what the idols cannot. He goes on record. “Remember the former things long past, for I am God, and there is no other … declaring the end from the beginning, and from ancient times things which have not been done” (Isaiah 46:9–10). He even explains why He speaks ahead of time: “I declared them to you long ago, before they took place I proclaimed them to you, so that you would not say, ‘My idol has done them’” (Isaiah 48:5). Prophecy is God’s signature on history. He announces events before they happen so that no one can give the credit to anyone else.

This is an unusual claim for a religion to make. Many religious traditions teach about God, or about how to live. Very few stake their truth on publicly checkable predictions. The Bible does. That makes the Bible vulnerable in a way other books are not: if the predictions fail, the claim fails. John Bloom, a physicist and apologist, made exactly this point in a paper to the Evangelical Theological Society: God Himself presents fulfilled prophecy as evidence, beginning with this very challenge to the idols.1

C. “My Purpose Will Be Established”

Notice that God does not merely forecast. He says, “My purpose will be established, and I will accomplish all My good pleasure” (Isaiah 46:10). The Hebrew word for “purpose” (etsah) means counsel or plan, and “will be established” (qum) means to stand up, to stand firm. God does not peer into a future that is already there, the way a weather forecaster reads clouds. He tells what He Himself will do. Prophecy is God announcing His plan, and fulfillment is God carrying it out.

That is why prophecy and promise belong together. A forecaster can be right about the weather and still be unable to change it. When God declares the future, He is also binding Himself to bring it about. The same God who said “I will accomplish all My good pleasure” is the God who later says, “he who believes has eternal life” (John 6:47). Both are His word, and both rest on His power and faithfulness.

Isaiah returns to this theme again and again: “Behold, the former things have come to pass, now I declare new things; before they spring forth I proclaim them to you” (Isaiah 42:9). God points backward to what He has already fulfilled and forward to what He is about to do. The fulfilled past becomes the ground of confidence for the unfulfilled future. That is exactly how this whole series will work. We will look at things God said and then did, so that we can trust what He has said and not yet done.

II. The Test for a Prophet

A. A 100 Percent Standard

God did not only test the idols. He gave Israel a test for anyone who claimed to speak for Him. Moses anticipated the question every listener would ask: “How will we know the word which the Lord has not spoken?” (Deuteronomy 18:21). The answer was plain:

22 When a prophet speaks in the name of the Lord, if the thing does not come about or come true, that is the thing which the Lord has not spoken. The prophet has spoken it presumptuously; you shall not be afraid of him.

— Deuteronomy 18:22 (NASB95)

There is no grading on a curve here. A prophet who is right most of the time is not a prophet of the LORD. The word “presumptuously” (zadon) describes arrogance, a man speaking on his own authority while claiming God’s. Jeremiah applied the same standard to the false prophet Hananiah: “when the word of the prophet comes to pass, then that prophet will be known as one whom the Lord has truly sent” (Jeremiah 28:9). Within the year, Hananiah was dead, exactly as Jeremiah had said (Jeremiah 28:16–17).

B. A Prophet Who Put His Life on It

The Bible shows prophets accepting this standard in public, at real risk. When King Ahab of Israel planned to go to war at Ramoth-gilead, four hundred court prophets promised victory. One prophet, Micaiah, said the king would die in the battle. Ahab had him thrown in prison and fed on bread and water until the king returned safely. Micaiah’s answer is the test of Deuteronomy 18 in one sentence: “If you indeed return safely the Lord has not spoken by me” (1 Kings 22:28). He staked his credibility, and probably his life, on the outcome. Ahab disguised himself, but a random arrow found him, and he died that evening (1 Kings 22:34–37).

Four hundred prophets were wrong and one was right. Popularity, numbers and confidence were not the test. Fulfillment was. That is still the right way to weigh any claim to speak for God.

C. Why the Standard Matters for Us

This test shapes how we read the Bible today. Scripture does not ask us to accept its prophets because they were sincere, eloquent, or religious. It invites us to check them. The prophets were willing to be measured against history, and the Bible itself records the measuring. That is a mark of truthfulness. A book written to deceive does not hand its readers the test that could expose it.

It also guards us against modern claims. Anyone who says “the Lord told me” about the future must meet the same standard Moses gave. The Bible’s own test protects believers from being frightened or manipulated by predictions that do not come true: “you shall not be afraid of him” (Deuteronomy 18:22).

III. Named Before They Were Born

Some prophecies are general: judgment will come, a nation will fall, a deliverer will arise. Others are specific enough that they could not be fulfilled by accident or arranged by the people involved. Two of the most striking name a person who was not yet born. Josh and Sean McDowell, in their revised Evidence That Demands a Verdict, gather many examples like these under the heading of prophecy fulfilled in history.2

A. Josiah: About Three Hundred Years Ahead

After Solomon died, the kingdom split. Jeroboam, the first king of the northern tribes, set up a rival altar at Bethel so his people would not go to Jerusalem to worship. While Jeroboam stood at that altar, an unnamed man of God from Judah cried out against it:

2 He cried against the altar by the word of the Lord, and said, “O altar, altar, thus says the Lord, ‘Behold, a son shall be born to the house of David, Josiah by name; and on you he shall sacrifice the priests of the high places who burn incense on you, and human bones shall be burned on you.’”

— 1 Kings 13:2 (NASB95)

The prophecy names the king (Josiah), his family line (the house of David), the place (the altar at Bethel), and what he would do there (burn human bones on it). Jeroboam reigned in the late tenth century BC. Josiah carried out his reform about three centuries later. Second Kings records it in deliberate detail: Josiah broke down the altar at Bethel, took bones from the nearby graves, and “burned them on the altar and defiled it according to the word of the Lord which the man of God proclaimed” (2 Kings 23:16). The writer wants us to see the connection. What God declared, God did.

B. Cyrus: Named and Given His Task

The second example is even more remarkable. In Isaiah 44–45 God speaks of a Persian king by name:

28 “It is I who says of Cyrus, ‘He is My shepherd!

And he will perform all My desire.’

And he declares of Jerusalem, ‘She will be built,’

And of the temple, ‘Your foundation will be laid.’”

— Isaiah 44:28 (NASB95)

1 Thus says the Lord to Cyrus His anointed,

Whom I have taken by the right hand,

To subdue nations before him

And to loose the loins of kings;

To open doors before him so that gates will not be shut:

— Isaiah 45:1 (NASB95)

Isaiah prophesied in the eighth century BC, when Assyria, not Babylon or Persia, was the great power.3 Yet he names Cyrus, calls him the LORD’s “shepherd” and even His “anointed” (mashiach, used here of a pagan king set apart for a task), and says he will order Jerusalem and its temple rebuilt. Babylon had not yet destroyed Jerusalem. Persia was not yet an empire. Cyrus was not yet born.

History unfolded exactly as described. Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BC. In his first year he issued a decree allowing the Jews to return and rebuild the house of the LORD in Jerusalem (Ezra 1:1–3). A clay cylinder from Cyrus’s reign, found at Babylon in the nineteenth century and now in the British Museum, records his policy of returning deported peoples and their sanctuaries. It does not name the Jews, but it matches the policy Ezra describes.4 Notice also the purpose God gives: “so that you may know that it is I, the Lord, the God of Israel, who calls you by your name” (Isaiah 45:3). The naming itself was meant to be evidence.

C. Why Specific Prophecy Matters

There is a difference between a prediction that could fit almost anything and one that names names. Ancient oracles were famous for vague answers. The best-known example comes from the Greek historian Herodotus, who tells of King Croesus of Lydia asking the oracle at Delphi whether he should attack Persia. He was told that if he did, a great empire would fall. He attacked, and a great empire did fall. It was his own. An answer like that cannot fail, because it can be read either way.

The prophecies about Josiah and Cyrus are the opposite. They name a person, a family, a place, a task, and a result. They could be shown false. A specific prophecy is a risk only a God who truly knows the future can afford to take. Bloom observes that some prophecies, such as Daniel’s seventy weeks, would have been far too risky for a later editor to invent; they expose the text to being checked.5

D. The Obvious Objection

Because the Cyrus prophecy is so specific, many critical scholars have concluded that Isaiah 40–66 must have been written later, during or after the exile, by an unknown author sometimes called “Second Isaiah.” Notice the logic: the prophecy is too accurate to be prediction, so it must be history written afterward. That conclusion assumes the very thing in question, that God cannot tell the future.

Conservative scholars answer from the text and from the New Testament. John quotes from both halves of Isaiah, Isaiah 53 and Isaiah 6, and attributes both to the one prophet: “These things Isaiah said because he saw His glory” (John 12:38–41). The great Isaiah scroll from the Dead Sea Scrolls presents the book as a single work. And the passage itself makes the claim: God named Cyrus ahead of time on purpose, so that no one could credit an idol (Isaiah 45:3–4; 48:5). We will return to objections like this in Study 4.6

IV. A More Sure Word

A. Peter’s Confidence

At the end of his life Peter wrote about the reliability of what he preached. He had seen the transfiguration with his own eyes (2 Peter 1:16–18). Then he adds something surprising:

19 So we have the prophetic word made more sure, to which you do well to pay attention as to a lamp shining in a dark place, until the day dawns and the morning star arises in your hearts.

— 2 Peter 1:19 (NASB95)

The phrase “made more sure” translates bebaioteron, a comparative form of the word for firm or reliable. Peter’s point is that the prophetic Scriptures stand confirmed. What he saw on the mountain fit and confirmed what the prophets had already written. He calls that word a lamp in a dark place, something to pay attention to until the day dawns.7

B. Where Prophecy Comes From

20 But know this first of all, that no prophecy of Scripture is a matter of one’s own interpretation,

21 for no prophecy was ever made by an act of human will, but men moved by the Holy Spirit spoke from God.

— 2 Peter 1:20–21 (NASB95)

Peter explains why the prophetic word is reliable. It did not start with the prophet’s own reading of events (“one’s own interpretation”), and it was never produced “by an act of human will.” The prophets were “moved by the Holy Spirit.” The verb (pherō) means to carry or bear along; Luke uses the same verb for a ship driven by the wind (Acts 27:15, 17). The prophets spoke, but God carried them. That is why their words came true. Fulfilled prophecy is not the reason Scripture is God’s word. It is one of the ways God has shown that it is.8

Think about how this would have sounded to Peter’s first readers. They had not stood on the mountain. They had not seen the cloud or heard the voice. Peter tells them that they are not at a disadvantage. They have something “more sure” to hold in their hands: the prophetic word, confirmed by events and carried along by the Spirit. The same is true for us. We were not eyewitnesses, but we have the written word and the record of its fulfillment. A lamp is a humble thing, but on a dark road it is exactly what you need.

V. Why This Matters: The God Who Keeps His Word

A. Evidence God Gave on Purpose

God did not leave His people to guess whether He was real or whether His word could be trusted. He gave evidence and told them it was evidence: “so that you may know” (Isaiah 45:3). Jesus did the same with His disciples: “From now on I am telling you before it comes to pass, so that when it does occur, you may believe that I am He” (John 13:19). Using prophecy as evidence is not a modern invention. It is God’s own method.

Jesus said it twice in the upper room. Before Judas left to betray Him, He said, “From now on I am telling you before it comes to pass, so that when it does occur, you may believe that I am He” (John 13:19). Later, speaking of His departure, He said, “Now I have told you before it happens, so that when it happens, you may believe” (John 14:29). The disciples would soon watch everything collapse: betrayal, arrest, the cross. Jesus wanted them to remember, when it happened, that He had told them beforehand. The fulfillment would steady their faith rather than shake it. That is still one of prophecy’s main purposes for believers. It steadies us when circumstances seem to say God has lost control.

B. What Fulfilled Prophecy Does and Does Not Do

It is worth being clear about what this evidence can and cannot do. Fulfilled prophecy can remove a barrier. It can show a skeptic that the Bible is not a collection of myths, that its God speaks and acts in history, and that its claims deserve a hearing. It can strengthen a believer whose confidence in Scripture has been shaken.

What fulfilled prophecy cannot do is give eternal life. No one is saved by being impressed with Cyrus. A person receives eternal life by believing in Jesus Christ for it (John 3:16; 5:24). Prophecy points to the reliability of the One who makes that promise. The evidence opens the door; the promise is what a person is asked to trust.

C. From Fulfilled Prophecy to a Promise You Can Trust

The goal of all this is not that we win arguments. It is that we trust the God who speaks. If He kept His word about Josiah three centuries ahead and about Cyrus a century and a half ahead, He will keep His word about the things He has promised us. The most important of those promises is the simplest: “he who believes has eternal life” (John 6:47). That promise does not depend on our performance. It depends on the faithfulness of the One who made it.9

So when you read Isaiah’s challenge to the idols, read it as an invitation. The God who declares the end from the beginning has declared what He will do for everyone who believes in His Son. His track record is the reason you can rest in His promise today.

Most of us decide whom to trust by their record. A friend who has always shown up when he said he would is a friend whose next promise you believe. The Bible invites us to treat God the same way. Look at His record: Josiah, Cyrus, and, as we will see in the coming studies, empires, cities, the nation of Israel, and the Messiah Himself. Then look at His promise. The One who has never failed to do what He said has said that whoever believes in His Son has eternal life and will not come into judgment (John 5:24).

Lord, thank You.

Study Questions

Part 1 · Review from the Commentary

What test does God set for the idols in Isaiah 41:21–23?Declare what is going to happen, so that we may know that you are gods.
Why does God announce events before they happen (Isaiah 48:5)?So that no one could say an idol had done them; prophecy is His signature on history.
What is the test for a prophet in Deuteronomy 18:21–22?If what he says in the LORD’s name does not come about, the LORD has not spoken it. The standard is 100 percent.
Whom did God name about three centuries before he acted?Josiah (1 Kings 13:2), fulfilled at Bethel (2 Kings 23:15–16).
What did Isaiah say Cyrus would do?Act as God’s shepherd, and declare that Jerusalem would be built and the temple’s foundation laid (Isaiah 44:28; Ezra 1:1–3).
According to 2 Peter 1:21, where did prophecy come from?Not from human will; men moved (carried along) by the Holy Spirit spoke from God.

Part 2 · Discussion Questions

1. Why is it significant that the Bible stakes its claim on checkable predictions, when most religious books do not?

Show Leader Note

It makes Scripture testable. A book written to deceive would not hand its readers the test that could expose it (Deuteronomy 18:21–22). God invites examination (Isaiah 41:21) because He has nothing to hide.

2. What is the difference between a forecast and a prophecy in Isaiah 46:10?

Show Leader Note

A forecaster reads what may happen; God announces what He will do. “My purpose will be established” joins prediction to God’s power to carry out His plan.

3. How would you answer someone who says the Cyrus prophecy must have been written after the fact?

Show Leader Note

That conclusion assumes God cannot tell the future. John 12:38–41 credits both halves of Isaiah to the one prophet, and the text says God named Cyrus ahead of time on purpose (Isaiah 45:3–4; 48:5). Study 4 develops this further.

4. Peter had seen the transfiguration. Why does he still point to the prophetic word (2 Peter 1:19)?

Show Leader Note

His experience confirmed what the prophets had already written. The written word is a lamp every believer can hold, long after eyewitnesses are gone.

5. How does God’s track record in prophecy strengthen your assurance of eternal life?

Show Leader Note

The same God who kept His word about Josiah and Cyrus has promised that “he who believes has eternal life” (John 6:47). That promise rests on His faithfulness, not ours.

⑤ Application

Living and sharing what we have studied

Application 1 — Trust the God Who Goes on Record

Isaiah 46:9–10

From ③ I.B–C.

God did not keep His plans secret and then claim credit afterward. He announced them in advance. When you are unsure what tomorrow holds, remember that your future is in the hands of the One who has already declared the end from the beginning.

Reflection: What uncertainty about the future are you carrying right now?

Practical Step: Write Isaiah 46:9–10 on a card and read it each morning this week before you check the news.

Application 2 — Hold Every Claim to the Bible’s Standard

Deuteronomy 18:21–22

From ③ II.A–B.

Scripture gives its own test for anyone who claims to speak for God about the future. Believers do not need to be frightened or manipulated by confident predictions. A word from the LORD comes true every time.

Reflection: Have you ever been unsettled by someone’s prediction about the future “from the Lord”?

Practical Step: Read Jeremiah 28 this week and note how the test of Deuteronomy 18 was applied.

Application 3 — Learn Two Names

1 Kings 13:2; Isaiah 44:28

From ③ III.A–B; cf. McDowell and McDowell, Evidence That Demands a Verdict.

You do not need a long list to show that God tells the future. Josiah and Cyrus, each named before birth, are enough to start a conversation. Know where to find them and what happened.

Reflection: Could you explain the Josiah or Cyrus prophecy to a friend in two minutes?

Practical Step: Practice telling one of these two stories, with the references, to someone in your group.

Application 4 — Rest in His Promise

John 6:47; John 13:19

From ③ V.A–B; Wilkin (GES).

Jesus told His disciples things before they happened so that they would believe. The goal of fulfilled prophecy is trust in the God who speaks — and His clearest promise is eternal life to everyone who believes in His Son.

Reflection: Are you resting in Jesus’ promise of eternal life, or still wondering if you have done enough?

Practical Step: Memorize John 6:47 and say it aloud whenever doubt about your salvation comes.

End of Study · Isaiah 46:9–10
