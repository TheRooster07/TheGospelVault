Is It Wrong for Christians to Fear Death?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Natural fear and grief are not sin — Jesus wept and agonized. But Christ has broken death’s power, and for the believer, to die is gain.

Key versesHebrews 2:14–15 · Philippians 1:21 · 1 Corinthians 15:55–57

Scripture takes a remarkably honest, non-judgmental view of the instinctive human fear of death, while offering the believer substantial grounds to hold that fear in its proper, diminished place rather than treating any felt fear as a spiritual failure. Jesus Himself, facing the cross, experienced such intense dread that “His sweat became like great drops of blood falling down to the ground” (Luke 22:44, a verse missing from some early manuscripts but widely attested and long received) — the incarnate Son of God did not treat the approach of death with casual indifference, which should caution against treating ordinary human fear of death as automatically sinful or evidence of weak faith.

At the same time, Scripture presents Christ’s own death and resurrection as the specific means by which this fear’s ultimate power over the believer has already been broken: Christ shared in humanity’s condition “that through death He might destroy him who had the power of death, that is, the devil, and release those who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage” (Hebrews 2:14–15). The fear in view here is not merely the ordinary discomfort of facing an unknown transition, but the deeper, enslaving dread of death as a final, hopeless end — precisely the dread the resurrection directly and permanently answers.

Paul’s own confidence facing possible execution models a fear appropriately relativized rather than eliminated: “to live is Christ, and to die is gain” (Philippians 1:21); “we are confident ... to be absent from the body and to be present with the Lord” (2 Corinthians 5:8). This confidence is grounded not in personal courage or a naturally fearless temperament, but in the specific, settled facts of Scripture: the security of the believer’s salvation, the certainty of Christ’s own resurrection as guarantee of the believer’s own (1 Corinthians 15:20–23), and the reality that death delivers the believer immediately into Christ’s conscious presence rather than into judgment or uncertainty (John 5:24, “shall not come into judgment, but has passed from death into life”).

It is worth distinguishing between the settled theological fact of the believer’s secure future and the ordinary, embodied human experience of approaching death, which can include real grief, real apprehension about pain or the process of dying, and real sorrow at leaving loved ones behind — none of which Scripture treats as spiritually disqualifying. Paul himself grieved deeply over the near-death illness of his own friend Epaphroditus (Philippians 2:27), and Jesus wept openly at Lazarus’s tomb even knowing He was about to raise him (John 11:35) — grief and fear surrounding death are not incompatible with genuine faith; they simply are not meant to have the final word.

The distinctively Christian hope is not the absence of every natural human response to death, but the presence of a settled confidence underneath it: “O Death, where is your sting? O Hades, where is your victory? ... thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 15:55, 57). A believer may feel the ordinary human weight of death’s approach and still, beneath that feeling, hold with complete confidence to the fact that death has already been decisively, permanently defeated on their behalf.
