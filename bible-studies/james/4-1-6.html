Wars Within and a Greater Grace

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

Source: The Gospel Vault · Developed from teaching outlines by Dr. C. Norman Sellers, ThD, used by permission

Leader / Learner Guide

① The Biblical Text

James 4:1–6 · New American Standard Bible (NASB95)

2 You lust and do not have; so you commit murder. You are envious and cannot obtain; so you fight and quarrel. You do not have because you do not ask.

② Devotional

“But He gives a greater grace. Therefore it says, ‘God is opposed to the proud, but gives grace to the humble.’” — James 4:6

James asks a hard question: why are there quarrels and conflicts among believers? His answer goes deeper than personalities. The war outside begins with a war inside. Our desires for pleasure, for things, for position, fight within us, and when they cannot get what they want, they turn on others.

Worldliness is not owning things; it is being owned by them. James calls it spiritual adultery. God is like a faithful husband, and He jealously longs for the undivided love of the people in whom He has made His Spirit live. Friendship with the world means turning our backs on the One who loves us most.

But James does not leave us there. “He gives a greater grace.” However strong the pull of the world, God’s grace is stronger, and it flows to the humble. The way back is not to try harder, but to bow lower.

Reflection Questions

  1. What desire in me is fighting hardest for its own way right now?
  2. Have I been praying for what I want, or for what God wants?
  3. Where do I need to humble myself to receive His greater grace?

Lord, You have loved me jealously. Turn my heart from the world, and give me grace to walk humbly with You.

Introduction: When the Church Becomes a War Zone

Chuck Colson once told of a church business meeting in New England that ended in a fistfight between the pastor and a deacon. Hymnals flew, a vase shattered, and the police were called. When the members came before a judge, he dismissed the charges but told them, in effect, that while their Lord might put up with fights among His followers, the state would not (cf. Charles Colson, The Body, Word, 1992). We laugh, and then we wince, because most of us have seen the same war fought with words instead of fists.

James has just said that wisdom from above is “first pure, then peaceable” and that “the seed whose fruit is righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace” (3:17–18). Now he asks the obvious question: then why is there so much fighting among you? His answer is one word: worldliness.

A. What Worldliness Is

To be worldly is to be devoted to the affairs, pleasures and things of this world. The key word is devoted: given over to them, living for them. John describes the world’s system this way:

“Do not love the world nor the things in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him. For all that is in the world, the lust of the flesh and the lust of the eyes and the boastful pride of life, is not from the Father, but is from the world.”

1 John 2:15–16 (NASB95)

B. What Worldliness Is Not

There are two common misunderstandings. The first is that God does not want His people to enjoy anything. But look at how He made us: with appetites for food, for love, for beauty. Look at where He put the first man: in a garden with “every tree that is pleasing to the sight and good for food” (Genesis 2:9). Paul says God “richly supplies us with all things to enjoy” (1 Timothy 6:17). Pleasure is God’s idea.

The second misunderstanding is that spirituality means denying pleasure, and that a list of rules will keep us from worldliness. Paul answered that in Colossians:

“If you have died with Christ to the elementary principles of the world, why, as if you were living in the world, do you submit yourself to decrees, such as, ‘Do not handle, do not taste, do not touch!’ (which all refer to things destined to perish with use)—in accordance with the commandments and teachings of men? These are matters which have, to be sure, the appearance of wisdom in self-made religion and self-abasement and severe treatment of the body, but are of no value against fleshly indulgence.”

Colossians 2:20–23 (NASB95)

Rules may look wise, but they cannot change the heart. Worldliness is not a matter of the things around us. It is a matter of what rules us.

I. The Conflicts of the Worldly (4:1)

“What is the source of quarrels and conflicts among you? Is not the source your pleasures that wage war in your members?”

James 4:1 (NASB95)

A. Wars and Fights Among You

James raises the question like a good lawyer who already knows the answer. He uses two words. The first, polemos, means war, a prolonged state of conflict. The second, machē, means a battle or skirmish, the individual fights within the larger war. This church had become a war zone.

It was a civil war. In a civil war every casualty is on your own side. There is no enemy losing soldiers, because the enemy is yourselves. And all the while the real enemy goes untouched. God has given believers an enemy to fight, and it is not each other:

“Put on the full armor of God, so that you will be able to stand firm against the schemes of the devil. For our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the powers, against the world forces of this darkness, against the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places.”

Ephesians 6:11–12 (NASB95)

Paul warned the Galatians, “if you bite and devour one another, take care that you are not consumed by one another” (Galatians 5:15). Satan is delighted when believers fire on each other instead of on him.

B. Pleasures at War Within

Where do the fights come from? James answers with another question that expects a yes: “Is not the source your pleasures that wage war in your members?” The conflict among them came from the conflict within them.

The word for pleasures is hēdonē, from which we get “hedonism”: the self-indulgent pursuit of pleasure as the main goal of life. If it feels good, do it; if it tastes good, eat it; if it looks good, get it. Outside James, the word appears only three times in the New Testament (Luke 8:14; Titus 3:3; 2 Peter 2:13). Paul uses it to describe what we were before Christ:

“For we also once were foolish ourselves, disobedient, deceived, enslaved to various lusts and pleasures, spending our life in malice and envy, hateful, hating one another.”

Titus 3:3 (NASB95)

And he warned that in the last days men would be “lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God” (2 Timothy 3:4), using a related word. That description fits much of our culture. A great deal of our economy runs on the promise of pleasure.

Jesus used the same word in the parable of the sower. Some seed fell among thorns:

“The seed which fell among the thorns, these are the ones who have heard, and as they go on their way they are choked with worries and riches and pleasures of this life, and bring no fruit to maturity.”

Luke 8:14 (NASB95)

Notice that the seed among the thorns springs up; there is life. But the life is choked, and it brings “no fruit to maturity.” That is exactly James’s concern. Pleasures do not take away a believer’s eternal life, but they can choke the Word in his life until nothing ripens. In a culture of comfort and plenty, believers are especially open to that danger.

The verb James uses, “wage war” (strateuomai), is a military word: to campaign, to serve as a soldier. Our desires are like an army camped inside us. Peter uses the same word: “fleshly lusts which wage war against the soul” (1 Peter 2:11). Paul felt the same war: “I see a different law in the members of my body, waging war against the law of my mind” (Romans 7:23).

II. The Characteristics of Worldliness (4:2–3)

“You lust and do not have; so you commit murder. You are envious and cannot obtain; so you fight and quarrel. You do not have because you do not ask. You ask and do not receive, because you ask with wrong motives, so that you may spend it on your pleasures.”

James 4:2–3 (NASB95)

Four times in two verses James tells them they lack what they want: “you do not have,” “cannot obtain,” “you do not have,” “you do not receive.” These were not wealthy people. James is not rebuking the “haves” but the “have nots.” Poor people can be just as worldly as rich ones. Worldliness is not the possession of things but the obsession with things. James lists its marks.

A. Worldliness Is Lustful

“You lust and do not have.” The word epithymeō is stronger than “want.” It means to set the heart on something, to crave it. It can be used of good desires, but usually of wrong ones. When we hear “lust” we think of sexual desire, and that is included. But the flesh also craves possessions, position, power and the last word in every argument.

At salvation the believer is set free from sin’s mastery, but the flesh is still present, and it makes its desires felt (Romans 6:12). And lust is never satisfied. The old temperance saying put it well: first the man takes a drink, then the drink takes a drink, then the drink takes the man. Whether it is a bottle, a shopping cart or a second helping, one always calls for another. If you lack something and do not crave it, you are at peace. If you lack it and do crave it, you are frustrated and miserable. James already traced the path: “each one is tempted when he is carried away and enticed by his own lust” (1:14).

B. Worldliness Is Hateful

“So you commit murder.” Can craving really lead to murder? It has. Ahab wanted Naboth’s vineyard, and when Naboth would not sell, Jezebel arranged false witnesses, and Naboth was stoned (1 Kings 21). David saw Bathsheba, wanted her, took her, and then arranged her husband’s death (2 Samuel 11). Both men were God’s people, and both ended in blood.

More likely James is speaking as Jesus did. Jesus said that anger and contempt toward a brother are murder in the heart (Matthew 5:21–22). John wrote, “Everyone who hates his brother is a murderer” (1 John 3:15). Most of us will never take a life, but we can kill a reputation, a friendship or a spirit with our words. That is the line from chapter 3 to chapter 4.

C. Worldliness Is Envious and Quarrelsome

“You are envious and cannot obtain.” The word zēloō means to burn with zeal, to be jealous. They were not only wanting what others had; they were passionate about it. And when they could not get it, frustration boiled over: “so you fight and quarrel.” James uses the same two words as in verse 1, battles and war, now in reverse order. Craving leads to envy, envy to frustration, frustration to fighting.

D. Worldliness Is Prayerless

“You do not have because you do not ask.” Here is the simplest diagnosis of all. They were scheming, envying and fighting for what they wanted, but they never brought their needs to God. Jesus said, “Ask, and it will be given to you” (Matthew 7:7). Paul said, “in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God” (Philippians 4:6). Prayer is not a guarantee of whatever we want, but prayerlessness is a guarantee of frustration. The worldly person looks to everyone and everything except God.

E. Worldliness Prays Selfishly

“You ask and do not receive, because you ask with wrong motives, so that you may spend it on your pleasures.” Some of them did pray, but their prayers were shopping lists. The word kakōs means wrongly, with bad motives. The verb dapanaō, spend, is used of the prodigal son, who “spent everything” (Luke 15:14). They wanted God to fund their pleasures. God is not a vending machine. John gives the key: “if we ask anything according to His will, He hears us” (1 John 5:14).

III. The Condemnation of the Worldly (4:4)

“You adulteresses, do you not know that friendship with the world is hostility toward God? Therefore whoever wishes to be a friend of the world makes himself an enemy of God.”

James 4:4 (NASB95)

A. “You Adulteresses”

This is shocking language. Two questions come up. To whom is it addressed? And is the adultery physical or spiritual?

The NASB, following the oldest manuscripts, reads “You adulteresses.” The Majority Text, followed by the KJV and NKJV, reads “Adulterers and adulteresses.” The single feminine word fits the Bible’s great picture: God’s people as His wife. In the Old Testament, Israel was the wife of the Lord:

“‘For your husband is your Maker, Whose name is the Lord of hosts; And your Redeemer is the Holy One of Israel, Who is called the God of all the earth.”

Isaiah 54:5 (NASB95)

When Israel turned to idols, the prophets called it adultery (Jeremiah 3:20; Ezekiel 16:32). God told Hosea to love an adulterous wife “even as the Lord loves the sons of Israel, though they turn to other gods” (Hosea 3:1). Jesus called His own generation “adulterous” (Mark 8:38).

In the New Testament, the church is the bride of Christ. Paul wrote, “I betrothed you to one husband, so that to Christ I might present you as a pure virgin” (2 Corinthians 11:2), and he described Christ’s love for the church as a husband’s love for his wife (Ephesians 5:25–27). Since James has just spoken of wars and murder in a figurative sense, the adultery is figurative too. It is spiritual adultery: giving to the world the love that belongs to the Lord.

And these are believers. James calls them “brethren” both before and after this verse (3:1, 12; 4:11). Only a wife can commit adultery. An outsider can be immoral, but only someone in a marriage can be unfaithful. That is what makes it so serious.

B. Friendship with the World Is Hostility Toward God

“Do you not know that friendship with the world is hostility toward God? Therefore whoever wishes to be a friend of the world makes himself an enemy of God.” The word philia, friendship, appears only here in the New Testament. Its opposite is echthra, enmity, hostility.

Every person starts out as God’s enemy. “While we were enemies we were reconciled to God through the death of His Son” (Romans 5:10). The cross made peace with God possible, and every believer has that peace permanently: the moment we believed, we were reconciled and given eternal life that cannot be lost.

But worldliness brings back hostility on a different level. James does not say the worldly believer loses his salvation. He says the believer who chooses to be the world’s friend “makes himself” God’s enemy in his relationship and walk. Remember Abraham, who “was called the friend of God” (2:23) because his faith went to work. Here is the opposite: a believer who chooses the world as his friend sets himself against God. Zane Hodges observed that James’s Christian readers, craving the world’s acceptance and standing, were committing spiritual adultery and turning away from friendship with their Lord, much as an unfaithful husband turns away from his wife (cf. Zane C. Hodges, “Who’s Friend Are You?” Grace Evangelical Society, September 11, 2017, https://faithalone.org/blog/whos-friend-are-you). Jesus said, “You are My friends if you do what I command you” (John 15:14). No one can serve two masters (Matthew 6:24).

IV. The Jealous Longing of God (4:5)

“Or do you think that the Scripture speaks to no purpose: ‘He jealously desires the Spirit which He has made to dwell in us’?”

James 4:5 (NASB95)

This is one of the hardest verses in James. The translations differ widely. The NKJV reads, “The Spirit who dwells in us yearns jealously.” The NASB reads, “He jealously desires the Spirit which He has made to dwell in us.” Four questions need answers.

A. Where Does the Quotation Come From?

No Old Testament verse says exactly these words. Most likely James is summarizing what “the Scripture” says in many places: that God is jealous for His people. “I, the Lord your God, am a jealous God” (Exodus 20:5). “The Lord, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God” (Exodus 34:14). Israel “made Him jealous with strange gods” (Deuteronomy 32:16). James asks, Do you think Scripture says all that “to no purpose” (kenōs, in vain, emptily)? It means what it says.

B. Who Is Jealous, and for What?

The Greek can be read more than one way. One reading makes the Holy Spirit the subject: the Spirit who lives in us longs jealously for our devotion (NKJV). Another makes God the subject: God jealously longs for the spirit, or the Spirit, He has caused to live in us (NASB; cf. NIV, ESV). A third, less likely, makes the human spirit the subject, which tends toward envy. Since James has just described God as the wronged husband (4:4), the best reading is that God Himself longs jealously over His people, in whom He has made His Spirit dwell. Either of the first two readings leads to the same point: God is not indifferent when His people flirt with the world.

C. What Kind of Jealousy?

The word phthonos usually means envy, and epipotheō means to long for, to yearn after. God’s jealousy is not the petty envy of chapter 3. It is the right jealousy of a faithful husband who will not share his wife’s love with another. Paul felt it for the Corinthians: “I am jealous for you with a godly jealousy” (2 Corinthians 11:2). A husband who did not care whether his wife was faithful would not really love her. God’s jealousy is the measure of His love.

V. A Greater Grace (4:6)

“But He gives a greater grace. Therefore it says, ‘God is opposed to the proud, but gives grace to the humble.’”

James 4:6 (NASB95)

A. The Promise

Here is the turning point. After the charge of adultery and enmity, James says, “But He gives a greater grace.” The demands are great, the pull of the world is strong, and our flesh is weak. But God’s grace is greater than all of it. It is greater than our craving, greater than our failures, greater than the world’s pull. Paul learned the same lesson: “My grace is sufficient for you, for power is perfected in weakness” (2 Corinthians 12:9). And we are invited to come for it: “let us draw near with confidence to the throne of grace, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need” (Hebrews 4:16).

B. The Principle

Then James quotes Proverbs 3:34 in its Greek form (the Septuagint): “God is opposed to the proud, but gives grace to the humble.” Peter quotes the same verse (1 Peter 5:5). The word for “opposed” (antitassō) is a military term: to line up in battle against. The proud person, who insists on his own way and his own pleasures, finds God arrayed against him. The humble person finds God giving grace.

Grace is always available, but it flows downhill, to the low places. The tax collector who beat his breast and said, “God, be merciful to me, the sinner!” went home justified, “for everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but he who humbles himself will be exalted” (Luke 18:13–14). In the next study James will tell us how to humble ourselves: “Submit therefore to God. Resist the devil and he will flee from you. Draw near to God and He will draw near to you” (4:7–8).

Conclusion: Am I Worldly?

How do we know if we are worldly? Not by a list of rules; Colossians 2 closed that door. Ray Stedman once gave examples that cut both ways: dressing to draw attention can be worldly, but so can dressing shabbily to look spiritual; being hurt because no one noticed you is worldly; choosing a program or a hobby over something you know the Lord wants you to do is worldly (cf. Ray C. Stedman, sermon on James 4, outside FGR). The point is not a new list. Anything is worldly if the heart is set on the approval or the pleasures of the world. Anything is spiritual if the eye is fixed on the Lord.

Every believer has eternal life as a free gift that can never be lost. James is not asking whether we are saved. He is asking whose friend we are. The world offers pleasure and leaves us fighting. God, our faithful Husband, longs jealously for our hearts, and He gives a greater grace to all who humble themselves.

Lord, thank You.

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. Why is worldliness not the same as enjoying God’s good gifts?

Show Leader Answer

God made us with appetites and “richly supplies us with all things to enjoy” (1 Timothy 6:17). Worldliness is devotion to pleasure and things, not the enjoyment of them.

2. How do inner desires turn into outward conflicts?

Show Leader Answer

Craving leads to frustration and envy, and when we cannot get what we want, we fight those who stand in the way (4:1–2).

3. What is the difference between “you do not ask” and “you ask with wrong motives”?

Show Leader Answer

One neglects prayer and schemes instead; the other prays, but to fund its own pleasures. Both leave God out of the center.

4. Why does James use the picture of adultery, and why does it apply only to believers?

Show Leader Answer

Only someone in a covenant relationship can be unfaithful. God’s people belong to Him; giving their love to the world is betrayal of their Husband.

5. How does “He gives a greater grace” encourage a believer who has been worldly?

Show Leader Answer

God’s grace is greater than the world’s pull and our failures, and it is given to all who humble themselves (4:6; Hebrews 4:16).

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Fight the Right Enemy

James 4:1; Ephesians 6:11–12

Source: Commentary ③ — I

Conflicts among believers are a civil war that only the devil wins. Look inward for the source, and outward at the real enemy.

Reflection: Is there a believer I have been treating as my enemy?

Practical Step: Before your next conversation with that person, pray for them by name and ask God to show you your own part.

Name the Craving

James 4:2; 1 John 2:15–16

Source: Commentary ③ — II.A–C

Worldliness is not possession but obsession. Find the desire that is fighting for its own way.

Reflection: What do I want so badly that it is making me envious or angry?

Practical Step: Write it down, confess it to God, and thank Him for one thing He has already given you.

Ask, and Ask Rightly

James 4:2–3; 1 John 5:14

Source: Commentary ③ — II.D–E

Bring your needs to God instead of scheming, and let His will shape your requests.

Reflection: Are my prayers mostly shopping lists?

Practical Step: This week, begin each prayer by asking what God wants before asking what you want.

Humble Yourself and Receive Grace

James 4:4–6; Hebrews 4:16

Source: Commentary ③ — III–V

God jealously loves His people, and His grace is greater than the world’s pull. It flows to the humble.

Reflection: Where have I been a friend of the world?

Practical Step: Turn from one specific worldly attachment this week, and ask God for His greater grace.
