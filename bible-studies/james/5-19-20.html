James 5:19–20 · Small Group Bible Study · Study 16 of 16

Bring Them Back Alive

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

Source: The Gospel Vault · Developed from teaching outlines by Dr. C. Norman Sellers, ThD, used by permission

Leader / Learner Guide

① The Biblical Text

James 5:19–20 · New American Standard Bible (NASB95)

19 My brethren, if any among you strays from the truth and one turns him back,

20 let him know that he who turns a sinner from the error of his way will save his soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins.

Everyone reads before anything else.

Scripture quotations taken from the (NASB®) New American Standard Bible®, Copyright © 1960, 1971, 1977, 1995 by The Lockman Foundation. Used by permission. All rights reserved. lockman.org

② Devotional

“Let him know that he who turns a sinner from the error of his way will save his soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins.” — James 5:20

James does not end his letter with greetings. He ends with a rescue mission. Any believer can wander from the truth: a little at first, then farther, until his life is in danger. And any believer can be the one who goes after him.

Turning a brother back is hard. People are proud, defensive or ashamed. So we go gently, humbly, with the log out of our own eye, and with the heart of a shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to find the one.

And James tells us what is at stake. The wandering believer does not lose his eternal life, but sin can cost him his physical life. The one who brings him back saves his life from death and covers a multitude of sins. There are few greater works of love.

Reflection Questions

  1. Is there a brother or sister I have watched drift away without saying a word?
  2. Am I humble enough to go, and gentle enough to be heard?
  3. Where might I be wandering myself?

Lord, keep me close to Your truth, and send me after those who stray.

Introduction: The Last Word of the Letter

Most New Testament letters end with greetings, travel plans and a benediction. James ends with none of these. His last word is a call to rescue. It is as if he looks out over the scattered believers he has been writing to and says, “Now go and get the ones who have wandered.”

It is a fitting end. From the beginning James has been concerned with saving the life. The implanted Word “is able to save your souls” (1:21). Idle faith cannot “save” a brother in need (2:14). God “is able to save and to destroy” (4:12). The prayer of faith “will restore” (save) the sick (5:15). Now he ends with the believer who “will save his soul from death.” In every case the word is sōzō, and in James it is consistently about the deliverance of life. Study 01 pointed ahead to this passage, and now we have arrived.

It also fits the last few verses. James has just told the church to confess sins to one another and pray for one another (5:16). Restoring the wanderer is the same love carried one step further: not only praying for the brother who comes to us, but going after the brother who does not.

I. The Wanderer (5:19a)

“My brethren, if any among you strays from the truth and one turns him back,”

James 5:19 (NASB95)

A. Who He Is: A Brother

“My brethren, if any among you.” James is not speaking about unbelievers. Again and again in this letter he has addressed his readers as brethren (1:2, 16, 19; 2:1, 14; 3:1; 4:11; 5:7, 12), and now he speaks of one “among you.” An unbeliever cannot backslide; you have to go forward before you can slide back. This is not regeneration but restoration. Like “revival,” it is a word for believers only. You cannot revive what was never alive.

And it is “any” believer. The little word tis, anyone, appears twice in this verse: “if any among you strays” and “one turns him back.” It could happen to anyone. It could happen to you. When we see a brother stumble, we dare not pull our robes around us like the Pharisee. “Let him who thinks he stands take heed that he does not fall” (1 Corinthians 10:12). The Christian life is like riding a bicycle: if you do not keep moving forward, you fall over.

That is why the writer of Hebrews tells believers to watch over one another: “encourage one another day after day, as long as it is still called ‘Today,’ so that none of you will be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin” (Hebrews 3:13). Wandering is rarely noticed by the one who wanders. It is usually noticed first by those who love him.

B. What He Does: Strays

The word is planaō, to wander, go astray. From it we get our word “planet.” Before Copernicus, people watched the planets move against the fixed stars, first forward, then seemingly backward, here and then there, and called them “wandering stars.” Jude uses the same picture for false teachers: “wandering stars, for whom the black darkness has been reserved forever” (Jude 13). Jesus used the word when He told the Sadducees, “You are mistaken, not understanding the Scriptures” (Matthew 22:29).

The picture is of a sheep that has drifted from the flock. It rarely happens in one leap. A little grass here, a little farther there, and soon the sheep is lost. “All of us like sheep have gone astray” (Isaiah 53:6). The opposite is John’s joy: “I have no greater joy than this, to hear of my children walking in the truth” (3 John 4).

C. From What: The Truth

The believer strays “from the truth.” James has used that word before. God “brought us forth by the word of truth” (1:18). The worldly wise “lie against the truth” (3:14). Straying usually begins with believing a lie: that sin will satisfy, that God does not care, that this one compromise will not matter. The lie affects our attitudes, and our attitudes produce our actions. That is why the Word is so important. It is the truth that keeps us on the path.

D. Two Kinds of Wandering

A believer can wander doctrinally. The New Testament is full of examples. The Galatians were told they had to be circumcised to be saved, and Paul called it “a different gospel” (Galatians 1:6). Some at Corinth said “there is no resurrection of the dead” (1 Corinthians 15:12). John warned of deceivers who denied that Jesus Christ came in the flesh (2 John 7). The Thessalonians were shaken by a report that “the day of the Lord has come” (2 Thessalonians 2:2). Sometimes believers leave truth they once knew; sometimes they were never well taught.

A believer can also wander practically. Some sincere believers who care deeply about living for the Lord are led into legalism. They put themselves back under the Law of Moses, which has been replaced by the New Covenant (Hebrews 8:13), or under man-made rules about food, days, dress and customs (Colossians 2:16–17). “It was for freedom that Christ set us free; therefore keep standing firm and do not be subject again to a yoke of slavery” (Galatians 5:1). Others go the opposite way, into license: “I am under grace, so I can do as I please.” Paul answers, “Are we to continue in sin so that grace may increase? May it never be!” (Romans 6:1–2). And some simply know the truth and do not care. They ignore it for their own purposes. That is the most dangerous wandering of all.

Whatever form it takes, wandering has a direction. It moves away from the truth, away from fellowship with God, and away from the people of God. The wanderer often stops attending, stops reading, stops praying, and surrounds himself with voices that tell him what he wants to hear. By the time anyone notices, he may be a long way from home.

II. The Restorer (5:19b)

A. Someone Turns Him Back

“And one turns him back.” The word epistrephō means to turn around, to bring back. James does not say the elders must do it, or the pastor. He says “one,” anyone. Restoring a wandering brother is the responsibility of every believer. Jesus said to Peter on the night he would deny Him, “I have prayed for you, that your faith may not fail; and you, when once you have turned again, strengthen your brothers” (Luke 22:32). Peter wandered, was turned back, and then turned others back.

For the wanderer, being turned back involves recognition (admitting the sin), repentance (changing his mind about it), and return (turning from it to God). “He who conceals his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will find compassion” (Proverbs 28:13).

B. Why It Is Hard

If this were easy, more of us would do it. But people who have wandered are often proud and do not want to admit they were wrong. They are often defensive and assume the worst about anyone who raises the subject. And they are often ashamed, carrying guilt that makes them avoid the very believers who could help. The restorer must be ready for all three.

The restorer has his own fears as well. He fears being rejected, being thought judgmental, or making things worse. Many of us would rather say nothing and hope the problem goes away. But silence is not always kindness. Sometimes the most loving thing we can do is to speak, carefully and gently, before the wanderer goes too far.

C. How to Do It

First, gently. Paul wrote:

“Brethren, even if anyone is caught in any trespass, you who are spiritual, restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness; each one looking to yourself, so that you too will not be tempted. Bear one another’s burdens, and thereby fulfill the law of Christ.”

Galatians 6:1–2 (NASB95)

Second, humbly and with clean hands. “You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother’s eye” (Matthew 7:5). The one who restores must be spiritually healthy himself, and aware that he too could fall.

Third, privately where possible. Jesus said, “If your brother sins, go and show him his fault in private; if he listens to you, you have won your brother” (Matthew 18:15). Fourth, patiently, trusting God to do the work: “with gentleness correcting those who are in opposition, if perhaps God may grant them repentance” (2 Timothy 2:25). And fifth, with discernment, for different people need different approaches. Jude says:

Jesus went on to describe what to do if the brother will not listen: take one or two others, and then, if needed, tell it to the church (Matthew 18:16–17). But the goal at every step is the same: to win the brother back. Most restorations never need to go beyond the first, private conversation, if that conversation is held in love.

“And have mercy on some, who are doubting; save others, snatching them out of the fire; and on some have mercy with fear, hating even the garment polluted by the flesh.”

Jude 22–23 (NASB95)

D. The Shepherd’s Heart

Behind all of this is the heart of our Lord. “What man among you, if he has a hundred sheep and has lost one of them, does not leave the ninety-nine in the open pasture and go after the one which is lost until he finds it? When he has found it, he lays it on his shoulders, rejoicing” (Luke 15:4–5). When the prodigal came home, “while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion for him, and ran and embraced him and kissed him” (Luke 15:20). The restorer goes after the wanderer not to scold him but to bring him home.

The Lord did the same for Peter. After the resurrection, by the Sea of Galilee, He did not shame the disciple who had denied Him three times. He asked him three times, “Simon, son of John, do you love Me?” and three times gave him a task: “Tend My sheep” (John 21:15–17). The wanderer was not only brought back; he was put back to work.

III. The Rescue (5:20)

“let him know that he who turns a sinner from the error of his way will save his soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins.”

James 5:20 (NASB95)

A. “Let Him Know”

James speaks to the restorer: “let him know.” This is encouragement for the one who goes. It may feel awkward. It may be met with anger. But let him know what is at stake, and what good he is doing. He is turning “a sinner from the error (planē, the noun form of ‘stray’) of his way.” “Sinner” here does not mean an unbeliever; James called his readers “sinners” in 4:8. It describes a believer who is living in sin.

B. “Will Save His Soul”

What does it mean to “save his soul”? The word “save” has several meanings in the New Testament:

Meaning of “save”ExampleWhat it delivers from
Eternal salvation“By grace you have been saved through faith” (Ephesians 2:8)The penalty of sin
Present deliverance“Work out your salvation” (Philippians 2:12)The power of sin in daily life
Future salvation“Salvation is nearer to us than when we believed” (Romans 13:11)The presence of sin
Physical healing“Your faith has made you well” (Matthew 9:22)Sickness
Rescue from danger“Save us, Lord; we are perishing!” (Matthew 8:25)Physical peril

Context decides the meaning. And the word “soul” (psychē) also has a range: the immaterial part of a person, but very often the physical life or the whole person. When Eutychus fell from the window, Paul said, “his life (psychē) is in him” (Acts 20:10). “All the persons (psychai) of the house of Jacob, who came to Egypt, were seventy” (Genesis 46:27). So “save his soul” can rightly be understood as “save his life” or “save the person.”

C. “From Death”

What death is in view? Not eternal death. The wanderer is a brother, and every believer “has eternal life, and does not come into judgment, but has passed out of death into life” (John 5:24). Eternal life is eternal; it cannot be lost (John 10:28). James is speaking of physical death, premature death, as the consequence of persistent sin. He has already traced the path: “when sin is accomplished, it brings forth death” (1:15).

Scripture gives sobering examples of believers whose sin cost them their lives. Ananias and Sapphira lied to the Holy Spirit and fell dead (Acts 5:5, 10). At Corinth, some who abused the Lord’s Table were weak and sick, “and a number sleep” (1 Corinthians 11:30). Paul delivered an immoral man “to Satan for the destruction of his flesh, so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus” (1 Corinthians 5:5). And John speaks of “a sin leading to death” (1 John 5:16).

The story of David shows both the danger and the rescue. After his sin with Bathsheba and the death of Uriah, David kept silent for months, and later wrote, “my body wasted away … day and night Your hand was heavy upon me” (Psalm 32:3–4). Then God sent Nathan. Nathan went to the king with a story, and then with a word: “You are the man!” (2 Samuel 12:7). David confessed, “I have sinned against the Lord,” and Nathan answered, “The Lord also has taken away your sin; you shall not die” (2 Samuel 12:13). That is James 5:20 in action. One man turned another back, and a life was spared.

Bob Wilkin of the Grace Evangelical Society puts it plainly: “The issue is saving a straying believer from premature physical death” (Robert N. Wilkin, “Have You Saved Any Wandering Sinners Lately? James 5:19–20,” Grace Evangelical Society, November 18, 2025, https://faithalone.org/blog/have-you-saved-any-wandering-sinners-lately-james-519-20/). The believer who brings him back has saved his life. That is a matter of life and death.

D. “Will Cover a Multitude of Sins”

Finally, the restorer “will cover a multitude of sins.” The phrase echoes Proverbs: “Hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all transgressions” (Proverbs 10:12). Peter uses it too: “love covers a multitude of sins” (1 Peter 4:8). Whose sins? Most naturally the wanderer’s: the sins he has committed and the sins he would go on to commit if no one turned him back.

They are covered in three ways. First, before God. When the wanderer confesses, God forgives and cleanses (1 John 1:9), and the discipline that was heading toward death is turned aside. “How blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered!” (Psalm 32:1). Second, before other believers. Once sins are confessed and forgiven, love does not keep bringing them up. Third, before himself. The restored brother should not live under the weight of what God has forgiven. Like the father of the prodigal, we put a robe on him, not a sign.

This does not mean that every consequence disappears. David still faced painful results of his sin. But the relationship is restored, the threat of death is turned aside, and the joy returns: “Restore to me the joy of Your salvation” (Psalm 51:12). God’s discipline, painful as it is, “yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness” (Hebrews 12:11) for those who are trained by it.

IV. Looking Back over James

With these words James ends his letter, and our series. It is worth looking back over the road we have traveled:

StudiesPassageThe theme
01–04James 1:1–18A servant’s greeting; joy in trials; wisdom; the crown of life and the good Giver
05–06James 1:19–27Quick to hear: receive the Word, and do it
07–08James 2:1–13No favorites; the royal law and the triumph of mercy
09–10James 2:14–26Faith that works is useful faith; Abraham and Rahab
11James 3:1–18Slow to speak: the tongue and the wisdom from above
12–13James 4:1–17Worldliness, greater grace, humility, “if the Lord wills”
14James 5:1–12Rusted gold and the patient farmer
15–16James 5:13–20Prayer for every season; bringing the wanderer back

From beginning to end, James has written to people who already have eternal life as a free gift. He has not asked whether they are saved. He has asked whether their faith is working, whether their tongues are bridled, whether they are friends of God or of the world, and whether they are ready for the Judge at the door. His concern is the believer’s life now and his reward later. And he ends where love always ends: going after the one who has strayed.

Read this way, James is not a threat hanging over the believer’s assurance. It is a coach’s voice calling believers to run well. The faith that saves us from hell is a free gift received in a moment (John 6:47). The faith that saves our lives from the ruin of sin is a faith that works, every day, until the Lord comes.

Conclusion: Anyone

If you remember one word from these last two verses, remember the little word that appears twice in verse 19: tis, “anyone.” Anyone can wander from the truth. And anyone can be the one who brings him back. Let us be careful not to be the first. And let us always be ready to be the second.

Think of one name. Someone who once walked with the Lord and has drifted. Pray for that person. Then, gently and humbly, go. You may save a life from death and cover a multitude of sins.

And if you are the one who has wandered, hear this as an invitation, not a threat. Your Father has not disowned you. Your eternal life is secure. But He loves you too much to leave you where you are. Turn back. Confess. Come home. He is watching the road, and He will run to meet you.

Lord, thank You.

Part 2 — Study Questions

1. Why does it matter that the wanderer in 5:19 is a believer?

Show Leader Answer

It means James is speaking of restoration, not regeneration, and of physical death, not loss of eternal life. It also warns every believer that he could wander.

2. What makes restoring a brother so difficult, and how does Galatians 6:1 help?

Show Leader Answer

Pride, defensiveness and shame. Paul calls for spiritual people to restore gently, watching themselves.

3. How do the meanings of “save” and “soul” affect our reading of 5:20?

Show Leader Answer

Both words often refer to physical life. In context, James means saving the brother’s life from premature death.

4. What does it look like for love to “cover a multitude of sins”?

Show Leader Answer

Forgiving and not repeating forgiven sins, and helping the restored brother not to live under their weight.

5. Looking back over James, which study has most changed how you live?

Show Leader Answer

Answers will vary. Invite each person to name one study and one change.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Watch Your Own Step

James 5:19; 1 Corinthians 10:12

Source: Commentary ③ — I

Anyone can wander. Stay close to the truth and keep moving forward.

Reflection: Where am I drifting a little at a time?

Practical Step: Name one small compromise and bring it to God today.

Go After the One

James 5:19–20; Luke 15:4–7

Source: Commentary ③ — II

Restoring a brother is every believer’s work, done with a shepherd’s heart.

Reflection: Who has drifted while I watched?

Practical Step: Pray for that person by name every day this week, then reach out with a call or visit.

Restore Gently

Galatians 6:1–2; Matthew 7:5

Source: Commentary ③ — II.C

Go humbly, privately and patiently, with the log out of your own eye.

Reflection: Am I more ready to correct than to restore?

Practical Step: Before you speak to anyone about a sin, confess your own to God first.

Let Love Cover

James 5:20; 1 Peter 4:8

Source: Commentary ③ — III.D

When a brother is restored, help him leave forgiven sin behind.

Reflection: Do I keep bringing up sins God has forgiven?

Practical Step: Choose not to mention a forgiven failure of someone else again, and tell God you have let it go.
