The Crown, the Trap, and the Good Giver

James: Living Wisely Under Pressure

Source: The Gospel Vault · Developed from teaching outlines by Dr. C. Norman Sellers, ThD, used by permission

Leader / Learner Guide

① The Biblical Text

James 1:12–18 · New American Standard Bible (NASB95)

12 Blessed is a man who perseveres under trial; for once he has been approved, he will receive the crown of life which the Lord has promised to those who love Him.

13 Let no one say when he is tempted, “I am being tempted by God”; for God cannot be tempted by evil, and He Himself does not tempt anyone.

14 But each one is tempted when he is carried away and enticed by his own lust.

② Devotional

“Every good thing given and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights.” — James 1:17

When life gets hard, an old lie whispers that God is against us, that He set a trap and is waiting for us to fall. James answers that lie head-on. God never tempts anyone to sin. The pull toward sin comes from inside us, from our own desires, and if we let it grow, it gives birth to something deadly.

God, on the other hand, gives only good. Every good and perfect gift comes down from Him, and He never changes. He does not cast shifting shadows the way the sun and moon do. The proof is that He has already given us new birth through His Word, by His own choice and for His own glory.

So when trials come, we do not need to doubt His heart. We can endure, love Him, and look forward to the crown He has promised to those who do.

Reflection Questions

  1. When I am tempted, where do I tend to put the blame?
  2. What “good and perfect gifts” from God have I taken for granted this week?
  3. How does remembering my new birth help me trust God in a trial?

Father of lights, You do not change. I trust Your heart today.

Introduction: From the Trial to the One Behind It

James has now given us four keys for meeting trials: a settled attitude (1:2–3), a surrendered will (1:4), believing prayer (1:5–8), and an eternal perspective (1:9–11). Verses 2–11 have focused on how we view the trial itself. Now, in verses 12–18, James turns to how we view God in the middle of our trials.

That is where the deepest battle often lies. When hardship drags on, we are tempted not only to sin but to suspect God, to wonder whether He is really good and whether He is somehow behind our failures. James answers with three movements: a promise to those who endure (1:12), a warning against blaming God (1:13–16), and a declaration of God’s unchanging goodness (1:17–18).

One word ties the section together. The Greek noun peirasmos means “trial” (1:2, 12), and the related verb peirazō means “to tempt” (1:13–14). The same outward situation can be a trial that God means for our good, or a temptation that pulls us toward evil. Which it becomes depends on how we respond.

I. The Reward of Enduring Trials (1:12)

“Blessed is a man who perseveres under trial; for once he has been approved, he will receive the crown of life which the Lord has promised to those who love Him.”

James 1:12 (NASB95)

A. Happy Is the One Who Endures

“Blessed” is makarios, the word Jesus used in the Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3–11), and the word the Greek Old Testament uses to open the Psalms: “How blessed is the man…” (Psalm 1:1). It describes a person who is truly well off, deeply happy in God’s sight, whatever his circumstances look like.

Who is this happy man? The one who “perseveres,” hypomenō, the verb form of the “endurance” we met in 1:3–4. He remains under the load. He does not run, quit or turn bitter. James will use the same word later: “We count those blessed who endured. You have heard of the endurance of Job” (5:11).

B. Approved After Testing

Why is he blessed? “For once he has been approved.” The word dokimos means tested and found genuine, like a coin that has passed the assayer’s inspection. It comes from the same family as the “testing” of 1:3. The trial was the test; approval is the result. Paul describes the same sequence:

“and perseverance, proven character; and proven character, hope;”

Romans 5:4 (NASB95)

The word Paul uses for “proven character” is from the same family again. The divinely desired result of our troubles is approved character.

Note carefully: the approval in view is not the approval that makes someone acceptable to God for eternity. Every believer is already accepted in Christ, by grace through faith, apart from works (Ephesians 2:8–9). This is the approval of a servant whose faithfulness has been tested, the kind of approval Paul hoped for when he worked so as not to be “disqualified” from the prize (1 Corinthians 9:25–27).

C. The Crown of Life

“He will receive the crown of life which the Lord has promised.” The word for crown, stephanos, was the victor’s wreath given to athletes and honored citizens. Paul draws on the same picture:

“Everyone who competes in the games exercises self-control in all things. They then do it to receive a perishable wreath, but we an imperishable.”

1 Corinthians 9:25 (NASB95)

Is the crown of life simply eternal life itself? James does not say so, and the context argues against it. The crown is promised to the one who endures and is approved, but eternal life is a free gift received by faith alone (Romans 6:23; John 3:16). What is earned by endurance is a reward, not the gift. The crown of life is a reward to be received at the Judgment Seat of Christ:

“For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may be recompensed for his deeds in the body, according to what he has done, whether good or bad.”

2 Corinthians 5:10 (NASB95)

Jesus promised the same crown to suffering believers in Smyrna:

“Do not fear what you are about to suffer. Behold, the devil is about to cast some of you into prison, so that you will be tested, and you will have tribulation for ten days. Be faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life.”

Revelation 2:10 (NASB95)

Bob Wilkin suggests that “The crown of life here might refer to ruling with Christ in the life to come” (Robert N. Wilkin, “What Is the Crown of Life in James 1:12?” Grace in Focus blog, Grace Evangelical Society, October 3, 2022, https://faithalone.org/?p=104436). Scripture speaks of other crowns too, such as the “crown of righteousness” for all who have loved Christ’s appearing (2 Timothy 4:8). They all point to the same truth: the Lord notices and rewards faithfulness.

There may also be a present side to the crown. Jesus came that we might have life “and have it abundantly” (John 10:10). A believer who endures a trial with God often comes out of it with a richer experience of life with Him. James points to Job, whose trial ended in blessing: “The Lord blessed the latter days of Job more than his beginning” (Job 42:12; cf. James 5:11). Not every trial ends with material restoration in this life, but every trial endured in faith yields something of eternal worth (cf. Zane C. Hodges, The Epistle of James: Proven Character Through Testing [Irving, TX: Grace Evangelical Society, 1994]).

D. For Those Who Love Him

The crown is promised “to those who love Him.” This is a striking phrase. It does not say “to those who believe,” as if to describe all Christians. It describes believers whose love for God is shown in their response to trials. Every trial tests our love for God. It tempts us to resent Him, and resentment stops His character-building work in us. When we bow lovingly under His hand, we find the crown waiting at the end. As the psalmist says, “Weeping may last for the night, but a shout of joy comes in the morning” (Psalm 30:5).

II. Do Not Blame God (1:13)

“Let no one say when he is tempted, ‘I am being tempted by God’; for God cannot be tempted by evil, and He Himself does not tempt anyone.”

James 1:13 (NASB95)

A. When a Trial Becomes a Temptation

Here the word shifts from noun to verb, from trial to temptation. A trial is an outward hardship. But inside every trial lies a possibility: we may respond with sin. Hardship can tempt us to grumble, to lie our way out, to grab for comfort in forbidden places, or to give up on God altogether.

B. The Old Excuse

When we give in, our first instinct is to shift the blame. It is as old as Eden:

“The man said, ‘The woman whom You gave to be with me, she gave me from the tree, and I ate.’”

Genesis 3:12 (NASB95)

“Then the Lord God said to the woman, ‘What is this you have done?’ And the woman said, ‘The serpent deceived me, and I ate.’”

Genesis 3:13 (NASB95)

Adam blamed Eve, and even God (“the woman whom You gave”). Eve blamed the serpent. People still do the same: “It’s in my genes.” “It’s how I was raised.” “It’s the world I live in.” “If God didn’t want me to do it, He shouldn’t have put me in this situation.” James shuts the door on all of it: “Let no one say … ‘I am being tempted by God.’”

C. The Character of God

James gives two reasons. First, “God cannot be tempted by evil.” The word apeirastos means untemptable, without any experience of evil’s pull. Evil finds nothing in God to grab hold of. As Habakkuk said, “Your eyes are too pure to approve evil” (Habakkuk 1:13).

Second, “He Himself does not tempt anyone.” God permits trials and may permit Satan to tempt us, as He did with Job. But He never solicits anyone to do evil. When Jesus taught us to pray, “Do not lead us into temptation” (Matthew 6:13), He was teaching us to ask God to keep us from situations that would overwhelm us, not suggesting that God lures us into sin. And Paul promises that with every temptation God provides “the way of escape also” (1 Corinthians 10:13).

D. What About Jesus’ Temptation?

If God cannot be tempted, how could Jesus, who is God, be tempted? Scripture says plainly that He was: “tempted in all things as we are, yet without sin” (Hebrews 4:15). The answer lies in His two natures. As a true man, Jesus faced real temptation from Satan in the wilderness, real pressure from outside (Matthew 4:1–11). But there was no evil desire within Him to answer it, no inner pull toward sin. He met each temptation as a man filled with the Spirit and armed with the Word: “It is written…” (Matthew 4:4). That is good news for us. The same Spirit and the same Word are available to every believer.

III. How Temptation Works (1:14–15)

“But each one is tempted when he is carried away and enticed by his own lust.”

James 1:14 (NASB95)

“Then when lust has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and when sin is accomplished, it brings forth death.”

James 1:15 (NASB95)

If God is not the source of temptation, what is? James answers: “his own lust.” Then he describes the process in the language of courtship, conception and birth. He is not saying that conception and birth are sinful; he is using a picture everyone understands to show how sin grows.

A. The Lure (1:14)

Two words describe the start. “Carried away” (exelkō) means to be drawn out, as a fish is drawn out from its hiding place. “Enticed” (deleazō) means to be lured with bait. The hook is hidden; only the bait shows. Proverbs describes a young man drawn this way:

“Suddenly he follows her As an ox goes to the slaughter, Or as one in fetters to the discipline of a fool,”

Proverbs 7:22 (NASB95)

The bait is attractive because it appeals to something already inside us. “Each one is tempted … by his own lust.” The word epithymia means strong desire; here, desire for what God forbids. The particular sin may be dishonesty, sexual sin, greed, bitterness or something else. Whatever it is, the pull comes from within. We flirt with it: “I’ll just think about it; I won’t do anything.” That is how it starts.

B. The Conception (1:15a)

“When lust has conceived.” The moment comes when desire stops being a passing thought and is welcomed, entertained and embraced. David saw Bathsheba from his rooftop (2 Samuel 11:2). The first look was a temptation; the lingering look and the inquiry that followed were conception.

C. The Birth (1:15b)

“It gives birth to sin.” What was conceived inwardly is now carried out in action. The thought becomes a deed.

D. The Outcome: Death (1:15c)

“And when sin is accomplished, it brings forth death.” Sin “accomplished” (apoteleō) is sin grown to full maturity, allowed to run its course. And full-grown sin always produces death.

What kind of death does James mean? He is writing to believers, “my beloved brethren” (1:16). They have eternal life, and eternal life cannot be lost (John 3:16; 10:28). So James is not threatening them with hell. But sin is still deadly for a believer. It kills fellowship, joy, usefulness and peace. And, if allowed to run its course, it can lead to physical death as God’s discipline. Paul says of believers who abused the Lord’s Supper, “a number sleep” (1 Corinthians 11:30). James will return to this at the very end of the letter, where turning a wandering brother back “will save his soul from death” (5:20).

The law of sowing and reaping still stands for believers:

“Do not be deceived, God is not mocked; for whatever a man sows, this he will also reap.”

Galatians 6:7 (NASB95)

“For the one who sows to his own flesh will from the flesh reap corruption, but the one who sows to the Spirit will from the Spirit reap eternal life.”

Galatians 6:8 (NASB95)

In context, Paul is speaking to believers about the harvest of their lives. The one who sows to the Spirit reaps a richer experience of the life of God, now and as a reward in the age to come. The one who sows to the flesh reaps corruption.

E. Stopping Sin Before It Is Born

How do we keep sin from coming to birth? The picture suggests the answers:

  • Refuse the first flirtation. Do not play with the bait. Take the first “way of escape” God provides (1 Corinthians 10:13).
  • Stay away from the places where you are weak. Do not walk on the rooftop at evening if you know what you will see.
  • If desire has been entertained, deal with it at once. Confess it to God before it is carried out: “If we confess our sins, He is faithful and righteous to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness” (1 John 1:9).
  • If sin has been born, do not let it grow up. Confess it, turn from it, and seek help from mature believers (5:16, 19–20).

IV. Stop Being Deceived About God (1:16)

“Do not be deceived, my beloved brethren.”

James 1:16 (NASB95)

The Greek construction (mē planasthe, a present imperative with a negative) often carries the sense, “Stop being deceived.” James may be addressing a problem already present. Some of his readers were beginning to think of God the way pagans thought of their gods: as tricksters who lure people into sin and then enjoy punishing them. That would be a very bad god.

Notice that James calls them “my beloved brethren.” Even beloved believers can be deceived. The word planaō means to wander or go astray; our word “planet” comes from the same root, because the ancients saw the planets wandering across the sky. Deception always moves us away from God and His Word. James will use the same word at the end of the letter for a brother who “strays from the truth” (5:19).

How do we avoid being deceived about God? By keeping close to what His Word actually says, not to what tradition says or what our feelings suggest. Every generation, and every religious tradition, can drift from the Word, ours included. Jesus warned, “See to it that no one misleads you” (Matthew 24:4).

V. The Goodness of God Declared (1:17)

“Every good thing given and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shifting shadow.”

James 1:17 (NASB95)

If God is all-powerful, all-knowing and everywhere present, but not good, He would be man’s worst nightmare. But He is good. The psalmists never tire of saying so: “O taste and see that the Lord is good” (Psalm 34:8); “The Lord is good to all, and His mercies are over all His works” (Psalm 145:9). God’s goodness includes His holiness and truthfulness, but in Scripture it most often describes how He treats His creatures: with love, mercy, grace and compassion.

A. Every Good Gift Comes from Him

We might expect James to say, “God gives good gifts.” He says more: “every good thing given and every perfect gift is from above.” Every truly good thing in our lives has one source. James uses two words: dosis, the act of giving, and dōrēma, the gift itself. Both the giving and the gift are good. And the gift is “perfect,” teleios, the same word used of the mature believer in 1:4. God’s gifts are complete; they lack nothing.

B. His Giving Is Constant and Free

“Coming down” is a present participle: God’s gifts keep coming, time after time, without end. And they are gifts, which means they are free. The greatest gift of all is eternal life itself: “by grace you have been saved through faith; and that not of yourselves, it is the gift of God” (Ephesians 2:8). Paul reasons from the greatest gift to all the rest:

“He who did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him over for us all, how will He not also with Him freely give us all things?”

Romans 8:32 (NASB95)

C. The Father of Lights Does Not Change

God is “the Father of lights,” the Creator of the sun, moon and stars. But unlike the heavenly lights He made, He has “no variation or shifting shadow.” The sun rises and sets; the moon waxes and wanes; shadows move across the ground as the earth turns. God has no such turning. He is the same yesterday, today and forever. Theologians call this His immutability.

“‘For I, the Lord, do not change; therefore you, O sons of Jacob, are not consumed.”

Malachi 3:6 (NASB95)

“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.”

Hebrews 13:8 (NASB95)

That is why His gifts are certain. A trial does not mean God has changed toward us. The One who was good to us yesterday is good to us today, even in the dark.

VI. The Goodness of God Demonstrated: The New Birth (1:18)

“In the exercise of His will He brought us forth by the word of truth, so that we would be a kind of first fruits among His creatures.”

James 1:18 (NASB95)

James offers proof of God’s goodness, and it is the best proof there is: what He has already done for us.

A. The Source: His Own Will

“In the exercise of His will.” Our new birth was God’s idea. We had no claim on Him. We had sinned and deserved death. He did not have to save anyone. Yet He chose to give life, freely, by His own determined will.

B. The Act: He Brought Us Forth

“He brought us forth.” Here James does something striking. The verb is apokyeō, “to give birth.” It is the same verb he used three verses earlier: sin, “when it is accomplished, brings forth death” (1:15). Desire gives birth to sin, and sin gives birth to death. But God gives birth to life. Two births, two families, two outcomes.

We were “dead in [our] trespasses and sins” (Ephesians 2:1). God gave us life the moment we believed in Jesus (John 3:16). We were not patched up; we were made new: “if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creature” (2 Corinthians 5:17).

C. The Means: The Word of Truth

God gave us new birth “by the word of truth,” the gospel. Peter says the same:

“for you have been born again not of seed which is perishable but imperishable, that is, through the living and enduring word of God.”

1 Peter 1:23 (NASB95)

Faith comes from hearing the Word (Romans 10:17), which is why the gospel must be told. James will build on this immediately: the same Word that gave us life must now be received with humility, because it “is able to save your souls” (1:21).

D. The Purpose: First Fruits

“So that we would be a kind of first fruits among His creatures.” In Israel, the first fruits were the first part of the harvest, offered to God as a pledge that the rest of the harvest would follow. The early Jewish believers James wrote to were the first fruits of the whole Church. And all believers are first fruits of a coming new creation, when creation itself will be set free (Romans 8:19–23). Our new birth is God’s down payment on a renewed world.

Only a good God would do all this for people who were His enemies. Now that we are His children, we can be sure He is not plotting our downfall in our trials. He is working for our good.

Conclusion: Two Births

James sets two births side by side:

  • Desire gives birth to sin, and sin gives birth to death (1:14–15). That process starts in us.
  • God gives birth to life by His Word (1:18). That process starts in Him.

When trials come, we must choose which process we will feed. We can let a trial become a temptation, blame God and drift toward death. Or we can endure, love God, trust His unchanging goodness, and look forward to the crown of life He has promised to those who love Him.

Lord, thank You.

2. What excuses for sin are common today? How does 1:13–14 answer them?

Show Leader Answer

Genes, upbringing, environment, circumstances, or “God made me this way.” James says the source is our own desire, and God never tempts anyone to evil.

3. Walk through the stages of temptation in 1:14–15. At which stage is it easiest to stop sin? What helps?

Show Leader Answer

At the first lure, before desire is welcomed. Refuse to flirt with it, avoid places of weakness, take God’s way of escape, and confess immediately (1 Corinthians 10:13; 1 John 1:9).

4. How does the “Father of lights” picture (1:17) encourage a believer in a long trial?

Show Leader Answer

Heavenly lights change and cast shifting shadows; God does not. His goodness toward us is the same in the dark as in the light.

5. What is the significance of James using the same verb for sin “bringing forth” death (1:15) and God “bringing us forth” (1:18)?

Show Leader Answer

It contrasts two births: desire births sin and death, God births new life through His Word. We choose which process to feed.

⑤ Application

3–4 applications, drawn from the sections above

Endure for the Crown

James 1:12; 2 Corinthians 5:10

Source: Commentary ③ — I.B–D; cf. Wilkin, “What Is the Crown of Life in James 1:12?”

Our eternal life is secure as a gift. But the Lord promises a crown to believers who endure trials out of love for Him.

Reflection: Which trial am I most tempted to quit or resent right now?

Practical Step: Tell the Lord this week, in your own words, that you love Him and will keep trusting Him in that trial.

Stop the Blame Game

James 1:13–14; Genesis 3:12–13

Source: Commentary ③ — II.B; III.A

Blaming God, people or circumstances keeps us from dealing with the real source of temptation: our own desires.

Reflection: What sin do I most often excuse, and whom do I blame for it?

Practical Step: Name that sin honestly before God, without excuses, and confess it (1 John 1:9).

Kill It Before It Grows

James 1:15; 1 Corinthians 10:13

Source: Commentary ③ — III.E

Sin is easiest to stop at the first lure. Planning ahead for our weak places is wise, not weak.

Reflection: Where and when am I most vulnerable to temptation?

Practical Step: Identify one “rooftop” in your life and decide in advance how you will avoid it or escape it.

Count the Good Gifts

James 1:17–18; Romans 8:32

Source: Commentary ③ — V–VI

In a trial, remembering God’s past gifts, above all our new birth, guards us against doubting His heart.

Reflection: Do I tend to forget God’s gifts when life gets hard?

Practical Step: Write down ten good gifts from God this week, beginning with your new birth, and thank Him for each.
