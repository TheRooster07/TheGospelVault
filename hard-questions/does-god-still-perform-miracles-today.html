50 Hard Questions · Question 25 of 50

Does God Still Perform Miracles Today?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

God still answers prayer and can act miraculously at any time. The apostolic sign-gifts confirmed new revelation, and every claim today should be tested by Scripture.

Key versesHebrews 2:3–4 · James 5:14–15 · 1 John 4:1

Scripture never suggests that God has become less powerful or less active since the apostolic era; the question is not whether God can act miraculously, but how His ordinary and extraordinary activity are related, and whether the specific sign-gifts of the apostolic age continue in the same form today. Both careful biblical observation and honest pastoral experience call for a measured answer rather than a flat yes or no.

Miracles in Scripture tend to cluster at particular hinge points in redemptive history — the Exodus, the ministries of Elijah and Elisha, and above all the ministry of Christ and the apostles — where God was authenticating a new stage of revelation. The writer of Hebrews describes the apostolic sign-gifts as God’s own testimony “bearing witness both with signs and wonders, with various miracles, and gifts of the Holy Spirit” to the message “which at the first began to be spoken by the Lord” (Hebrews 2:3–4) — language that ties the miracles to the founding, authenticating moment of the message rather than presenting them as a permanent baseline of every era.

At the same time, nothing in Scripture teaches that God has stopped answering prayer for healing, provision, or deliverance, or that He cannot intervene in extraordinary ways in a believer’s life today. James instructs the church to pray for the sick and anticipates that “the prayer of faith will save the sick” (James 5:14–15) as an ordinary pastoral practice, not a temporary apostolic-era exception. The distinction worth holding is between God’s ongoing providential and answered-prayer activity, which continues without interruption, and the specific apostolic sign-gifts (such as the gift of healing exercised at will, or unmediated apostolic authority) tied to confirming revelation that was, at the time, still being completed and had not yet been written down and confirmed as Scripture.

This means claims of miraculous activity today should be tested carefully rather than accepted or dismissed reflexively. Scripture itself commends this kind of discernment: “Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits, whether they are of God” (1 John 4:1); “Test all things; hold fast what is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21). A genuine work of God will never contradict His written Word, and will point toward Christ and toward greater dependence on Scripture rather than toward the personality or platform of whoever reports the miracle.

Whatever position a believer takes on this debated question, none of it touches the security or completeness of salvation, which rests entirely on Christ’s finished work received by faith and is never made to depend on witnessing or experiencing a miracle (John 20:29, 31 — “these are written that you may believe ... and that believing you may have life in His name”). God remains fully able to act miraculously whenever He chooses; the ordinary Christian life is built not on expecting or chasing miraculous confirmation, but on the sufficiency of what He has already spoken.
