How Do We Know the Bible Is Trustworthy?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Manuscript evidence, fulfilled prophecy, and archaeology give strong reasons to trust the Bible, and Scripture claims to be God-breathed. Faith in it is reasonable, not blind.

Key verses2 Timothy 3:16 · 2 Peter 1:21 · Micah 5:2

The Bible’s trustworthiness rests on several independent lines of evidence that reinforce one another rather than on a single, isolated claim. The manuscript evidence alone is unmatched by any other document from the ancient world: the New Testament survives in over 5,800 Greek manuscripts, with fragments dating within decades of the originals, compared to the mere handful of manuscript copies, often separated from their originals by centuries, on which historians confidently rely for other ancient texts like Caesar’s Gallic Wars or Tacitus’s Annals. The sheer volume and early dating of the manuscript evidence allow textual scholars to reconstruct the original wording with a degree of confidence no other ancient text can claim.

Fulfilled prophecy provides a second, independent line of evidence. Old Testament texts written centuries before Christ’s birth specify remarkably precise details later fulfilled in Him — His birth in Bethlehem (Micah 5:2), His betrayal for thirty pieces of silver (Zechariah 11:12–13), the manner of His death by crucifixion described in graphic detail centuries before Rome used that method of execution (Psalm 22:16–18), and the timing of His appearance calculated from Daniel’s prophecy of the “seventy weeks” (Daniel 9:24–26). The cumulative improbability of a single figure fulfilling this many specific, independent predictions by chance is, by any reasonable statistical estimate, vanishingly small.

Archaeology has repeatedly confirmed details of the biblical record that critics once dismissed as legendary or historically inaccurate — the existence of the Hittite civilization, the pool of Bethesda with its five porticoes (John 5:2), the Pilate Stone confirming Pontius Pilate’s historical governorship, and the practice of crucifixion itself, confirmed by the discovery of a crucified man’s heel bone still pierced by a nail near Jerusalem. While archaeology cannot prove the Bible’s theological claims, it has a consistent track record of vindicating its historical and geographical details against skeptical challenges.

Beyond external evidence, Scripture’s own claim about itself deserves consideration on its own terms: “All Scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness” (2 Timothy 3:16). Peter describes the process by which this occurred: “prophecy never came by the will of man, but holy men of God spoke as they were moved by the Holy Spirit” (2 Peter 1:21) — human authors writing in their own styles and historical circumstances, while the Holy Spirit superintended the result so that what they wrote was, in its original manuscripts, exactly what God intended communicated.

None of this evidence is offered to replace faith with proof — Scripture’s trustworthiness is ultimately received by faith, as with any historical testimony (John 20:29, 31) — but it is offered to show that this faith is reasonable rather than blind, resting on evidence a fair-minded skeptic can examine rather than on an unverifiable leap unsupported by anything checkable. On this basic conviction — that Scripture is God’s own trustworthy, authoritative Word — Bible-believing Christians of many traditions stand together, as the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy sets out.1 For further reading on the evidential case sketched above, see Josh McDowell and Sean McDowell, Evidence That Demands a Verdict: Life-Changing Truth for a Skeptical World, fully updated 3rd ed. (Nashville: Thomas Nelson, 2017).2
