How Should Christians Think About Suffering for Their Faith?

Translation: NKJV · Free Grace / Dispensational · Source: The Gospel Vault

In Brief

Persecution is promised, not exceptional. Scripture calls it a privilege that refines faith and brings reward, and nothing can separate believers from God’s love.

Key verses2 Timothy 3:12 · Matthew 5:11–12 · 1 Peter 1:7

Persecution for genuine faith in Christ is not presented in Scripture as an unfortunate exception to normal Christian experience but as an expected, even promised, feature of it. “Yes, and all who desire to live godly in Christ Jesus will suffer persecution” (2 Timothy 3:12); Jesus told His disciples plainly, “If the world hates you, you know that it hated Me before it hated you” (John 15:18). A life of visible faithfulness to Christ, in a world still under the influence of the fallen order (see Q30, “What Happened to Human Nature at the Fall?”), will predictably provoke opposition rather than universal approval.

Scripture’s response to this reality is not resignation but a striking reframing of suffering for Christ’s sake as a genuine privilege and occasion for joy rather than only hardship to be endured. The apostles, having just been flogged for preaching Christ, departed “rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for His name” (Acts 5:41). Peter instructs suffering believers directly: “if you are reproached for the name of Christ, blessed are you, for the Spirit of glory and of God rests upon you” (1 Peter 4:14); Jesus Himself pronounces a specific beatitude over exactly this experience: “Blessed are you when they revile and persecute you ... for My sake. Rejoice and be exceedingly glad, for great is your reward in heaven” (Matthew 5:11–12) — directly connecting persecution to the doctrine of future reward (see Q39, “Will There Be Rewards in Heaven, and What Are They For?”).

This does not mean suffering for one’s faith is pleasant or that Scripture asks believers to pretend it does not hurt; Paul catalogs his own extensive suffering for the gospel in visceral, honest detail — beatings, shipwreck, hunger, danger (2 Corinthians 11:23–27) — without minimizing any of it, while simultaneously affirming that “our light affliction, which is but for a moment, is working for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory” (2 Corinthians 4:17). The honest acknowledgment of real pain and the settled confidence in its ultimate purpose are held together, not played against each other.

Persecution also serves purposes Scripture identifies as valuable rather than merely tolerated: it refines and authenticates faith, “that the genuineness of your faith, being much more precious than gold that perishes, though it is tested by fire, may be found to praise, honor, and glory at the revelation of Jesus Christ” (1 Peter 1:7), and it has historically been the very means by which the gospel spreads most rapidly, as the persecuted early church, “scattered” by opposition in Jerusalem, went “everywhere preaching the word” as a direct result (Acts 8:1, 4).

For believers living where persecution is rare, this doctrine calls for solidarity with the global church, where persecution remains a daily reality for millions (Hebrews 13:3, “remember the prisoners as if chained with them”), and for a settled expectation, rather than surprise, should faithfulness to Christ ever bring real cost — held throughout, as with every other hardship this paper addresses, in the unshakable confidence that “neither death nor life ... shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 8:38–39).
